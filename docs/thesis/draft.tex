  \large Bayesian Score-Informed Performance Transcription
  with Calibrated Uncertainty}
\author{Raynaldi Lalang \\[0.5em]
  \small Supervisor: Kazuyoshi Yoshii \\
  \small Kyoto University}
\date{Thesis draft, compiled \today}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent This thesis reads a recording of a musical performance as four
layers: the score that was written, the expression with which each note was
played, the timbre that the instrument adds, and the noise. Given the score
and an observation of the performance, which is aligned piano MIDI in the
first setting, tracked audio of strings and winds in the second and the
waveform itself in the third, the expression layer is inferred note by note
together with a predictive distribution. The method is Bayesian
score-conditioned inference under one multi-output Gaussian process on the
score graph. Notes related in the score are related in performance, the
expressive channels are coupled by a coregionalization matrix, and
hand-built score features and per-note embeddings from a small symbolic
music model trained from scratch (the \emph{music model}) enter as linear
kernels, that is, as marginalized Bayesian linear means. One exact marginal
likelihood per piece fits the covariance parameters.

On \nConfPieces{} held-out piano pieces the model recovers hidden notes with
lower error than the strongest two-stage pipeline, a plug-in mean followed
by a graph Gaussian process on the residual, with RMSE~\rmseGP{} against
\rmseConfOldHead{} and a paired difference of \dRmseConf{} whose interval
excludes zero. Its nominal-90\% intervals cover $\approx\covGP$ of held-out
values, and the graph improves the log score inside the model by
\dNllGraphConf{}. The pooled log score against the two-stage pipeline shows
no difference. On 13 held-out strings-and-winds pieces the same prior
improves intonation recovery and the vibrato log scores against estimator
targets, with coverage of $0.88$ to $0.91$ on all six channels, and the
secondary timing hypothesis is not supported. A correlated timing-noise
model and a tonal pitch metric were then tested on the same pieces,
disclosed as their second use. The first was supported and the second was
not.

Development ablations on both corpora place the graph's value in smoothing
over a short time window, two beats of score time on piano and two seconds
of performance time on strings and winds, with the pitch term adding nothing
to intonation. The returns of a motif link notes outside that window. An
edge family that joins them, built from annotations of Beethoven sonata
movements, lowers the articulation error of hidden returns on the development set. In
one pre-specified evaluation on $58$ further performances of $18$ of those
movements the effect holds, $-0.029$ with an interval excluding zero, and
the timing log-score prediction is not supported.

At the waveform, a differentiable harmonic model with exactly marginalized
amplitudes recovers within-note pitch curves without a pitch tracker and
refines note boundaries given a local alignment, but its bands under-cover
the reference, because the two measure slightly different quantities. Inside
that inference the score graph is better than a purely temporal prior at
phrase scale, once also on held-out tracks, and none of the three spans
tested shows a gain beyond the shared offset of a passage.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation}
A score specifies what is to be played, and a performance realises how it is
played. The difference, in micro-timing, articulation and dynamics, and on
continuously pitched instruments in intonation and vibrato, is where musical
expression lives. Recovering it from a recording is of use in analysis, in
pedagogy, in expressive rendering and as structured supervision for audio
models. The thesis reads a recording as four layers. The score is given, the
expression is the object of inference, the timbre is a nuisance that the
waveform setting integrates out exactly, and the noise is modelled per note
and per channel. Two commitments shape the work. The score is treated as
known, so the problem is the inverse of expressive synthesis rather than
full transcription, and uncertainty is a primary output, evaluated with the
same weight as accuracy. With each note of the score is associated a small
\emph{bundle} of expressive variables, so that a performance becomes a
per-note field over the score support and transcription becomes posterior
inference over that field.

\section{Problem statement}
Let a symbolic score fix, for each note, its pitch, its score onset and its
notated duration. A performance assigns to each note a small vector of
continuous expressive variables, here the onset residual $\tau$, the log
articulation ratio $\log r$ and the normalised velocity $v$. Given the score
and an aligned performance, the task is to infer the posterior over these
per-note variables, including for notes that are held out (masked), and to
do so with calibrated uncertainty. This is score-informed performance
transcription, or equivalently probabilistic inverse synthesis. In symbols,
with $S$ the score, $x$ the observation of the performance and $z$ the
latent expressive variables,
\begin{equation*}
  p(z \mid x, S) \;\propto\; p(x \mid z, S)\, p(z \mid S),
\end{equation*}
where $S$ is the score layer and $z$ the expression layer. The timbre and
noise layers live inside the observation model, in the waveform setting as
the harmonic amplitudes $a$, which are marginalised exactly, and the
residual $\varepsilon$. The score-structured prior $p(z \mid S)$ is the
subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:model}. The observations are per-note values
read from aligned MIDI (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}), per-note estimates from
tracked audio with their variances (Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}), and the
waveform itself through a differentiable harmonic synthesizer
(Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}). Inferring
the score itself, that is, full automatic music transcription, is out of
scope (\S\ref{sec:scope} and \S\ref{sec:future-amt}).

The thesis asks three questions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item RQ1 (representation). What do interpretable per-note variables,
        and later within-note trajectories, capture of expressive
        performance, and what do they measurably discard?
  \item RQ2 (structured inference). Does a score-structured prior improve
        both the recovery of expressive variables and the calibration of
        their uncertainty, compared with independent or purely temporal
        baselines, when observations are missing (piano) or noisy (strings
        and winds)?
  \item RQ3 (direct waveform inference). Can a differentiable harmonic
        observation model recover expressive trajectories directly from
        the waveform, and can its uncertainty separate genuine performance
        ambiguity from the model's own mismatch?
\end{itemize}
RQ2 is the core of the thesis. It is answered on held-out pieces, with
recovery and calibration evaluated separately (\S\ref{sec:metrics}). RQ1 is answered by construction
together with a measured account of what the per-note representation
discards (\S\ref{sec:phase2-delta}). RQ3 is studied on the development set
(Chapter~\ref{ch:phase3}), where the score prior is also placed inside the
waveform inference and one prediction is tested on held-out tracks. The
attribution of the graph's value to a short time window
(Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}) raises a last question, answered in
Chapter~\ref{ch:motif}: whether a structure that the time window does not
represent, the repetition of a motif, carries expressive information of its
own.

\section{The proposed model and the two-stage pipeline}\label{sec:twostage}
\textbf{The proposed model} is one multi-output Gaussian process over all
notes and channels, in which the score graph, the hand-built score features
and the per-note representation of the music model enter a single kernel,
with its covariance parameters learned from one per-piece marginal
likelihood (\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}) and the graph geometry fixed. The music
model is a small symbolic language model trained from scratch.
\textbf{The two-stage pipeline} is the standard construction used as its
comparator: a plug-in mean, a cross-piece point estimate of each note's
expression, followed by a separate per-channel graph Gaussian process on
the residual. The proposed model contains it on a restricted slice of its
parameters (\S\ref{sec:special-cases}).

\section{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A multi-output graph Gaussian process for expressive performance
        (\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}). Channels are coupled by coregionalization,
        the graph kernel is a spectral function of the score-graph
        Laplacian, side information enters as marginalized Bayesian linear
        means, and the covariance parameters are learned per piece by the
        exact evidence.
  \item An attribution of the model's behaviour to its parts
        (\S\ref{sec:attribution}): an exact decomposition of the posterior
        by prior component, nested ablations that contain the two-stage
        pipeline on a restricted slice, and development ablations on both
        corpora that place the graph's value in a short time window rather
        than in pitch proximity (Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}).
  \item A map of where the structure helps (\S\ref{sec:boundary}): in
        note-level judgements but not in whole-performance summaries, and in
        interpolation but not in extrapolation from an excerpt.
  \item An evaluation protocol (\S\ref{sec:hygiene}): a development and
        held-out split with every re-use disclosed, filtering of recordings
        shared with the pretraining corpus, and a music-model read-out that
        cannot see a note's own target, enforced by a structural test and a
        bitwise audit.
\end{enumerate}
The waveform setting (Chapter~\ref{ch:phase3}) and the motif study
(Chapter~\ref{ch:motif}) bound how far the score structure carries. At the
waveform the score prior transfers the shared offset of a passage and is
better than a purely temporal prior at phrase scale, once also on held-out
tracks, with nothing measurable beyond the offset. Inference of a whole
performance from its waveform is outside the evaluated scope. The returns of a motif
add articulation information on the development set and in one evaluation
on further performances of the same movements, and the timing log-score
prediction is not supported (\S\ref{sec:motif-heldout}).

\section{Scope and non-goals}\label{sec:scope}
The score support is known: note identities, score onsets and score
durations are given, and in the piano and strings-and-winds settings an
alignment of score and performance is given as well. Evaluation is on
held-out notes and pieces and always includes calibration. Discovering an
unknown score from audio, which is automatic music transcription, is outside
the scope (\S\ref{sec:future-amt}), as are performer-controlled intonation on
a fixed-pitch keyboard, arbitrary instruments and room acoustics. Three
observation types are studied. Aligned piano MIDI tests the recovery of
masked notes with negligible observation noise, tracked audio of strings
and winds adds measurement uncertainty and the pitch channels, and the
waveform supplies the acoustic observation directly. The motif study
changes the prior rather than the observation: it tests, on aligned piano
data, what repeated score structure adds beyond local smoothing.

\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{ch:related}

\section{Computational models of expressive performance}
Expressive-performance research models how performers deviate from the
notated score in timing, dynamics and articulation. Historically this was
done with rule-based systems, and more recently with sequence models trained
on aligned corpora \cite{cancino2018,oore2018}. Most such work predicts a
single expressive rendering or studies aggregate statistics. This thesis
differs in framing the problem as per-note Bayesian inference with
calibrated uncertainty. It asks not only how accurately expression can be
predicted but also how \emph{trustworthy} the per-note confidence is.

\section{Score-to-performance alignment}
Aligned corpora pair a symbolic score with a performance through a note- or
beat-level correspondence, from which continuous deviations can be read off.
ASAP provides score$\leftrightarrow$performance alignments for piano with
beat and downbeat annotations \cite{asap}, and it is the source of the
targets used here. The quality of this alignment bounds from above the
quality of the extracted targets.

\section{Structured priors: graph Gaussian processes, GMRFs, and the SPDE approach}
Gaussian Markov random fields place a Gaussian prior whose sparse precision
encodes conditional-independence structure \cite{rueheld2005}. The SPDE
approach links Gaussian fields to GMRFs and yields Mat\'ern-type priors with
interpretable range and smoothness \cite{lindgren2011}. These constructions
extend to graphs, giving Mat\'ern Gaussian processes on graph nodes
\cite{borovitskiy2021}. For vector-valued outputs, both the
intrinsic-coregionalization construction of \cite{borovitskiy2021} and the
Kronecker output coupling of Gaussian processes over graphs
\cite{venkitaraman2018} prescribe an explicit cross-output covariance.
Gaussian processes on \emph{evolving} graphs \cite{blancomulero2021} address
dynamic structure, which a fixed score does not need. The proposed model is
exactly such a multi-output graph Gaussian process, with the graph built
from musical adjacency rather than physical space. Its graph kernel is a
spectral function of the score-graph Laplacian, its channels are coupled by
a coregionalization matrix, its side information is marginalized into the
kernel as Bayesian linear means, and its covariance parameters are learned
by the exact evidence.

\section{Symbolic music language models}
Transformer and RNN models over tokenised MIDI, among them the Music
Transformer, REMI and performance RNNs, learn strong distributions over
symbolic music \cite{musictransformer,remi,oore2018}. This thesis builds a
small decoder-only model from scratch, in the spirit of nanoGPT
\cite{nanogpt}, rather than using a pretrained one, so that its read-out
can be taken before each note's own target.

\section{Differentiable synthesis}
Differentiable digital signal processing renders audio from interpretable
controls, namely fundamental frequency, harmonic amplitudes and noise,
through a differentiable synthesizer \cite{ddsp}. This is the intended
waveform likelihood of the thesis. It connects the latent performance
variables to a waveform while keeping the amplitudes conditionally
Gaussian, so that they can be marginalised in closed form
(Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}). The DDSP line has since grown hierarchical
note-to-performance-to-audio \emph{generation} (MIDI-DDSP
\cite{wu2022midi}), physically structured interpretable effect modules such
as a differentiable guitar amplifier \cite{yeh2024ddspamp}, and semantically
disentangled timbre control for multitimbral synthesis \cite{dalri2026}.
Those works run the synthesis direction with learned neural encoders and
decoders. This thesis runs the inverse direction: the synthesizer is a
hand-structured observation model with Gaussian amplitudes marginalised
exactly, and the recovered object is the posterior over score-aligned
performance variables.

A complementary line puts periodicity into a Gaussian-process kernel:
spectral mixture kernels \cite{wilson2013}, their non-stationary
generalization \cite{remes2017}, and harmonic covariances for music audio
\cite{alvarado2016}. They are natural priors for within-note structure such
as a drifting vibrato. Both places where they could enter, a curve-level
estimator and the pitch-curve prior of the waveform setting, were tried on
the development set without improvement (Chapter~\ref{ch:phase3}). The
design difference is a choice: kernel constructions marginalize a note's
structure away, whereas this thesis keeps named per-note variables because
the variables are the object of study.

\chapter{Model and Formulation}\label{ch:model}
This chapter derives the proposed model in full for the piano setting. The
additions that the strings-and-winds setting makes to this model are specified
together with its results in Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}, and the mathematics of
the waveform setting is given in Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}.
Appendix~\ref{app:notation} summarises the notation.

\section{Setting}
A score is the known discrete support
\begin{equation}
  \score = \{ s_i \}_{i=1}^{N}, \qquad s_i = (p_i,\, b_i,\, d_i),
  \label{eq:support}
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is the pitch (MIDI number), $b_i \ge 0$ the onset in score time
(beats), and $d_i > 0$ the notated duration (beats). A performance attaches
to each score event a vector of continuous expressive variables. In the piano
setting this vector is
\begin{equation}
  y_i \;=\; \bigl[\, \tau_i,\; \log r_i,\; v_i \,\bigr] \in \R^{3},
  \label{eq:variables}
\end{equation}
and its entries are defined relative to the monotone score-time warp
$T(\cdot)$, which maps score beats to performance seconds. The warp is read
off the alignment. The local tempo, in seconds per beat, is estimated by
finite differences of performed time against score time along the aligned
beat grid, and $T$ is the cumulative integral of the local tempo over score
time, so that $T(b)$ is the tempo-implied arrival of beat $b$. Because $T$ is
estimated from the same performance's aligned beat grid and then treated as
known, its estimation error is not propagated. This is a limitation, and the
strings-and-winds setting addresses it with a leave-one-out warp
(Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}). Conditional on $T$, the three channels are
\begin{align}
  \tau_i   &= t_i - T(b_i), \label{eq:tau}\\
  \log r_i &= \log \frac{\delta_i}{\,T(b_i + d_i) - T(b_i)\,}, \label{eq:logr}\\
  v_i      &= \tilde v_i / 127 - \overline{\tilde v/127}. \label{eq:vel}
\end{align}
Here $\tau_i$ is the onset residual in seconds, the performed onset $t_i$
minus the tempo-implied onset $T(b_i)$. The articulation $\log r_i$ is the
log ratio of the performed duration $\delta_i$ to the warp-implied duration,
and the dynamics $v_i$ is the centred, unit-range MIDI velocity
$\tilde v_i$. Conditioning on the warp $T$ is what makes the per-note field
$y$ well defined and jointly Gaussian.

Later settings add channels on the same support. The strings-and-winds
setting adds the intonation $c_i$ in cents, the vibrato parameters and the
fundamental curve $f_0$, and the waveform setting adds the harmonic
amplitudes $a$. Not every variable is available, measurable, or even
meaningful on every instrument, and Table~\ref{tab:variables} records for
each one the instruments on which it is realistic. The piano gives reliable
timing, articulation and dynamics but no performer-controlled intonation,
which is why intonation and vibrato are studied on continuously pitched
instruments. The task is score-informed in the sense that $\score$ is given
and the posterior over $y$ is inferred, in the inverse direction, from
observations generated by the same model that, run forward, synthesises a
performance.

\section{The score graph}\label{sec:scoregraph}
Let $\graph = (V, E)$ be a weighted graph on the $N$ score events with
Gaussian-kernel edge weights
\begin{equation}
  W_{ij} \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl( -\tfrac{(b_i - b_j)^2}{2\,\ell_b^2} - \tfrac{(p_i - p_j)^2}{2\,\ell_p^2} \Bigr)
  \;+\; w_v\, \mathbf{1}[\text{voice}_i = \text{voice}_j],
  \qquad W_{ii} = 0,
  \label{eq:adjacency}
\end{equation}
with length scales $\ell_b$ and $\ell_p$ (semitones) and an optional
same-voice bonus $w_v \ge 0$. Throughout, these are untuned defaults,
$\ell_b = 2$, $\ell_p = 4$ and $w_v = 0$. On piano $b_i$ is in beats, and
on strings and winds, whose corpus annotates onsets in seconds, $b_i$ and
$\ell_b$ are in seconds (\S\ref{sec:phase2-delta}). One adjacency is shared
by all channels. The ASAP corpus carries no voice
labels, so the voice bonus is inactive in every reported experiment and is
retained for corpora that annotate voices. The combinatorial graph Laplacian
is
\begin{equation}
  \Lg \;=\; D - W, \qquad D = \diag\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_j W_{ij}\Bigr).
  \label{eq:laplacian}
\end{equation}
Two baselines are defined on the same support. The temporal-only baseline
replaces $W$ by the chain adjacency of score-time order, so that each note is
coupled only to its temporal neighbours, which gives a smoother of AR(1)
type. The independent baseline drops all edges, so that
$\Lg = 0$ and the notes are a priori unrelated.

\subsection{Music-theoretic graph constructions}\label{sec:graphvariants}
Music theory suggests two logically distinct interventions on
\eqref{eq:adjacency}, one that \emph{replaces} the semitone ruler and one
that \emph{adds} relations on top of it. The first replaces semitone
distance by the circle-of-fifths distance. With pitch class
$\mathrm{pc}(p) = p \bmod 12$ placed on the circle of fifths by
$\pi(p) = 7\,\mathrm{pc}(p) \bmod 12$,
\begin{equation}
  d_5(i,j) \;=\; \min\bigl(\,|\pi(p_i) - \pi(p_j)|,\; 12 - |\pi(p_i) - \pi(p_j)|\,\bigr)
  \;\in\; \{0,\dots,6\},
  \label{eq:fifths}
\end{equation}
so a perfect fifth is $1$ step and a semitone $5$. Since $d_5$ is blind to
register, an octave-band term is added, giving
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{tonal}}_{ij} \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl( -\tfrac{(b_i - b_j)^2}{2\ell_b^2}
    \;-\; \tfrac{d_5(i,j)^2}{2\ell_5^2}
    \;-\; \tfrac{\bigl((p_i - p_j)/12\bigr)^2}{2\ell_{\mathrm{oct}}^2} \Bigr),
  \qquad W^{\mathrm{tonal}}_{ii} = 0 .
  \label{eq:tonaladj}
\end{equation}
The second keeps the base graph and adds edges between notes struck together
and between notes a melodic step apart within a short window,
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{chord}}_{ij} \;=\; W_{ij}
    \;+\; w_{\mathrm{ch}}\, \mathbf{1}\bigl[\, b_i = b_j,\; i \neq j \,\bigr]
    \;+\; w_{\mathrm{vl}}\, \mathbf{1}\bigl[\, b_i \neq b_j,\;
        |b_i - b_j| \le \Delta_{\mathrm{vl}},\;
        1 \le |p_i - p_j| \le 2 \,\bigr],
  \label{eq:harmadj}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta_{\mathrm{vl}} = 2$ beats. The indicator $\mathbf 1[b_i = b_j]$
is onset simultaneity used as a proxy for chord membership, and no harmonic
analysis is performed. The augmented graph is called the \textbf{chord
graph}, a name that keeps the word ``harmonic'' for the harmonic amplitudes
of the waveform synthesiser. The chord graph is the graph of the strongest
two-stage comparator, and the circle-of-fifths metric is tested on
intonation in \S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}.

\section{The graph kernel}\label{sec:graphgp}
Each expressive channel is a scalar field over the $N$ notes, and the graph
turns it into a Gaussian process on the score graph, a graph GP, that is, a
multivariate Gaussian whose covariance is built from the graph. Let
$\Lg = U \diag(\nu)\, U^\top$ be the Laplacian's eigendecomposition, with
eigenvalues $0 = \nu_1 \le \nu_2 \le \dots$ playing the role of graph
frequencies. A graph kernel is obtained by shaping this spectrum,
\begin{equation}
  K_G(s) \;=\; U \operatorname{diag}\bigl(g(\nu;\, s)\bigr)\, U^\top,
  \label{eq:spectral}
\end{equation}
with a decreasing spectral profile $g$. Components that vary smoothly along
the graph, those with small $\nu$, keep their prior weight, rapidly varying
components are suppressed, and a single shape parameter $s$ sets how
aggressively. The profile is normalised so that $g(0) = 1$, and $K_G$
therefore carries shape only, with all scale living in the coregionalization
matrix of \S\ref{sec:coreg}. In the product $B \otimes K_G$ the split of scale between the
two factors is unidentifiable, because $(cB) \otimes (K_G/c)$ is the same
covariance, so the scale must live in exactly one place. Three classical
profiles are used:
\begin{align}
  \text{additive / regularized Laplacian:}\quad & g(\nu;\, s) = \frac{1}{1 + s\,\nu},
  \label{eq:additive}\\[2pt]
  \text{graph Mat\'ern:}\quad & g(\nu;\, s) = \Bigl(\frac{s}{\,s + \nu\,}\Bigr)^{\!\alpha},
  \qquad \alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1},
  \label{eq:matern}\\[2pt]
  \text{diffusion / heat:}\quad & g(\nu;\, s) = e^{-s\,\nu}.
  \label{eq:diffusion}
\end{align}
The Mat\'ern profile is the graph Mat\'ern Gaussian process of
\cite{borovitskiy2021}, the graph analogue of a Euclidean Mat\'ern GP
through the SPDE link of \cite{lindgren2011}, with $s = \kappa^2$ and its
scale carried by $B$. The diffusion profile is its $\alpha \to \infty$ limit
under $s = \alpha/t$, whereas at fixed $s$ the limit keeps only the
Laplacian's null space. The additive profile is the graph Mat\'ern at
$\alpha = 1$ with the shape parameter inverted, also known as the
regularized-Laplacian kernel \cite{smolakondor2003}.

Under any of these profiles, neighbouring notes are encouraged, not forced,
to share expressive behaviour, and distant notes are coupled only weakly.
The constant profile $g \equiv 1$ gives $K_G = I$, the graph switched off
and the notes a priori independent, which is the ablation that isolates the
graph's marginal value. The additive profile is the default throughout, and
the kernel comparison of \S\ref{sec:kernels} shows that the classical
alternatives perform no differently. Throughout, $\alpha$ is only the
Mat\'ern exponent and $\sigma_g$ the Mat\'ern prior scale, while plain
$\sigma$ is reserved for the posterior standard deviation
(\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}).

\section{Coupling the channels}\label{sec:coreg}
Timing, articulation, and velocity are not independent, since a stressed
note tends to be louder, longer, and delayed together. The model therefore
couples the channels with an intrinsic coregionalization structure
\cite{bonilla2008}, one latent field over the $3N$ (note, channel) pairs with
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Cov}\bigl[\, y_{i,c},\; y_{j,c'} \bigr]
  \;=\; B_{cc'}\; \bigl[K_G(s)\bigr]_{ij},
  \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad
  \operatorname{Cov}[\, y \,] \;=\; B \otimes K_G(s),
  \label{eq:icm}
\end{equation}
where $B \in \R^{3 \times 3}$ is a positive semidefinite matrix learned
through its log-Cholesky factor. Because the $g(0) = 1$ normalisation
resolves the scale ambiguity of \S\ref{sec:graphgp}, $B$ carries all of the
scale. The per-note prior variance of channel $c$ is $B_{cc}\,[K_G]_{ii}$,
since the normalisation fixes the shape of the spectrum rather than the
diagonal of $K_G$, and the off-diagonal entries of $B$ let an observed
channel inform an unobserved one at a coupled note. Constraining $B$ to be
diagonal severs exactly this coupling and makes the three channels
independent scalar graph GPs, which is one of the constraint switches of
\S\ref{sec:special-cases}.

\section{Side information as kernels}\label{sec:features}
Two per-note feature sets summarise what is known about each note beyond the
graph. The first is a set of hand-built score features $X_{\mathrm{feat}}$,
$\sim$25 interpretable score-only columns plus a bias, covering the metric
position in the bar, pitch and melodic interval, contour, local rhythm,
chord and harmonic context, note density, and related quantities. The second
is the set of music-model embeddings $X_{\mathrm{emb}}$, the per-note
embeddings $h_i \in \R^{d}$ of the music model. Both sets enter the model in
the same way, and they differ only in what they encode.

The embedding of a note is read out before the model has seen that note's
own target. The embedding $h_i$ is read at the token preceding note $i$'s
velocity token, which is its duration token. A causal model's hidden state
at position $t$ already contains token $t$, so reading at the velocity token
would hand each note its own velocity, which is the target. The general rule
is that a read-out used at observed notes must never contain its own target.
A bidirectional model instead needs a leave-one-out read-out, in which each
note is read at a masked copy of its own velocity position. Under the strict
protocol, the velocities of held-out notes are also removed from the music
model's input, so the embedding of a held-out note depends only on the score
and on the observed notes' performances.

Each feature set enters the GP as a linear kernel with per-channel scales
$c_f \in \R^{3}_{\ge 0}$, contributing $\diag(c_f) \otimes X_f X_f^\top$ to
the covariance. Each feature matrix has its columns z-scored within the
piece, with a bias column appended, before entering the kernel. Because the
single per-channel scale $c_{f,c}$ is isotropic over columns, this
standardisation gives the columns equal prior weight. A GP whose mean is a linear model
$X_f \beta_c$ with unknown per-channel weights
$\beta_c \sim \N(0,\, c_{f,c} I)$ becomes, once each $\beta_c$ is integrated
out, a zero-mean GP with $c_{f,c}\, X_f X_f^\top$ added to channel $c$'s
kernel. A linear kernel \emph{is} the marginalized Bayesian linear mean. The
feature term is channel-diagonal, so the coregionalization $B$ acts on the
graph component only and cross-channel information flows exclusively through
$B \otimes K_G$. The regression weights are thereby re-inferred for every
piece, jointly with everything else, and their uncertainty flows into the
predictive variance.

The marginalized construction differs from the classical plug-in
alternative, which fixes the weights once. That alternative fits a point
estimate $\hat\beta$ across pieces, subtracts the resulting plug-in mean
$\mu = X_f \hat\beta$, and models the residual with the graph GP. It is one
of the constraint switches of \S\ref{sec:special-cases}, and the attribution
experiments locate most of the proposed model's accuracy gain precisely at
this switch (\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

\section{Observation model and posterior}
\label{sec:gpfirst}
Assembling \S\ref{sec:graphgp} to \S\ref{sec:features}, the latent field is
observed through independent Gaussian noise,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde y \;=\; y + e, \qquad e \sim \N\bigl(0,\, \Sigma_e\bigr),
  \qquad \Sigma_e = \diag(\varsigma^2) \otimes I,
  \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
with one learned noise variance $\varsigma_c^2$ per channel. The covariance
of the stacked observation vector, which states the whole model in one
equation, is
\begin{equation}
K \;=\;
\underbrace{B \otimes K_G(s)}_{\text{coupled graph GP}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_f \operatorname{diag}(c_f) \otimes X_f X_f^\top}_{\text{marginalized linear means}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\operatorname{diag}(\varsigma^2) \otimes I}_{\text{observation noise}} .
\label{eq:gpfirst}
\end{equation}
Masked (held-out) notes contribute no likelihood term. Write $o$ for the
observed entries. A held-out note hides all three of its channels, which
keeps the observed block Kronecker-compatible, and exact conjugate GP
regression then gives the posterior over the whole field in closed form:
\begin{equation}
  p(y \mid \tilde y_o) = \N\bigl(m,\, \Sy\bigr), \qquad
  m \;=\; C_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, \tilde y_o, \qquad
  \Sy \;=\; C \,-\, C_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, C_{o \cdot},
  \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the latent covariance, the first two terms of
\eqref{eq:gpfirst}, and $K_{oo}$ the observed block of $K$, noise included.
Masked notes are predicted from the observed ones through the conditional
Gaussian, which is the held-out imputation task. The per-note posterior
standard deviation is $\sigma_i = \sqrt{(\Sy)_{ii}}$.

By construction the latent field is a sum of independent Gaussian
components, the coupled graph GP and one draw per feature kernel, so this
posterior decomposes exactly by prior component (\S\ref{sec:attribution}).
Figure~\ref{fig:arch-phase1} lays the assembly out as a computation graph.
The graph-structure branch and the evidence branch fuse as a sum of
Kronecker terms into the joint kernel \eqref{eq:gpfirst}. The likelihood is
the block that changes most between settings, and the tracked-audio model
also drops the embedding kernel and uses per-entry masks
(Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}).

\subsection{Predictive variance for held-out observations}
A held-out observation at entry $i$ is $\tilde y_i = y_i + e_i$, so its
predictive variance must include the observation-noise variance of that
entry. This variance is written $\sigma_e^2$ throughout. In the present
model it is the channel's $\varsigma_c^2$, and in the per-channel form of
Appendix~\ref{app:lineage} it is the per-note $(\Sigma_e)_{ii}$. The
predictive variance is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}\bigl[\tilde y_i \mid \tilde y_{o}\bigr]
  \;=\; (\Sy)_{ii} + \sigma_e^2 .
  \label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
The latent variance $(\Sy)_{ii}$ collapses toward zero at well-determined
notes, so scoring held-out data against $(\Sy)_{ii}$ alone is severely
overconfident, and both NLL and coverage deteriorate sharply.
Equation~\eqref{eq:floor} is therefore required for all held-out
evaluation, and it is referred to below as the variance floor.

\section{Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:eb}
Every hyperparameter is learned jointly, per piece, by maximising the exact
log marginal likelihood, the evidence, of the observed block. The
hyperparameters are the graph shape $s$, the coregionalization $B$, the
feature scales $c_f$, and the per-channel noise $\varsigma^2$, and the
objective is
\begin{equation}
  \log p(\tilde y_o \mid \theta)
  \;=\; \log \N\bigl(\tilde y_o;\; 0,\; K_{oo}(\theta)\bigr),
  \qquad \theta = \bigl(s,\, B,\, \{c_f\},\, \varsigma^2\bigr),
  \label{eq:marglik}
\end{equation}
The objective is optimised by L-BFGS-B in log space for $s$, $c_f$ and
$\varsigma^2$ and in log-Cholesky space for $B$, so positivity and positive
semidefiniteness hold by construction. The graph geometry of
\eqref{eq:adjacency} is fixed. One evidence per piece decides the remaining
parameters, including how much to trust each information source, a choice
that a plug-in construction must make once, across pieces.

\subsection{Noise floor}
On a minority of mask realisations the unconstrained maximiser of
\eqref{eq:marglik} degenerates, with $\sigma_e^2 \to 0$, and produces an
overconfident posterior. The same principle as \eqref{eq:floor} therefore
applies to the fit itself. Each noise variance is constrained above a small
floor $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nv}}$ inside the optimisation, for example a
fixed fraction of the observed residual variance. The constraint binds only
where the unconstrained maximiser would fall below the floor.

\subsection{Validation of the fitted hyperparameters}
Rarely, the maximiser of \eqref{eq:marglik} lands on a degenerate
configuration that the noise floor cannot catch. A safeguard therefore fits
the hyperparameters to the observed notes, conditions the fitted model on
about $70\%$ of them, and screens its predictions on the remaining $30\%$.
The fit is rejected if, on that subset, its RMSE exceeds twice the mean-only
RMSE or its NLL exceeds the mean-only NLL by more than $0.5$ nats. On
rejection the fit falls back to a refit with a higher noise floor and then
to a conservative decoupled fit. The one failure mode it cannot detect is
described in \S\ref{sec:guard-gp}.

\section{Constraints and special cases}\label{sec:special-cases}
The model \eqref{eq:gpfirst} has four independent \textbf{constraint
switches}: decouple the channels by a diagonal $B$ (\S\ref{sec:coreg}), drop
the side information by $c_f = 0$, plug the mean in by replacing the
per-piece weights with a cross-piece point estimate $\hat\beta$
(\S\ref{sec:features}), and fix the graph parameters per channel. Turning
all four on yields the classical \textbf{two-stage pipeline}, a
point-estimate trend plus per-channel residual kriging on the graph. It is
the null model against which every hypothesis of the form ``relaxing
constraint X helps'' is tested, and in one regime, rendering from a short
excerpt, it remains the better tool (\S\ref{sec:boundary}). Its
precision-side form is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:lineage}.

\subsection{Exact nesting of the two-stage pipeline}
Constraining $B$ to be diagonal, dropping the feature kernels, and fixing
the graph recovers the two-stage pipeline's per-channel model
(Appendix~\ref{app:lineage}) exactly on the shared-shape slice. The joint
marginal likelihood \eqref{eq:marglik} equals the sum of the per-channel
ones under $(\lambda_c, \eta_c) = (1/B_{cc},\, s/B_{cc})$, and the equality
is verified numerically. The per-channel pipeline, fit independently, may
select three different shapes, so the nesting statement is about the model
family and not about coinciding optima. Re-inserting the
two-stage pipeline's own cross-piece mean as a fixed mean inside
\eqref{eq:gpfirst} reproduces its results almost unchanged. This is how the
proposed model's gain is attributed to the per-piece Bayesian feature
weighting rather than to coupling or to joint fitting as such
(\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

\chapter{Data and Experimental Protocol}\label{ch:data}

\section{Pretraining corpus (MAESTRO)}
The music model is pretrained on the MAESTRO corpus alone \cite{maestro}.
MAESTRO consists of competition piano performances captured on Disklavier
instruments as finely aligned audio and MIDI, of which only the MIDI is used
here. The music model is a decoder-only Transformer trained from scratch over
note-structured MIDI tokens by next-token prediction. The reference model used
throughout this thesis reaches a validation perplexity of $10.85$.

\section{Evaluation corpus (ASAP) and target extraction}
ASAP \cite{asap} supplies the aligned piano scores and performances, and
with them the piano targets $y = [\tau, \log r, v]$
through the score-to-performance beat warp $T(\cdot)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:variables}).
High-quality piano MIDI yields these targets almost exactly, so the
observation noise in the piano setting is negligible ($\Sigma_e \approx 0$).
The inference task is therefore not denoising but \emph{structured
prediction}: the score graph prior is exercised by imputing masked notes from
the rest of the performance.

\section{Strings-and-winds corpus (URMP)}\label{sec:urmp}
The strings-and-winds setting uses \textbf{URMP}, a corpus of 44
multi-instrument chamber pieces assembled from separately recorded monophonic
tracks. Each track comes with its audio, frame-level annotated pitch at a
10\,ms hop, note-level onset, pitch and duration annotations, and the score,
across 13 instruments of strings, woodwind and brass. Three measurements
govern its use. First, the pitch tracker was calibrated against the
annotated pitch before any model was fit: its median error is 2--5 cents
per instrument, its voicing recall is at least $0.95$, and its per-frame
confidence predicts its errors, with the share of frames more than 50 cents
off falling from $9.6\%$ to $0.4\%$ across confidence quintiles, so the
lowest quintile is discarded. Second, the split into development and
held-out pieces is fixed at the level of the composition, because
arrangements of one composition share identical track recordings
(\S\ref{sec:hygiene}). Third, no clean intonation ground truth exists. The
targets are estimator outputs, so held-out recovery measures agreement with
\emph{the estimator's} targets at masked notes. The same estimator run on
the annotated pitch curves gives targets independent of the tracker, though
not of the estimator, called the reference targets or the reference centre.
Figure~\ref{fig:vibrato-compare} shows the three layers, reference,
estimate and prediction, on two notes.

\section{Contamination control and the strict protocol}\label{sec:contam}
ASAP reuses many MAESTRO recordings, so every ASAP performance whose MAESTRO
twin was in pretraining is removed ($1036 \to 653$ aligned performances), and
the model is tested only on performances the music model has not seen. A
\emph{strict} protocol additionally blanks every hidden note's velocity from
the music model's input, so that no hidden target reaches the model through
the token stream. The cost of the strict protocol is negligible on error,
$+0.0003$ for the music-model system and $+0.0000$ for the feature and
music-model stack, and small on calibration, approximately $+0.014$ in NLL,
and the strict numbers are the ones reported. The music model's read-out is
taken before each note's own velocity (\S\ref{sec:features}), a structural
test enforces this, and the proposed model's full pipeline is audited
bitwise: corrupting held-out targets changes no prediction. One dependence
lies upstream of that audit: the velocity centring constant is the piece mean over all matched
notes, held-out ones included, an $O(1/N)$ coupling identical for every
system and recorded as a limitation.

\section{Development and held-out evaluation}\label{sec:hygiene}
Two disjoint sets play two different roles. In the piano and
strings-and-winds settings they are sets of pieces, and in the motif study
they are one development performance of each movement and further
performances of the same movements. Model selection used the \nPieces{}
piano development pieces and the development compositions of URMP, and every
number obtained there is labelled as a development number, since a set
reused for selection favours whatever survived it \cite{nosek2018}. The
held-out sets were consulted only after the modelling choices were settled.
For each held-out comparison the systems, the primary and secondary measures
and the decision rule were specified in advance, the comparison was run
once, and every result is reported, including those that did not support
the hypothesis. The piano held-out set comprises \nConfPieces{} further
contamination-filtered pieces from the same corpus shuffle, evaluated with
mask seeds disjoint from the development seeds. A further 30 piano pieces
outside every selection decision were evaluated afterwards without
pre-specification, as a replication of the within-model contrasts. The
strings-and-winds held-out set comprises 13 of the 44 URMP pieces, split at
the level of the composition (\S\ref{sec:urmp}).

The 13 URMP test pieces were used three times. The first use tested the
main strings-and-winds hypotheses (\S\ref{sec:phase2-conf}). The second
tested two follow-up hypotheses, a correlated timing-noise model and a tonal
pitch metric, developed on the development set with the first results known
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}). The third tested the phrase-scale waveform
hypothesis (\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}), the first use of those pieces at the
waveform level. Each later use is a weaker check than the one before, and
each result is reported with its use. The motif study's held-out material is
of another kind. Its hypotheses were developed on twenty-one Beethoven
movements, one performance each, and the held-out evaluation uses further
performances of the same movements, at most four per movement, with the
movement as the unit of analysis. The scores, annotations, masking rule and
seeds are those of the development study, and each performance supplies its
own aligned excerpt, edges, masks and targets. That evaluation tests
generalisation across performers of the same pieces, not across pieces.

Every comparison between two systems is paired: the two systems see
identical data, masks and seeds, so their difference is computed per unit
and only then aggregated. The unit differs by study. Piano contrasts pair
pieces, the main strings-and-winds and tonal contrasts pair track and seed,
the correlated-noise contrast resamples tracks, the waveform phrase contrast
pairs phrases, and the motif contrast pairs movements. The interval reported
with each paired difference is a percentile bootstrap over those units,
$B = 2000$ resamples, with the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of the
resampled mean. A difference is called significant when that interval
excludes zero, and tables mark such intervals with a superscript asterisk.
Pairing matters because piece difficulty varies far more than the gap
between systems.

\section{Sensitivity to the masking level}\label{sec:masksweep}
The main evaluation hides \maskRate{} of the notes. On the development
pieces, from 50\% hidden down to the leave-one-out limit, where the
hyperparameters are fitted on the full piece, the proposed model has the
lowest pooled RMSE at every level and coverage stays at 0.92--0.93. The
graph's paired contribution has an interval excluding zero at every level
except 10\% hidden.

\section{Metrics for recovery and calibration}\label{sec:metrics}
Predictions on held-out notes are scored on both recovery and calibration.
With held-out values $y_i$, predictive means $m_i$, and floored predictive
standard deviations $\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}} = \sqrt{(\Sy)_{ii} + \sigma_e^2}$
over $n$ held-out notes, the four measures are
\begin{align}
  \text{RMSE} &= \Bigl( \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i (y_i - m_i)^2 \Bigr)^{1/2},
  &
  \text{NLL} &= \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i \Bigl[ \tfrac12 \log\bigl(2\pi (\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}})^2\bigr)
        + \tfrac{(y_i - m_i)^2}{2 (\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}})^2} \Bigr],
  \\[3pt]
  \text{cov}@\gamma &= \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i \mathbf{1}\bigl[\, |y_i - m_i| \le z_{\gamma}\, \sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}} \bigr],
  &
  \text{cal-err} &= \sup_u \bigl|\, \hat F_{\mathrm{PIT}}(u) - u \,\bigr|,
\end{align}
where $z_\gamma$ is the two-sided standard-normal quantile for the level
$\gamma$, which is 0.9 throughout, and $\hat F_{\mathrm{PIT}}$ is the
empirical CDF of the probability integral transform
$u_i = \Phi_{\N}\bigl((y_i - m_i)/\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}}\bigr)$, which is
Uniform$(0,1)$ under a calibrated predictive distribution. Pooled over
notes, it is a marginal diagnostic and does not establish calibration piece
by piece. Here
$\Phi_{\N}$ is the standard normal CDF, distinct from the waveform synthesis
operator $\Phi(z)$. Recovery and calibration are reported separately, and
each held-out comparison names in advance the measure that decides it. The
pooled piano RMSE concatenates the three channels in their own units,
seconds, a log ratio and normalised velocity, so its value is dominated by
articulation, the channel with the largest spread. The per-channel results
(\S\ref{sec:perchannel}) report each channel separately.

\section{Attribution and posterior decomposition}\label{sec:attribution}

\subsection{Refitting controls}
Two development-set controls, each a refit of the model, identify the
mechanism behind the differences in Table~\ref{tab:headline-conf}. The first
is a fixed-mean control. Inserting the two-stage pipeline's cross-piece mean
as a fixed mean inside the coupled GP scores 0.390 / $-0.327$, statistically
at the level of the matching two-stage configuration with features, music
model and plain graph, while the two-stage pipeline with chord edges remains
ahead of this control by a margin whose interval excludes zero.
Coregionalization and joint fitting in themselves therefore contribute almost
nothing. The second is component removal, paired on the development set.
Removing the graph from the full model costs $+0.017$ RMSE and $+0.067$ NLL,
and removing the music-model embeddings costs $+0.008$ and $+0.034$, and both
intervals exclude zero. Per-piece Bayesian weighting of informative
features carries most of the recovery, the score graph contributes to
recovery and, more strongly, to the log score, and the embeddings add
information as a feature kernel rather than as a fixed mean.

\subsection{Exact decomposition by prior component}
The latent field is, by construction \eqref{eq:gpfirst}, a sum of
independent Gaussian components, the coupled graph GP and one draw per
feature kernel, and the observation adds independent noise:
\begin{equation}
  y \;=\; \sum_{\zeta} y^{(\zeta)}, \qquad
  y^{(\zeta)} \sim \N\bigl(0,\, C^{(\zeta)}\bigr) \text{ independent}, \qquad
  \tilde y \;=\; y + e,
  \label{eq:components}
\end{equation}
with $C^{(G)} = B \otimes K_G(s)$, $C^{(f)} = \operatorname{diag}(c_f)
\otimes X_f X_f^{\top}$, and $C = \sum_\zeta C^{(\zeta)}$. Here $\zeta$ indexes the
prior components, $\zeta \in \{G\} \cup \{f\}$, which in the piano setting
is $\zeta \in \{G, \mathrm{feat}, \mathrm{emb}\}$, and the primed $\zeta'$
below ranges over the same set. The components do \emph{not} correspond to
the three summands of \eqref{eq:gpfirst}: the middle summand there
contributes one component per feature set, and the noise summand contributes
none, for the reason given at the end of this subsection. Because the other
components and the noise are independent of $y^{(\zeta)}$, its
cross-covariance with the observations is just its own block,
$\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)},\, \tilde y_o\bigr) = C^{(\zeta)}_{\cdot o}$,
and conditioning the jointly Gaussian pair $\bigl(y^{(\zeta)}, \tilde y_o\bigr)$
gives each component its exact posterior:
\begin{align}
  m^{(\zeta)} \;&:=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\, y^{(\zeta)} \mid \tilde y_o \,\bigr]
  \;=\; C^{(\zeta)}_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, \tilde y_o,
  \label{eq:decomp}\\[2pt]
  \operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)},\, y^{(\zeta')} \mid \tilde y_o\bigr)
  \;&=\; \delta_{\zeta\zeta'}\, C^{(\zeta)}
  \;-\; C^{(\zeta)}_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, C^{(\zeta')}_{o \cdot}.
  \label{eq:decomp-cov}
\end{align}
Two exactness identities follow by linearity, and both are verified by unit
tests: summing \eqref{eq:decomp} over components recovers the posterior mean,
and summing \eqref{eq:decomp-cov} over all pairs recovers the posterior
covariance,
\begin{equation}
  m \;=\; \sum_\zeta m^{(\zeta)}, \qquad
  \Sy \;=\; \sum_{\zeta}\sum_{\zeta'}
  \operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)},\, y^{(\zeta')} \mid \tilde y_o\bigr).
  \label{eq:decomp-total}
\end{equation}
This is the functional-ANOVA decomposition of an additive GP. The prior
components are independent. A posteriori, the cross-terms of
\eqref{eq:decomp-cov} are negative wherever two components can explain the
same variation, which is the explaining-away effect, so their normalised
diagonals measure per-note redundancy between information sources. The
diagnostics of the next subsection are built directly on
\eqref{eq:decomp}--\eqref{eq:decomp-total}.

Observation noise is not a component of \eqref{eq:decomp}, by independence
rather than by convention. A held-out note's noise is never observed and is
independent of every observation, so its conditional mean is identically
zero. Noise enters only through the shared weights: $K_{oo}$ contains
$\Sigma_e$, so the fitted noise level sets how much of the observed variation
is explained at all, a uniform shrinkage of every component rather than a
competing direction. At \emph{observed} notes the decomposition does gain an
exact noise term, $\mathbb{E}[\,e_o \mid \tilde y_o\,] = \tilde y_o -
\mathbb{E}[\,y_o \mid \tilde y_o\,]$, which is the residual the model
declines to explain. In the predictive variance of a held-out observation the
noise reappears additively as the floor \eqref{eq:floor}, where it is
directly comparable to the components' posterior variances. Finally, the
split is the evidence-fitted model's own attribution, since it depends on the
learned $B$, $c_f$ and $\varsigma^2$. It describes how the fitted model
divides labour and makes no causal statement.

\subsection{Covariance shares, component correlations and per-piece dominance}
A third control needs no refitting at all: the exact component decomposition
of the posterior, \eqref{eq:decomp}--\eqref{eq:decomp-total}, is read off
the fitted model itself on the development pieces. The covariance shares
reported below are computed over $\nPieces \times 4$ anchor masks, and the
component correlations and the per-piece dominance and disagreement figures
over the first anchor mask only. Two diagnostics are derived from the
decomposition, per channel. The first, the covariance share, allocates the
posterior mean's variation over the held-out notes $\mathcal{H}$ of a piece
to the components,
\begin{equation}
  \omega^{(\zeta)} \;=\;
  \frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{i \in \mathcal{H}}\bigl(m^{(\zeta)}_i,\, m_i\bigr)}
       {\operatorname{Var}_{i \in \mathcal{H}}\bigl(m_i\bigr)},
  \qquad \sum_\zeta \omega^{(\zeta)} = 1
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
and the normalisation is exact by bilinearity applied to
$m = \sum_\zeta m^{(\zeta)}$, so cross-component interactions land inside the
shares rather than in an unexplained remainder. The second, the per-note
component correlation, normalises the diagonal of the posterior
cross-covariance \eqref{eq:decomp-cov} by the two component standard
deviations,
\begin{equation}
  \rho^{(\zeta\zeta')}_i \;=\;
  \frac{\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)}_i,\, y^{(\zeta')}_i \mid \tilde y_o\bigr)}
       {\sigma^{(\zeta)}_i\, \sigma^{(\zeta')}_i},
  \qquad
  \sigma^{(\zeta)}_i = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)}_i \mid \tilde y_o\bigr)},
  \label{eq:compcorr}
\end{equation}
which is negative where the two information sources explain the same
variation and is reported below as its average over held-out notes.

The measured shares put most of the mean on the score-feature kernel on every
channel ($0.69/0.60/0.40$ for $\tau$ / $\log r$ / $v$), give the embeddings
their largest share on velocity ($0.34$, the channel where they carry signal
the hand-built features lack), and leave the graph's share of the mean modest
($0.12/0.22/0.27$). This is consistent with the two refitting controls:
feature weighting recovers the mean, and the graph's contribution
concentrates in the covariance. The component correlations
\eqref{eq:compcorr} complete the picture. Graph by embeddings is near zero
($-0.004/-0.025/-0.059$ per channel), so the two information sources act as
complements rather than redundantly, while the visible explaining-away sits
between the two feature kernels (score by embeddings, $-0.18$ on velocity)
and between graph and score features ($-0.19/-0.18$ on articulation and
velocity). Figure~\ref{fig:posterior-components}
shows one development piece's posterior mean split into its component curves.

\section{An influential timing tail}\label{sec:guard-gp}
One held-out evaluation, one piece and one seed on the timing channel, has

the two-stage pipeline add nothing to the proposed model at the operating

\section{Error spotting}
Error spotting corrupts a small fraction of notes and ranks every note by
its suspiciousness. Scoring each note by its joint leave-one-out predictive
surprise, the proposed model reaches an AUROC of $0.995$ on velocity,
$0.986$ on articulation and $0.978$ on timing, above every structure-free
baseline.

\section{Denoising}
Denoising recovers the true values of the per-note variables from added
noise. With the noise level known, structure is better than per-note
shrinkage on both error and calibration, with all 8 paired differences having
intervals that exclude zero and coverage near $0.90$. The feature-only GP
with known noise is slightly better still, with RMSE $0.182$ and $0.276$ at
the two noise levels against $0.186$ and $0.282$ for the two-stage pipeline,
and coverage $0.90$. A
negative result holds unchanged under both models: estimating the noise level
blind from a single piece is unreliable in \emph{both} parameterizations,
because the intervals collapse, so transcription noise must be treated as
known for each system.

\section{Completion from an excerpt}
Completion predicts the expression of the rest of a piece from its opening.
Interpolation, with scattered hidden notes, is where the model is clearly
better, with RMSE falling from $0.450 \to 0.393$ and NLL from $0.21 \to 0.10$
at half the notes observed. When the prediction extrapolates far past the
excerpt, the music-model prediction carries it and the graph is neutral or
slightly harmful. The proposed model makes this boundary sharp. Its per-piece
feature weights, fit on the opening excerpt alone, can extrapolate wildly:
with a quarter of the piece observed as a prefix, the mean RMSE across
pieces is $16009$, the median $0.478$ and the worst piece $480266$, and
weakly determined weights also fail occasionally under random masking. The
cross-piece read-out head, which never adapts to the piece, is the
appropriate tool for excerpt extrapolation. Per-piece Bayesian
adaptation needs observed coverage of the feature space, and rendering from a
short excerpt does not provide it.

\section{Selective prediction}
Selective prediction discards the least-confident predictions and measures
the error on those that remain. Discarding lowers error most clearly for
articulation, where keeping the confident half cuts its RMSE from $0.83 \to
0.54$, and for catching the model's own misfires, where a timing blow-up at
$0.61$ overall falls to $0.07$ on the confident half. Only the graph provides
the per-note confidence that this triage needs, because the no-graph
baselines rank at piece level only. Under the proposed model the triage is
slightly better still, with a confident-half RMSE of $0.076$ against $0.080$
at matched abstention.

\section{Two boundaries}\label{sec:boundary}
Across the tasks, two boundaries emerge. Boundary (1), the structure
boundary, holds under both models: the graph prior helps whenever notes are
judged one by one for accuracy or confidence, and does not help when a
performance is collapsed into a summary. Boundary (2), the adaptation
boundary, belongs to the proposed model: per-piece Bayesian feature weighting
is better on the interpolation tasks reported here and breaks down at
excerpt extrapolation, where the cross-piece head remains the appropriate
tool.

\chapter{Results: Strings and Winds from Tracked Audio}
\label{ch:phase2}\label{sec:phase2}
This chapter carries the proposed model to strings and winds, where the
performer controls pitch and the targets carry real measurement
uncertainty.
Nothing in the latent prior changes. The graph and feature kernels of
\eqref{eq:gpfirst}, its first two terms, the hyperparameter fit
(\S\ref{sec:eb}) and the exact conjugate posterior (\S\ref{sec:gpfirst})
are reused. One term is absent: the music model is trained on piano, so
its embedding kernel takes no part here, and the evidence branch consists
of the hand-built score features alone. The observation-noise term belongs
to the likelihood, and the likelihood is what changes. Three things
therefore change, and the first section specifies each of them: the
channel set, the way the targets are obtained, and the observation noise,
which stops being negligible.


\section{The model in this setting: channels, targets and noise}
\label{sec:phase2-delta}

\subsection{The channel set}
A fixed-pitch keyboard affords no performer-controlled intonation, and its
dynamics is a MIDI velocity. Neither holds for strings and winds. In this
setting the per-note \textbf{bundle} of channels becomes
\begin{equation}
  y_i \;=\; \bigl[\, \tau_i,\ \log r_i,\ \ell_i,\ c_i,\ f_i^{\mathrm{vib}},\ \gamma_i,\ d_i^{\mathrm{vib}} \,\bigr]
  \;\in\; \R^{k},
  \label{eq:phase2-channels}
\end{equation}
one scalar per note of the known score per channel. In musical terms the
channels are the following. The timing $\tau_i$ is the onset residual
against the score-time warp, here anchored by the annotated onsets
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-delta}), and $\log r_i$ is the articulation ratio. The
intonation $c_i$ is how sharp or flat the held centre of the note sits
against equal temperament, in cents, with the vibrato removed. The vibrato
rate $f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ is the number of oscillations per second, the
extent $\gamma_i$ how far the pitch swings at its peak, in cents, and the
onset delay $d_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ how long after the note begins the
oscillation starts \cite{seashore1936}. The loudness $\ell_i$ replaces the
MIDI velocity and is read from the note's own audio. The note's span is cut
into four equal chunks $x^{(q)}$, and
\begin{align}
  \ell_i &\;=\; \frac{1}{4} \sum_{q=1}^{4} \log \mathrm{rms}\bigl(x^{(q)}\bigr)
  \;-\; \overline{\ell}_{\mathrm{track}},
  \label{eq:loudness}\\
  \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}[\ell_i] &\;=\; \frac{s^2_{\mathrm{chunks}}}{4}.
  \nonumber
\end{align}
The four chunks are internal replicates, so their spread gives a standard
error that grows when the level changes inside the note, and subtracting
the track mean keeps only relative loudness, since absolute level is
arbitrary per recording. The delay is identifiable on $95$--$97\%$ of
vibrato-identifiable notes, with a median agreement of $18$\,ms between
tracker and reference, so it is a channel and $k = 7$ as written. The
corpus has no annotated offset chain from which $\log r_i$ could be
computed, so the evaluated bundle has six channels.

None of these values is annotated by hand, and they come from different
measurements: the pitch-derived channels from the per-note fit of the
tracked pitch curve described next, loudness from the note's audio, and
timing from the annotated onsets, each with its own uncertainty. This is
why the observation noise of \eqref{eq:phase2-noise} becomes substantial in
this setting. The corpus's annotated pitch curves calibrate the tracker and
define the reference, and they never serve as channel values.

\subsection{From a pitch curve to per-note targets}
Let $f_0^{(i)}(t)$ be the fundamental estimated on the sounding interval
of note $i$, restricted to the samples the tracker marks as voiced. Its
deviation from the equal-tempered target, in cents, is
\begin{equation}
  c_i(t) \;=\; 1200\,\log_2 \frac{f_0^{(i)}(t)}{f_{\mathrm{ref}}\, 2^{\,(p_i - 69)/12}} ,
  \label{eq:cents-curve}
\end{equation}
with $f_{\mathrm{ref}} = 440$\,Hz the frequency of A4, whose MIDI number
is $69$, so that the denominator is the equal-tempered frequency of the
written pitch $p_i$. This curve is not itself a target, because it mixes
a static mistuning with an oscillation. Following the standard sinusoidal
description of vibrato \cite{seashore1936,prame1994,prame1997}, it is
decomposed as
\begin{equation}
  c_i(t) \;=\; \underbrace{c_i}_{\text{intonation}} \;+\; u_i(t) \;+\; \epsilon_i(t),
  \qquad
  u_i(t) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \gamma_i \sin\!\bigl(2\pi f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}\,(t - t_i - d_i^{\mathrm{vib}})\bigr), & t \ge t_i + d_i^{\mathrm{vib}},\\[2pt]
    0, & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:vibrato}
\end{equation}
with $t_i$ the performed onset. Thus $c_i$ (cents) is the vibrato-free
centre, $\gamma_i$ (cents) the vibrato extent, $f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ (Hz)
its rate, and $d_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ (s) the delay before it begins.

Estimating $(c_i, f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}, \gamma_i, d_i^{\mathrm{vib}})$ is a
small nonlinear least-squares fit per note on the voiced samples
\cite{driedger2016}. A continuous sinusoid supplies
$(c_i, \gamma_i, f_i^{\mathrm{vib}})$, and the delayed-onset form of
\eqref{eq:vibrato} supplies the delay, each with a variance obtained by
linearisation. The centre $c_i$ is not the mean of $c_i(t)$, which
coincides with the vibrato-free centre only over a whole number of cycles
\cite{dalessandro1994}.

The observation rule fixes every threshold by the development
measurements of \S\ref{sec:urmp}. Note $i$'s sample set is
\begin{equation}
  J_i \;=\; \bigl\{\, \text{frames } j:\; t_j \in [t_i,\, t_i + \delta_i),\;
  \text{voiced}_j,\; \pi_j \ge q_{0.2}(\text{track}) \,\bigr\},
  \label{eq:framerule}
\end{equation}
so that the tracker's per-frame confidence $\pi_j$ is thresholded at the
track's lowest quintile. The intonation entry is observed if $|J_i| \ge 4$
and $|\hat c_i| \le 150$ cents, the octave-failure rule. The vibrato
entries additionally require at least $8$ voiced frames and $1.5$ cycles at
the fitted rate. Every other entry is missing for the model.

\subsection{Per-channel missingness}
The identifiability rule makes missingness structural rather than
incidental. A short note may support $c_i$ but no vibrato parameters, so
a target can be missing for some channels of a note and not for others.
We call each (note, channel) pair an \textbf{entry}, and the resulting
pattern of observed entries the \textbf{observation mask}. The conjugate
posterior of \S\ref{sec:gpfirst} assumes per-note masks, which is exactly
what keeps the observed block Kronecker-compatible (\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}).
Per-entry missingness breaks that structure but not the exactness: the
conjugate posterior conditions on an arbitrary observed index set, so
what must give way is the per-note mask interface and any
Kronecker-structured shortcut, not conjugacy. The model therefore accepts
per-entry observation masks. Figure~\ref{fig:phase2-datapoint} shows one data point of each kind.

\eqref{eq:loudness} on the long note, where the crescendo inside the note

-0.18$.\label{fig:phase2-datapoint}}

The missingness has two mechanisms \cite{rubin1976}. The
evaluation's hidden notes are missing completely at random by
construction, through a seeded coin per note. The estimator's missing
entries are not: the identifiability rule removes a vibrato entry exactly
when the note's vibrato is short or weak, so this missingness is
informative, missing \emph{not} at random and dependent on the value it
hides. Two consequences follow. The held-out scores stay interpretable,
but for the identifiable sub-population only: random masking inside an
informatively filtered set of entries is unbiased for the notes whose
vibrato the estimator can measure, not for all notes. And the posteriors
the model supplies at estimator-missing entries borrow from vibrato-rich
observed neighbours and may therefore be biased toward audible vibrato,
of which the delay posterior's occasional sub-zero stretches are the
visible signature. Filled entries are posterior extrapolations under the
prior and are to be read as such, not as recovered measurements.

\subsection{Heteroscedastic observation noise}
On piano the targets are read from clean MIDI and $\Sigma_e \approx 0$.
Here every target is an estimate, and its uncertainty is both known and
unequal across notes: the $f_0$ tracker reports a per-frame periodicity
or confidence, and the per-note fit of \eqref{eq:vibrato} returns a
parameter covariance. Writing $\sigma_{e,ij}^2$ for the variance of
channel $j$ of note $i$,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde y \;=\; y + e, \qquad
  e \sim \N\bigl(0,\ \Sigma_e\bigr), \qquad
  \Sigma_e = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sigma_{e,ij}^2\bigr),
  \label{eq:phase2-noise}
\end{equation}
which replaces the per-channel term $\operatorname{diag}(\varsigma^2)\otimes I$ of
\eqref{eq:gpfirst} by a full diagonal over (note, channel). Gaussian
conditioning stays exact with entry-specific variances, so the posterior and
the marginal likelihood are unchanged in form. The noise floor and the
safeguard of \S\ref{sec:eb} carry over.

The estimator-supplied variances enter the likelihood unchanged, and are
called the \textbf{given variances}. A learned per-channel scale is the
alternative for a mis-calibrated tracker. On synthetic pieces with known
per-note truth, where the full pipeline recovers missing vibrato entries at
roughly $4\times$ better than prior scale with nominal coverage, the given
variances are better calibrated and the learned scale over-shrinks the
vibrato-extent channel (coverage $0.76$--$0.81$ against $0.86$--$0.87$). On
the development tracks the given variances are again the better-calibrated
choice.

\subsection{What the per-note compression discards}
The channels are time-averages within the note. A development drift study
(Appendix~\ref{app:drift}) finds a real within-note drift of the intonation
centre on about two thirds of notes, detected on the tracked and on the
reference curves independently, with a median of $10.5$ cents per note.
Drift slopes are almost uncorrelated between consecutive notes ($+0.03$,
against $+0.59$ for timing), so a chain over notes could not use them,
although their association with other graph neighbours and their effect on
coverage were not tested. The discarded structure belongs to the waveform
setting, whose pitch-curve model is built to carry it.

\subsection{The score-time warp and the alignment error}
The piano setting obtained the score-time warp $T(\cdot)$ from an aligned
beat grid supplied with the MIDI. There is no MIDI here. $T$ must come
from audio-to-score alignment, and its error propagates directly into
$\tau_i$, since $\tau_i = t_i - T(b_i)$ by definition. Worse, alignment
error is correlated along score time, which is precisely the structure
the graph prior expects expressive timing to have. A model fit naively
will therefore absorb some alignment error as expressive timing and
report it with confident error bars. On URMP the note-level onset
annotations anchor the warp without any audio aligner on
76 of 78 development tracks, half by exact score-order matching and half
by a pitch DTW. The two tracks without a usable warp remain in the
evaluation with their $\tau$ entries missing, not dropped. The
feasibility statistic uses a local leave-one-out warp: with score-onset
and performed-onset pairs $(b_j, t_j)$, each note gets a tempo line with
slope $\hat\varphi_i$ and intercept $\hat\psi_i$ fitted from its
neighbours alone,
\begin{align}
  (\hat\varphi_i,\, \hat\psi_i)
  &\;=\; \arg\min_{\varphi,\,\psi}\; \sum_{0 < |j - i| \le 8}
     \bigl(t_j - \varphi\, b_j - \psi\bigr)^2,
  \label{eq:localwarp}\\
  \tau_i &\;=\; t_i \,-\, \bigl(\hat\varphi_i\, b_i + \hat\psi_i\bigr),
  \nonumber
\end{align}
so the note being judged never enters its own warp. The result is a
median $\operatorname{std}(\tau)$ of $79$\,ms with lag-1 neighbour
correlation $+0.59$, that is, piano-scale timing with exactly the
structure the graph prior models. On this basis $\tau$ is a channel of
the evaluated bundle, and its noise row is the tempo line's leave-one-out
predictive variance, which carries the aligner error's scale but not its
correlation along score time (\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}).

\subsection{The score graph and the tonal metric}
The graph construction \eqref{eq:adjacency} carries over with one shared
adjacency for all channels. The corpus annotates note onsets in seconds and
carries no beat grid, so in this setting, and in the waveform setting of
Chapter~\ref{ch:phase3}, the onset $b_i$ of the graph is the annotated onset
in seconds and the time length $\ell_b = 2$ spans two seconds of
performance time. The annotated onsets therefore enter both the graph and
the timing residual, and timing recovery is conditional on that alignment.
Intonation is the one channel whose target is pitch, and
\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse} compares the semitone metric with the
circle-of-fifths metric \eqref{eq:tonaladj} on it.

\section{Two follow-up hypotheses on the held-out test set}
\label{sec:phase2-reuse}
Two hypotheses were tested on a second use of the same 13 test pieces,
with the targets, masks, entries and seeds of the first evaluation. They
were developed on the development set, and the plain-graph test results
were known when they were chosen (\S\ref{sec:hygiene}).

\subsection{Correlated timing noise}
Alignment error is correlated along score time, whereas the main timing
likelihood assumes independent errors. The revised
noise model places an AR(1) block in the timing channel's noise
covariance, $\Sigma_{\tau}(\rho)_{ij} = \sqrt{d_i d_j}\,\rho^{|i-j|}$ in
note order, with $d_i$ the given entry variances and $\rho$ chosen per
(track, seed) by the observed block's marginal likelihood on a fixed
grid. Held-out timing is predicted through the full covariance including
the noise cross-terms. Setting $\rho = 0$ recovers the diagonal
timing-noise baseline. The primary measure was the paired timing NLL, cluster-bootstrapped over the
40 unique tracks. The paired RMSE was a secondary measure that did not
enter the decision, and coverage and the share of (track, seed)
evaluations with $\rho > 0$ were descriptive.

The primary measure supported the hypothesis. The paired difference in
timing NLL is $-0.121$ with an interval of $[-0.156, -0.089]$, which
excludes zero, against a development basis of $-0.111$. The secondary
measure agrees: the paired RMSE difference is $-0.011$
$[-0.016, -0.007]$ s, from $0.058 \to 0.047$ s. Coverage stayed at
nominal, $0.908$ against the diagonal's $0.911$, a difference of $-0.0025$
$[-0.0095, +0.0049]$, and $\rho > 0$ on 79 of 80 (track, seed) evaluations,
with median $0.45$. The comparator differs from the main
evaluation's: the original contrast, graph against no graph, gave no clear
timing log-score gain, and the follow-up shows that correlated noise
improves on diagonal noise with the graph present.

\subsection{The tonal metric}
On the development set, replacing the semitone metric of
\eqref{eq:adjacency} by the circle-of-fifths metric \eqref{eq:tonaladj}
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-delta}) improved held-out intonation on both axes and
re-imposed a small timing penalty. On the test pieces the primary outcome
is the paired intonation NLL, tonal minus plain graph, under the given
variances. Intonation RMSE is secondary, with a stated power of
$0.52$--$0.63$, and the timing contrast descriptive.

The primary hypothesis was not supported. The paired intonation NLL
difference is $-0.023$ with an interval of $[-0.049, +0.001]$: the mean
lies on the predicted side but the interval reaches zero, against a
development basis of $-0.050$ $[-0.072, -0.029]$. The secondary measure
was supported, with a recovery difference of $-0.312$ cents
$[-0.599, -0.081]$ (development $-0.213$). The predicted timing penalty
did not appear under the given variances ($-0.002$ $[-0.011, +0.007]$),
and the metric stays exploratory. Two descriptive contrasts accompany the
hypotheses: a small adverse loudness calibration difference, $+0.020$
$[+0.003, +0.041]$, and the tonal graph improving on the no-graph ablation
in intonation calibration, $-0.053$ $[-0.099, -0.010]$, where the plain
graph had not ($-0.029$).

\chapter{The Waveform as the Observation}\label{ch:phase3}\label{app:phase3}\label{sec:phase3-math}
In this setting the observation is the waveform itself. The studies use
the development set, except one phrase-scale comparison on held-out
strings-and-winds tracks (\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}). In the piano and strings-and-winds settings the observation is note-level,
and a fraction of notes is masked to make the prior testable. Here the
observation is the audio, and the studies differ in what they see: single
notes and short chains cut at annotated boundaries, a fusion study in which
the waveform measurement enters the tracked bundle, and masked-element tests
of the score prior. Inferring every note of a whole performance jointly
under the graph prior remains open.

The audio likelihood replaces the per-note observation $\tilde y$ with the
waveform $x \in \R^{M}$:
\begin{equation}
  x \;=\; \Phi(z)\, a + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \N(0,\, K_n),
  \label{eq:waveform}
\end{equation}
where $z$ collects the nonlinear position variables (tempo, timing,
intonation, vibrato), $\Phi(z) \in \R^{M \times 2HN}$ is the deterministic
harmonic synthesis basis, and $a \in \R^{2HN}$ are the stacked per-note
harmonic amplitudes. The basis is the horizontal concatenation of one
$M \times 2H$ block per note, two quadrature columns per harmonic,
$\cos\phi_k(t), \sin\phi_k(t)$ and cumulative phase
$\phi_k(t) = 2\pi k \int_0^t f_0(s)\, ds$ determined by $z$, where $H$ counts
the harmonics. Per-note amplitudes are shaped in time by an amplitude
envelope $A_i(t)$, which multiplies the columns of $\Phi(z)$ belonging to
note $i$. Loudness lives in the amplitudes $a$ and articulation in the
envelope supports, and neither is placed under the graph prior in this
setting. With a Gaussian amplitude prior
$a \sim \N(\mu_a, \Sigma_a)$, the amplitudes marginalise exactly:
\begin{align}
  p(a \mid x, z) &= \N(m_a,\, S_a), \qquad
  S_a = \bigl( \Sigma_a^{-1} + \Phi^\top K_n^{-1} \Phi \bigr)^{-1}, \qquad
  m_a = S_a \bigl( \Sigma_a^{-1} \mu_a + \Phi^\top K_n^{-1} x \bigr),
  \label{eq:amp-post}\\[2pt]
  p(x \mid z) &= \N\bigl( x;\; \Phi\, \mu_a,\; \Phi\, \Sigma_a\, \Phi^\top + K_n \bigr),
  \label{eq:collapsed}
\end{align}
where $\Phi = \Phi(z)$, $\mu_a \in \R^{2HN}$ and
$\Sigma_a \in \R^{2HN \times 2HN}$ for $H$ harmonics with two quadrature
columns each. The implementation specialises the noise covariance to
$K_n = \sigma^2 I$, and the amplitude envelope $A_i(t)$ belongs to the
specification but is not yet implemented. Nonlinear pitch curves and
boundaries are fitted on local excerpts by gradient MAP and a Laplace
approximation (\S\ref{sec:phase3-curve}), and coupling all notes of a
performance in one waveform fit is the open step. The coupled graph prior, the first two
terms of \eqref{eq:gpfirst}, plays the role of the structured component of
$p(z)$.

Dashed: not implemented. The experiments of this chapter fit local excerpts

\section{Scalar intonation from the waveform}
\label{sec:phase3-study}

The scalar study measures the error and the interval coverage of the
intonation centre inferred from the waveform, against centres estimated
from the annotated development pitch curves.

\subsection{Design of the single-note study}
Each observation is one development note's audio, cropped at its annotated
boundaries: $376$ notes from seven tracks spanning all three instrument
families. The $f_0$ curve is parameterised by the variables of the bundle
(eqs.~\eqref{eq:cents-curve}--\eqref{eq:vibrato}), with the vibrato
extent, rate and delay of vibrato-identifiable notes taken from the tracked
estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}, so the intonation centre is the one
quantity inferred from the waveform. The design is the harmonic basis of
\eqref{eq:waveform} with amplitudes piecewise constant over the four chunks
of \eqref{eq:loudness} and integrated out analytically, and the posterior
of $c$ is evaluated on a grid. Four position models are compared: a
constant centre, then added correlated residual noise, a linear drift, and
a marginalised curve deviation. Each posterior is scored against the
reference centre by absolute error and coverage at nominal $90\%$, and the
same machinery is applied to synthetic notes on which the model is true.

\subsection{Accuracy and calibration against the reference}

The best variant reaches a median of $2.29$ cents from the reference,
approaching the estimator chain's $2.01$ overall and improving on it on the
winds ($2.67$ vs $3.27$), the family in which the tracker's failure modes
are concentrated (Table~\ref{tab:phase3-study}). Every model refinement
improves accuracy, yet coverage against the reference stays at
$0.01$--$0.03$: a better mean model shrinks the posterior width as fast as
it shrinks the error, and correlated residual noise does not help, because
the misfit is concentrated in band, at the harmonics. The machinery itself
is calibrated: on model-true synthetic notes the same posterior covers at
exactly $0.90$ (median $|z| = 0.53$).

\subsection{The estimand gap and its consequence for the bundle}

Together these three facts locate the overconfidence. It is an
\textbf{estimand gap}: the constant the harmonic model estimates and the
constant the NLLS estimator defines on the annotated curve are different
functionals of the same performance, about two cents apart, and no
within-model refinement can close a gap between two different questions,
which is why none of the three refinements did. The waveform posterior should enter the six-channel bundle the way
every estimator does, as a measurement whose noise row is its posterior
variance plus an empirically calibrated per-note discrepancy floor at the
observed $\sim 2$-cent scale. The floor calibrates the measurement against
the estimator targets of the bundle. It does not make the waveform's own
bands cover the reference.

On the same seven development tracks the waveform posterior then enters as
a seventh bundle channel, its coupling is learned per piece by the evidence, and the
discrepancy floor is calibrated from visible notes only, at a median of
$3.1$ cents, which is the estimand-gap scale found automatically. The
waveform measurement stays available at the target notes while their
tracked entries are hidden, as it would be in deployment. Intonation
recovery against the estimator targets then improves on every track-seed
pair: the paired difference is $-1.21$ cents and the difference in NLL is
$-0.24$, both with intervals excluding zero, and coverage is held. Removing the floor degrades
calibration against the reference by $+0.22$, with an interval excluding
zero, while improving nothing, so the floor performs its designed role.

\section{The differentiable curve posterior}
\label{sec:phase3-curve}

\subsection{Design of the curve study}

The curve model uses the same amplitude-marginalised likelihood
\eqref{eq:collapsed}. Inference over a within-note pitch curve, a constant $c$ plus knot
values $u$ under the two-component kernel of the estimator studies, here
acting as a prior over $u$, then runs by gradient descent, and a full joint
Laplace approximation at the MAP supplies bands and a per-note evidence.
This adopts the differentiable-synthesis idea of the DDSP line of work
\cite{ddsp} at the implementation level only. The objective remains the
exact amplitude-marginalised likelihood and the prior remains the Gaussian
process, with no neural encoder and no spectral surrogate loss. The five
kernel hyperparameters are fitted by evidence ascent, shared across notes.
Under the fitted
values both component scales loosen by a factor of $2$--$3\times$ while the
vibrato mean stays at $5.5$--$5.7$\,Hz. A matched-rank spectral-mixture
deviation prior \cite{wilson2013,remes2017} in place of the scalar study's
bump basis gave no clear gain once pieces were clustered.

waveform on development notes (violin, cello, clarinet). Each panel shows
the inferred curve and its band against the corpus's reference frames and

\subsection{Curve-level measurements}

The curve posterior is evaluated on the 376 development notes of
\S\ref{sec:phase3-study}. Against the reference pitch frames the curve posterior
with the evidence-fitted prior reaches a median RMSE of $3.16$ cents
against the pYIN frames' $4.09$, and the hand-set prior improves on the
tracker on 56\% of notes. It is clearly better on winds ($2.3$ vs $5.2$) and still
behind the tracker on strings ($4.9$ vs $3.1$), which reproduces the family
split of the scalar study, with a heavier failure tail (q90 $\sim 11$ vs
$8.6$ cents). The evidence-fitted prior is better than the hand-set scales
on 70\% of notes. The second concerns calibration. The Laplace band is a
parameter band and covers the annotation frames at $0.32$--$0.36$, which is
the estimand gap of \S\ref{sec:phase3-study} reproduced at the curve level,
while coverage on model-true synthetic notes is nominal.

The third measurement concerns integration into the bundle. Replacing the
scalar waveform channel of the bundle study by the curve's realised-average
read-out, a linear functional of the Laplace posterior, reproduces the
integration gain, a difference of $-1.36$ cents on all 14 pairs with an
interval excluding zero, and halves the empirical discrepancy floor
($3.1 \to 1.8$ cents). Reading out the estimator's own functional from the
inferred curve thus closes about half the estimand gap by construction,
and beyond this the curve and scalar read-outs show no measurable
difference at $n=14$. On two-note excerpts with a free boundary between
complementary sigmoid windows, gradient fitting converges independently of
its starting point, to an \emph{acoustic} transition $11$--$87$\,ms (median
$25$) from the annotated \emph{musical} onset.

\subsection{Joint chains and note transitions}
Chains of 3--5 consecutive notes are fitted jointly, per-note curves plus
boundaries, on 47 chains and 154 boundaries from four of the seven tracks.
A boundary is modelled as two sigmoid edges, the old note's fade-out and
the new note's fade-in, with a free width between them. The fitted widths
collapse to the 1\,ms minimum on almost every boundary, and on the 128
non-unison boundaries, under two starting conditions, they are better than
a fixed 10\,ms width in 77\% of 256 paired fits, so a transition is sharp to
a harmonic likelihood, with a median edge error of about 18\,ms. On these
annotation-aligned excerpts, with chains selected and cropped at the
annotated notes and interior boundaries initialised from the local
tempo-line prediction rather than the annotation, the joint fit halves the
median boundary error, $39.4 \to 19.5$\,ms. This is boundary refinement
given a local alignment. Joint chains are less accurate on the curves than
single-note fits (reference-frame RMSE $4.9$--$5.2$ vs $3.16$ cents,
excluding the boundary regions), and the residual ${\sim}18$\,ms measures
the difference between the acoustic transition and the annotated musical
onset.

\section{The score prior inside the waveform inference}
\label{sec:phase3-priorvalue}
This section places the score prior inside the waveform inference,
coupling intonation across notes and timing across boundaries at three
spans: short chains, note boundaries and phrases. The question has two
sides: whether score-informed structure adds value where the acoustic
evidence is absent, and whether it costs anything where the evidence is
strong. Two hypotheses are distinguished. The weak form holds that score
structure at least matches a naive temporal prior, and the strong form
holds that it carries information beyond the common offset shared by the
notes of a chain. Both are evaluated on the development set, and the weak
form at phrase scale also on held-out tracks (\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}).

\subsection{Design of the prior-value experiment}

Three cross-note priors are placed over one latent per element of a chain,
the per-note intonation constant $c$, or for the boundary span the per-joint
offset from the tempo line, at matched marginal variance and with
everything else identical: the amplitude-marginalised likelihood, the data,
the optimiser and its budget. The independent prior is $\N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 I)$.
The temporal prior is $\N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 K_{\mathrm{chain}})$ with the
note-order chain graph. The score-graph prior is $\N(0,\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 K_{G})$
with the score graph of \S\ref{sec:scoregraph}, which couples notes by time
and by pitch proximity. Both structured kernels are $(I+L)^{-1}$,
diagonally normalised, and $\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2$ is the track's own
variance, a population scale that is not tuned and is shared by every arm.
For intonation it is computed from the track's finite reference centres
within $150$ cents of zero, the hidden note's included, and for the
boundary span from the track's timing residuals. Every arm carries a free
per-chain common-mode scalar $t \sim \N(0, 50^2)$, with
$c = t\mathbf{1} + \tilde c$, marginalised as a rank-one term. This scalar
hands the independent prior the chain's observed mean, so that whatever
structured gain survives it is element-level transfer rather than the
transfer of a shared offset.

Each chain is evaluated under two conditions. In the \textbf{masked}
condition the middle eligible element is removed from the likelihood and
from the design, so that its posterior is the prior conditional given the
other notes, averaged over their posterior uncertainty, the waveform-level
analogue of the held-out
imputation task of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. In the \textbf{clean}
condition the full audio is used. The hidden element is removed from the
design and not only from the audio samples, because its window columns
would otherwise carry acoustic evidence from the neighbouring samples into
its prediction.

The comparisons ask whether structure is better than independence on masked
elements, whether the score graph beats the temporal chain where a pitch
recurs, and whether the arms agree on clean audio. A structured-versus-
independent contrast whose interval includes zero under the common-mode
scalar means that no element-level information beyond the shared offset is
detected. The hidden element is scored by its distance to the reference,
the reference centre for $c$ and the annotation for a joint, and by
coverage at $90\%$ from a joint Laplace approximation at the MAP.

\subsection{Intonation on short chains}

The chain-eligible notes of four development tracks give $47$ chains of
$3$--$5$ notes, $25$ of which contain a recurring pitch. The prediction was
that structure is better than independence on the hidden note. Without the
common-mode scalar, structure halves the hidden note's error, from $14.42$
to $6.80$ cents (temporal) and $7.62$ cents (score graph) at the median.
The paired differences are $-5.78$ with interval $[-8.16, -3.27]$ and
$-6.24$ with interval $[-8.49, -3.79]$, each better on $79\%$ of the
chains, while the two structured arms show no measurable difference
($-0.46$, $[-1.48, +0.51]$) and the recurring-pitch stratum shows a trend
short of significance ($-0.86$, $[-2.04, +0.34]$, $n=25$). With the scalar,
the independent arm reaches $6.42$ cents (temporal $6.55$, score graph
$5.90$) and the three arms show no measurable difference
(Table~\ref{tab:phase3-priorvalue}), the recurring-pitch stratum giving
$+0.01$, $[-0.88, +0.91]$.

The scalar moves the independent arm by $-6.85$, with an interval excluding
zero, and the structured arms by $-0.9$ and $-0.6$, neither with an
interval excluding zero. The whole structured gain was therefore the
transfer of the track's shared tuning offset. On $3$--$5$-note chains, neither score
structure nor plain temporal adjacency carries measurable note-level
intonation information beyond the shared tuning offset. On clean audio the
arms land at $2.22$--$2.37$ cents in both configurations, with paired clean
contrasts near zero, so the structure costs nothing where the evidence
dominates. The calibration is as follows. The structured conditional bands
cover at $0.94$ without and $0.89$ with the scalar. The zero-mean
independent band covers at $0.77$, being miscentred on tracks with a tuning
offset, and the scalar resolves this ($0.96$, conservative). The
boundaries are fixed at the annotation in this span.

\subsection{Boundaries}

The second span applies the same design to the per-joint boundary offsets
from the tempo line, the starting points of the chain study's deployment
arm, on the same $47$ chains ($40$ non-unison), with the hidden middle joint
removed from the design by hard cuts. On masked joints the arms show no measurable
difference (Table~\ref{tab:phase3-priorvalue}), with temporal against
independent at $-3.65$, $[-10.90, +3.03]$, and medians of $28$--$31$\,ms,
while every arm is better than the tempo line by $10$--$14$\,ms, each with
an interval excluding zero. A hidden joint inherits its chain's common
offset and nothing beyond it. On clean audio the three arms land on
identical boundaries ($19.6$\,ms median, against the chain study's $19.5$),
so the prior costs deployment nothing.

Two calibration facts accompany this outcome. The masked conditional bands
are far too wide, with coverage $1.00$ at $73$--$95$\,ms, because the prior
scale is the track's full timing variance. The observed joints' bands are
far too narrow, with coverage $0.05$ at $0.7$\,ms: the likelihood pins the
acoustic transition to under a millisecond while the annotation marks the
musical onset some $20$\,ms away, which is the estimand gap of
\S\ref{sec:phase3-study} restated at the boundary. Setting the prior width
at chain scale instead, as the median within-chain timing variance, brings
the independent arm's band to coverage $0.88$ and the structured arms'
bands to $0.75$. It also changes the masked errors, while the paired
differences between priors still include zero.

\subsection{Phrase scale}

The phrase comparison selects recurrences separated by intervening notes.
A search of the $78$ development tracks selected $48$ legato runs of
$6$--$10$ notes from $30$ of them, at most four per track, in which the
hidden middle note has a finite reference centre within $150$ cents of zero
and its pitch recurs at an index distance of at least two and not at
distance one (median three notes, $1.2$\,s, with a
hidden-to-recurrence edge weight of $0.83$). The primary
contrast is supported: the score graph is better than the temporal chain
by $-1.63$ cents ($[-2.93, -0.46]$, better on $65\%$ of phrases). The
proposed mechanism, however, is not supported. The graph shows no
measurable difference from the scalar-augmented independent arm ($-0.07$,
$[-0.42, +0.27]$), the temporal chain is worse than independent ($+1.56$,
$[+0.26, +2.92]$, an interval excluding zero), and there is no
dose-response with the edge weight ($r=-0.03$). At phrase scale, adjacency
pulls the hidden note toward neighbours that resemble it less than the
chain mean does, and the pitch edge pulls it back to about the chain mean.
The graph's advantage over the temporal prior is therefore the harm of that
prior undone. Clean rows show no difference, and coverage is
$0.90$--$0.96$ throughout.

Across the three spans the two hypotheses receive different support.
Against the thesis's own stated baselines, independent or purely temporal,
the score-graph prior is in no comparison worse with an interval
excluding zero, and at phrase scale it is better than the temporal prior,
so the weak form is supported. Against the common-mode
baseline that the first span showed to be mandatory, the score-graph prior
adds nothing measurable, for intonation at either span or for timing at
chain scale, so the strong form is not supported by the evidence so far.
A note or a joint with no acoustic evidence inherits its context's common
offset. The band of a hidden note covers near nominal, and the band of a
hidden joint is too wide.

Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale} compares time and pitch proximity at
whole-track scale on tracked targets. There the score graph is better
than a nearest-neighbour time chain on intonation and vibrato, with
intervals excluding zero, but on intonation it equals the same graph with
its pitch term removed, and in the piano setting the pitch-less graph is
slightly better than the principal model. Where the pitch term was
removed, on tracked strings and winds and on piano, the structure that
helps is smoothness over a short time window, two beats of score time on
piano and two seconds of performance time on strings and winds, and the
score-specific pitch coupling does not show beyond a small gain on
loudness. The waveform spans compare the full graph with a note-order path
and an independent prior and do not remove the pitch term, so they do not
isolate its contribution.

\chapter{Motif Structure in the Score Graph}\label{ch:motif}
On both corpora the score graph's value is temporal smoothing over a short
window, and the pitch term adds nothing measurable on intonation or
on piano
(Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}, Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}). This chapter
asks whether a different kind of score structure, one that the time window
does not represent, carries expressive information: the repetition of a
motif. A
performer who shapes a motif at its first statement is expected to shape
its returns in a related way, and a hidden return should then be
predictable from the statements that are observed. The chapter adds
one edge family to the score graph from motif annotations, states what the
family does to a hidden note, and evaluates it on Beethoven sonata
movements under a masking scheme that hides whole occurrences. The
evaluation uses score
features only, without the music model, since the question is one score
structure against another on identical features.

\section{An edge family from motif occurrences}\label{sec:motif-family}
The BPS-motif annotations \cite{hsiao2023bpsmotif} mark, in all thirty-two
first movements of the Beethoven piano sonatas, the occurrences of each
movement's motifs: an occurrence $O_k$ is a set of notes with a span
$[b_k^{\mathrm{start}}, b_k^{\mathrm{end}}]$ in beats and a type $q(k)$, and
two occurrences of one type are returns of one motif. Returns differ in
length and in note count, so there is in general no note-to-note map
between them. The family therefore works in a coordinate that every
occurrence shares, the relative position of a note inside its occurrence,
\begin{equation}
  u_{i,k} \;=\; \frac{b_i - b_k^{\mathrm{start}}}{\,b_k^{\mathrm{end}} - b_k^{\mathrm{start}}\,}
  \;\in\; [0, 1], \qquad i \in O_k,
  \label{eq:motif-u}
\end{equation}
and joins notes of two different occurrences of the same type by their
closeness in that coordinate,
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(W_{\mathrm{motif}}\bigr)_{ij} \;=\;
  \max_{k \neq l,\; q(k) = q(l)}\;
  \mathbf 1[i \in O_k]\, \mathbf 1[j \in O_l]\;
  \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(u_{i,k} - u_{j,l})^2}{2\,\ell_u^2}\Bigr),
  \qquad \bigl(W_{\mathrm{motif}}\bigr)_{ii} = 0 .
  \label{eq:motif-adj}
\end{equation}
The length scale $\ell_u$ is fixed at a tenth of the span. Every pair of
returns of one type receives a soft block of edges, strongest between notes
at the same relative position, whether or not the returns have equal
length. A note that lies in two occurrences has a position in each, and the
stronger edge is kept. Nothing is aligned and nothing inside the family is
learned. A note that belongs to no occurrence, or whose type occurs only
once in the excerpt, has no motif edge. Figure~\ref{fig:motif-graph} draws
the two families on a score excerpt.

\figph{proposal_motif_graph.png}{0.78}{Schematic of the two edge families
for three occurrences of one motif. Grey edges join consecutive notes in
score order and stand for the time-window neighbours of the graph of
Chapter~\ref{ch:model}, which are denser. Blue arcs join notes at
corresponding positions in different occurrences, the strongest edges of
the motif family \eqref{eq:motif-adj}, which also joins nearby positions
more weakly.\label{fig:motif-graph}}

The family enters the model of Chapter~\ref{ch:model} in two forms. In the
\textbf{shared form} the adjacency \eqref{eq:adjacency} gains the family
with one weight,
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; W_{\mathrm{time}} + w_{\mathrm m}\, W_{\mathrm{motif}},
  \qquad \Lg = D - W,
  \qquad K_G(s) = U \diag\bigl(g(\nu; s)\bigr) U^{\top},
  \label{eq:motif-shared}
\end{equation}
so that one graph kernel, built from the Laplacian of the combined
adjacency, is shared by the three channels through the coregionalization
matrix $B$ of \eqref{eq:gpfirst}, and $\log w_{\mathrm m}$ joins the
hyperparameters fitted by the per-piece evidence. As $w_{\mathrm m}$ tends
to zero the kernel tends to that of the time-only graph. In the
\textbf{per-channel form} the family is its own additive term with one
scale per channel,
\begin{equation}
  K \;=\; B \otimes K_G(s) \;+\; \diag(c_{\mathrm m}) \otimes K_{\mathrm m}
  \;+\; \sum_f \diag(c_f) \otimes X_f X_f^{\top}
  \;+\; \diag(\varsigma^2) \otimes I,
  \qquad K_{\mathrm m} = U_{\mathrm m} \diag\bigl(g(\nu_{\mathrm m}; 1)\bigr) U_{\mathrm m}^{\top},
  \label{eq:motif-pc}
\end{equation}
where $(\nu_{\mathrm m}, U_{\mathrm m})$ is the eigendecomposition of the
Laplacian of $W_{\mathrm{motif}}$ on the motif notes, $K_{\mathrm m}$ is zero
on every other note, and $c_{\mathrm m} \in \R^{3}$ is learned by the
evidence like the feature scales $c_f$. Since $K_{\mathrm m}$ factorizes as
$X_{\mathrm m} X_{\mathrm m}^{\top}$ with
$X_{\mathrm m} = U_{\mathrm m} \diag(g^{1/2})$, the per-channel form is a
feature kernel of \eqref{eq:gpfirst} and needs no change to the model's
inference. The two forms differ in what they let the evidence decide: the
shared form ties the three channels to one motif weight through $B$, and
the per-channel form lets a channel switch the family off on its own.

\section{What a motif edge does to a hidden note}\label{sec:motif-conditional}
For one channel with the additive kernel $g(\nu; s) = 1/(1 + s\nu)$ the
prior is the Gaussian Markov random field with precision $I + s \Lg$, and
the conditional of a hidden note $h$ given all others is explicit,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E\bigl[y_h \mid y_{-h}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{s \sum_{j} W_{hj}\, y_j}{1 + s\, D_{hh}},
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}\bigl[y_h \mid y_{-h}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + s\, D_{hh}},
  \qquad D_{hh} = \sum_j W_{hj} .
  \label{eq:motif-cond}
\end{equation}
Splitting the degree into its two families,
$D_{hh} = D_{hh}^{\mathrm{time}} + w_{\mathrm m} D_{hh}^{\mathrm{motif}}$, writes the
conditional mean as a mixture of two neighbourhood means,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E\bigl[y_h \mid y_{-h}\bigr] \;=\;
  \frac{s\, D_{hh}^{\mathrm{time}}}{1 + s D_{hh}}\;\overline{y}^{\,\mathrm{time}}_h
  \;+\; \frac{s\, w_{\mathrm m}\, D_{hh}^{\mathrm{motif}}}{1 + s D_{hh}}\;\overline{y}^{\,\mathrm{motif}}_h ,
  \label{eq:motif-mix}
\end{equation}
the mean of the temporal neighbours and the mean of the corresponding notes
in the other occurrences, weighted by relative position. The full posterior
of \eqref{eq:posterior} mixes the same two sources through $B$, the feature
kernels and the noise, and the scored band also carries the uncertainty
of the neighbouring values and the observation noise. Two readable
predictions follow. If performers shape
a motif in a related way at each return, the motif mean is closer to the
hidden value than the temporal mean, the evidence raises $w_{\mathrm m}$,
the error falls, and at a fixed scale the conditional variance falls with
the extra degree. If they do not, the motif mean is noise, the evidence
drives $w_{\mathrm m}$ toward zero and the motif arm approaches the
time-only graph.

\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:motif-design}
The data are the ASAP first movements with an annotated counterpart. The
annotation's note list is matched to the ASAP score by crotchet onset and
pitch after a global offset search over the opening notes, and a movement
is used when at least $85\%$ of its excerpt notes match. Twenty-five
annotated movements have an ASAP score. Two of them sit in the piano
held-out test set and are excluded, and two more fall below the matching
threshold, which leaves twenty-one movements, each as the first $400$
matched notes of one aligned performance, the thesis's excerpt convention.
A note belongs to an occurrence when its onset lies in the occurrence's
span and its annotated type is the occurrence's type. The arms are the
time-only Gaussian graph, which the attribution studies single out, and the
same graph with the motif family in one of its two forms, paired on
identical masks and data. Every arm carries the score features with a
per-piece bias column, the coregionalization matrix, the floored noise, the
exact per-piece evidence and the predictive-variance floor of
\eqref{eq:floor}.

Two masking schemes are used. The random scheme is the thesis protocol,
$40\%$ of notes hidden at random. The occurrence scheme hides whole
occurrences: for every motif type with at least two occurrences, each
occurrence is drawn for hiding with probability $0.4$, with at least one
occurrence of the type left out of the draw and at least one drawn, and
every note outside a motif observed. Occurrences can overlap, so a note of
an occurrence left out of the draw can still be hidden through another
one. The occurrence scheme is the application's case, a whole
statement of a motif reconstructed from the rest of the performance, its
other statements included, whether they come before or after it, and it is
where the family can act, since under the random scheme the two-beat time window
already interpolates inside an occurrence. Scores are computed on the hidden
motif notes whose type has another occurrence with an observed note, per
channel, as the Gaussian log score and the root mean squared error, paired
per piece with seeds averaged, with $95\%$ bootstrap intervals over pieces.

\subsection{Predicted occurrences}\label{sec:motif-stage2}
Edges can also be built from the occurrences predicted by a graph-based
motif discovery model \cite{lalang2026motif}, using for each
movement the cross-validation fold that held it out, mapped to the excerpt
by onset and pitch. The masks and the scored notes stay those of the
annotated occurrences, so the edge source is the only change. At the note level the
predicted membership has a precision of $0.12$ to $1.00$ and a recall of
$0.44$ to $0.99$ against the annotation, with a median F1 of $0.78$. On two

\section{What the result means}\label{sec:motif-meaning}
The motif family is the one score structure in this thesis whose measured
value adds to that of the two-beat time window, and the held-out evaluation
places that value where the development studies placed it, in how the
returns of a motif are detached and held. Temporal weights decay with
separation in beats. The motif edges also link corresponding positions in
the returns, and on articulation the performers of eighteen movements
shape their returns alike enough for the added edges to lower the error of a hidden return by about four percent, on
the performance the family was developed on and on up to four others. On
timing the secondary prediction is not supported. In the shared form the
log-score interval covers zero, although the timing error falls slightly,
and in the per-channel form the evidence sets the timing scale near zero,
on the development set and again here. On
velocity the family's value depends on its form. One weight shared with
timing and articulation over-ties the velocities of returns, and the
per-channel form, which lets each channel choose its own scale, gains on
both axes with coverage near nominal. The pre-specified effect is small in absolute terms and on one
channel, and the evaluation is across performers of the same movements and
not across repertoire, since the two other aligned annotated movements are
reserved for the piano test set and the evaluation therefore used further
performances of the development movements.

\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Interpretation}
The posterior decomposition of \S\ref{sec:attribution} shows how the model
divides its work. The feature kernels carry the largest share of the mean on
every piano channel, the graph contributes to recovery and most clearly to
the log score, and the graph and music-model components are close to
uncorrelated on average, so they act as complements. Which of them
dominates is a property of the piece, assigned by the evidence. The graph
improves calibration because coupling correlated notes lets each held-out
note borrow strength from its neighbours, so its predictive variance tracks
how well its context determines it. The music model's contribution
concentrates in velocity, the channel most predictable from learned
conventions such as phrase-level swells and metrical stress. Timing is
largely note-local and articulation comparatively idiosyncratic. The motif
edges of Chapter~\ref{ch:motif} add to the time window on articulation:
returns of a motif inform each other on how notes are detached and held.

\section{Limitations}
The piano and tracked-audio results rest on held-out pieces. The latter
rests
on a single corpus, the 13 held-out pieces of URMP, and two of its findings
run against the model. The secondary timing-calibration hypothesis was not
supported, and timing recovery showed a small adverse difference whose
interval excludes zero. Two follow-up hypotheses
were then evaluated on the same strings-and-winds test set a second time,
with the re-use disclosed (\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}). The revised timing
noise model was supported, and the tonal metric's primary calibration
prediction was not. Evidence from a re-used test set is weaker in kind than
evidence from a test set evaluated once.

The waveform evidence comes from the development set, with one exception.
Its tests of the thesis's methodological hypothesis, three spans
covering short chains, note joints and phrases
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-priorvalue}), support only the weak form of that
hypothesis. The score prior outperforms a purely temporal prior at phrase
scale, and that one finding was supported in a pre-specified evaluation
on the held-out strings-and-winds pieces, their third disclosed use
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}). Beyond the context's common offset the
score prior adds nothing measurable at the spans tested, on the
development set or on the held-out pieces. A further development-set finding qualifies the graph
itself. On both corpora the graph's value is reproduced, and on piano
slightly exceeded, by the same graph with its pitch term removed
(Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}). The structure that does the work is
therefore smoothness over a short time window, two beats of score time on
piano and two seconds of performance time on strings and winds, not the
pitch coupling that makes the graph a score graph. The results supported on the
held-out test sets stand as comparisons of the full model with its
alternatives, but their attribution is narrower than the model's name
suggests.

The motif study is a development result on twenty-one movements of one
composer with hand-made annotations, and its one pre-specified evaluation,
which supports the articulation effect, uses further performances of the
same movements rather than new pieces (\S\ref{sec:motif-heldout}), so it
tests generalisation across performers and not across repertoire. Its
pre-specified effect is on one channel, articulation. The timing effect of
the first development study did not hold on more seeds, and with edges
from a discovery model the shared form keeps about four tenths of the
articulation effect, with an interval that covers zero.

The remaining limitations concern the data and the model's form. The piano
targets depend on the quality of a single corpus's
score$\leftrightarrow$performance alignment (ASAP), and the evaluation sets,
although held out and filtered for contamination, are modest in size, with
30 development pieces and 20 held-out test pieces. Two limitations concern
the model. The Gaussian likelihood's
quadratic tail can amplify a handful of timing outliers on a very steady
piece into the pooled NLL (\S\ref{sec:guard-gp}), and per-piece feature
weighting breaks down under excerpt extrapolation (\S\ref{sec:boundary}).
Generalisation beyond these settings is untested.

\section{Threats to validity}
What remains open is a residual dependence of the velocity target on the
piece mean (\S\ref{sec:contam}), the finite size of each corpus, and
external validity beyond ASAP and URMP.

\chapter{Future Work}\label{ch:future}

\section{Robust observation models and safe completion}
The following refinements were measured on the development set, and none
is part of the reported model. A heavy-tailed
(Student-$t$) timing predictive lowers the NLL of a development example of
the tail of \S\ref{sec:guard-gp} from $105.2$ to $-1.7$, and as an
inference-level noise model it brings timing coverage toward nominal, while
predictive floors do not help, because the outliers already carry an
appropriate scale. For completion, a coverage test in the excerpt's
low-rank feature frame combined with a disagreement test against the
cross-piece head removes every blow-up, the worst evaluation falling from
$480{,}266$ to $0.7$, while keeping the per-piece model's advantage where
adaptation helps.

\section{Strings and winds: open questions}
The strings-and-winds results rest on one corpus with annotated onsets. On
a corpus without them the timing channel would depend on an audio aligner
and its error. Adopting the correlated timing-noise row as the default
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}) would change the published tables and needs its
own evaluation, the tonal metric needs a fresh corpus powered for recovery,
and the within-note drift slopes remain candidates for additional output
channels.

\section{The waveform: the joint loop}
\label{sec:future-phase3}The waveform fits cover short chains. Within those
chains the score prior couples the per-note constants
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-priorvalue}), where it transfers only the context's
common offset, and the curves themselves still carry independent priors.
What remains is the \emph{joint} loop, in which all notes of a performance
are inferred at once under the graph prior, by Laplace or variational
inference over $z$. Three further components belong to that loop: a
transient model at the note joints, which the chain study indicates as the
next step, the estimand bridge between the harmonic model's constant and the
annotation chain's functional, and the amplitude envelope $A_i(t)$ brought
under the prior. The evaluation design changes with it. With no per-note
observations there is no visible/hidden split, and the natural protocol is
held-out pieces with posterior-predictive resynthesis.

\section{Motif structure: the next steps}\label{sec:future-motif}
The motif results concern annotated movements by one composer, with one
performance per movement in development. Three steps follow. The first is a model across performers: the held-out evaluation
treats further performances of a movement as further draws of the same
targets, and a performer-level term, shared across the movements one
performer plays, would turn that into a model of how a performer shapes a
motif. The second is the discovery model as the only source of
occurrences, on scores without annotations and on other composers, since
in the shared form the predicted edges keep about four tenths of the
annotated articulation effect on the Beethoven movements, with an interval
that covers zero, and the question is whether that fraction holds where
no annotation exists to compare against. The third is the form of the
family. The present family is undirected and joins every pair of returns
alike. A directed form, in which earlier statements inform later ones but
not the reverse, matches how a listener hears a return, and the width
$\ell_u$ of the relative-position kernel, fixed in this thesis, could be
learned by the same evidence that learns the weight. The family also
transfers without change to the strings-and-winds bundle, where the
returns of a motif may share intonation and vibrato as well as timing and
articulation.

\section{Incorporating music theory}\label{sec:musictheory}
Music theory entered the model in two places, both measured on the
development set. In the graph, added chord and voice-leading edges help in
the two-stage regime and a replacement circle-of-fifths metric hurts piano
expression while helping intonation (\S\ref{sec:kernels},
\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}). In the feature kernels, fourteen theory columns
show no clear benefit beside the music-model embeddings, with a small
benefit without them at 10\% hidden (\S\ref{sec:devstudies}).
Functional harmonic edges, which need a harmonic analysis, remain untested.

\section{Unknown score support}\label{sec:future-amt}
Making the score support a latent variable would move the model toward
automatic music transcription, with the present model kept as the
conditional inference inside it.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}
This thesis reads a recording as score, expression, timbre and noise, and
recovers the expression layer note by note with a predictive distribution,
under a single multi-output Gaussian process on the score graph. On held-out
piano pieces it recovers hidden expression better than the strongest
two-stage pipeline, which it contains on a restricted slice of its
parameters, its intervals cover close to nominal, and the graph improves the
log score inside the model, while the pooled log score against the
pipeline shows no difference. On held-out strings-and-winds pieces the same
prior improves intonation recovery and the vibrato log scores against
estimator targets with coverage near nominal, the secondary timing
hypothesis is not supported, and a correlated timing-noise model is
supported on a disclosed second use of those pieces.

Development ablations place the graph's value in smoothing over a short
time window, and the returns of a motif add articulation information beyond
it, on the development set and once on further performances of the same
movements. At the waveform, pitch curves and note boundaries are recovered
from local excerpts without a pitch tracker, the bands under-cover the
reference because the two measure different quantities, and the score prior
transfers the shared offset of a passage and beats a purely temporal prior
at phrase scale, with no information beyond the offset detected at the
spans tested. Whole-performance inference from the waveform and transfer of
the motif result to new repertoire remain open.

\chapter{The two-stage comparator}\label{app:lineage}
The two-stage comparator combines a cross-piece plug-in mean with a
per-channel graph GP on the residual. It is the corner of the proposed
model's constraint space in which every constraint is switched on
(\S\ref{sec:special-cases}), and its precision form makes the shared-shape
restriction explicit.

\section{The per-channel form, precision-side}
With $B$ diagonal and the feature kernels switched off, the joint GP
factorises into three scalar graph GPs, each written through its
\emph{precision}, in one of two forms,
\begin{align}
  \text{additive:} \quad & \Qg = \lambda I + \eta\, \Lg,
      && \lambda > 0,\;\; \eta \ge 0;
  \label{eq:additive-prec}\\[2pt]
  \text{graph Mat\'ern / SPDE:} \quad & \Qg = \sigma_g^{-2}\,(\kappa^2 I + \Lg)^{\alpha},
      && \sigma_g > 0,\;\; \kappa > 0,\;\; \alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1},
  \label{eq:matern-prec}
\end{align}
where the additive form matches \eqref{eq:additive} with shape
$s = \eta / \lambda$ and scale $1/\lambda$, carried by $B_{cc}$, and the
Mat\'ern form matches \eqref{eq:matern} in the same way. The term
$\eta\,\Lg$ penalises differences between connected notes and the ridge
$\lambda I$ sets the scale. On a sparse graph the precision is sparse, the
Markov property of a Gaussian Markov random field. The reported Gaussian
adjacency has no neighbour cutoff, and the full model is evaluated
spectrally through \eqref{eq:spectral}. With a plug-in mean $\mu$ and a
diagonal observation precision $P$, equal to $1/\sigma_e^2$ on observed
notes and $0$ on masked notes, the per-channel posterior is
\begin{equation}
  \Sy \;=\; \bigl(\Qg + P\bigr)^{-1}, \qquad
  m \;=\; \Sy \bigl(\Qg\, \mu + P\, \tilde y\bigr)
  \label{eq:posterior-chan}
\end{equation}
to which \eqref{eq:posterior} reduces under the constraints. The plug-in
mean is zero, a ridge regression on the hand-built features, or the
\textbf{music-model mean} $\muLM$, a ridge head fitted once on a
piece-disjoint split and applied to the embeddings,
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{\mathrm{LM},i} = W_{\mathrm{head}}^{\top}\,[\, h_i;\, 1\,],
  \qquad
  y = \muLM + \xi, \qquad \xi \sim \N\!\bigl(0,\, \Qg^{-1}\bigr),
  \label{eq:lm-mean}
\end{equation}
with the graph modelling the residual. The hyperparameters are fitted per
channel by \eqref{eq:marglik} restricted to that channel, with a
per-channel safeguard whose screen tests recovery only.

\chapter{Piano development comparisons}\label{app:phase1dev}
Two comparisons on the piano development pieces support the summary of
\S\ref{sec:devstudies}: calibrated deep baselines and the comparison of
graph kernels.

\section{Calibrated deep baselines}
The comparisons of the main line give the deep model a single shared
predictive scale, which understates it as a rival on the calibration axis.
Two deep baselines that produce their own per-note uncertainty were
therefore evaluated on the development set under the identical strict
protocol and information set: the 25 features together with the music-model
embeddings, standardized per piece, with strict mask-aware embeddings at
evaluation and the same per-piece variance floor. The first is a
heteroscedastic MLP head, which outputs $\mu_c(x)$ and $\log\sigma_c^2(x)$
per channel and is trained by Gaussian negative log-likelihood on the head
pieces. The second is a five-member deep ensemble of such heads. The
proposed model is better than both on both axes by wide paired margins. Its
RMSE is $0.360$ against $0.450$--$0.454$, a paired difference of $-0.090$
with interval $[-0.115, -0.067]$, and its NLL is $-0.404$ against $-0.069$
to $-0.104$, a paired difference of $-0.30$ with interval $[-0.40, -0.21]$.
Ensembling five heads adds almost nothing over one. The comparison uses
read-out heads on fixed embeddings, and a fine-tuned end-to-end deep model
remains untested.

\section{Kernel comparison in the two-stage regime}\label{sec:kernels}
Under the two-stage baseline on the development set, with one scalar GP per
channel and identical masks, one design axis was moved at a time. The
spectral profile is inert: graph Mat\'ern at $\alpha = 2$ and $3$, the
diffusion kernel and the normalised Laplacian show no difference from the
additive Laplacian on RMSE ($0.392$--$0.393$ against $0.393$). The graph is
not inert: removing every edge raises RMSE to $0.446$ and NLL to $-0.190$,
and the temporal chain ties on RMSE but is worse calibrated, $+0.031$ in
NLL. Tonal distance in place of semitone distance is worse on both axes
(RMSE $0.403$, NLL $-0.300$), whereas added chord and voice-leading edges
improve both, by $-0.008$ RMSE and $-0.013$ NLL, robust to scaling their
weights by $\times 3$ and $\div 3$. Under the proposed model the chord edges
are redundant at the 40\% operating point, with a recovery gain only at
30/20/10\% hidden, so the plain graph is kept.

\chapter{Strings-and-winds development studies}\label{app:phase2dev}

\section{The drift study of within-note variation}\label{app:drift}
Every vibrato-identifiable development note that both pitch curves can
measure ($n = 5{,}145$) was refitted with a linear drift of the centre under
the vibrato, independently on the tracked and on the reference curve. About
two thirds of notes carry a significant slope on each curve ($65.5\%$ and
$67.1\%$), and among the notes significant on both, the two curves agree on
the direction $97\%$ of the time. The median drift is $10.5$ cents per note,
an order of magnitude above the median reported standard error of $0.9$
cents, so $c$ estimates the average intonation of the note. Among notes
with a significant loudness slope, $65\%$ fall, mostly decay envelope. The
lag-one correlation of slopes between consecutive notes is $+0.03$ for
intonation and $+0.06$ for loudness, against $+0.59$ for timing
(Figure~\ref{fig:drift-study}).

\section{Three priors at track scale}
\label{app:trackscale}
This comparison asks whether pitch proximity adds to temporal smoothing at
whole-track scale on tracked targets. The development evaluation
already holds out $30\%$ of the notes per track, and both the graph model
and the no-graph ablation carry a per-piece bias column, so every contrast
is a contrast beyond the common offset. Two further priors were added to
that evaluation on the development set, over $77$ evaluated tracks and $153$ track-and-seed combinations, under the
given variances and with paired differences per combination. The first is
a time-only chain, the same GP with the score graph replaced by unit-weight
edges between consecutive notes. The second is a time-only Gaussian graph,
the score graph with its pitch term removed and its two-second time
window kept.

The score graph is better than
the time-only chain on intonation, vibrato extent, vibrato rate and
loudness, with intervals excluding zero (Table~\ref{tab:trackscale}, first
column), and the chain itself is not reliably better than no structure,
since its NLL is worse on loudness and vibrato onset. The score graph shows
no measurable difference from the time-only Gaussian graph on intonation
($-0.02$ cents, with an interval covering zero) and is slightly worse on
the two vibrato channels. The time-only Gaussian graph against the chain
reproduces the margins of the score graph, and against no structure the
two graphs give similar development intonation gains ($-0.88$ against
$-0.90$). The value of the graph at
track scale is therefore temporal smoothing over a window of about two
seconds of performance time. The pitch-proximity coupling that makes it a \emph{score} graph adds
nothing measurable to intonation here, slightly helps loudness and
slightly hurts vibrato. The waveform spans did not remove the pitch term,
so they do not isolate its contribution.

The piano counterpart gives the same answer. The piano model of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} with the pitch term removed from its graph, the
two-beat window kept, which on polyphonic piano links the notes of a chord
at full weight, was paired per piece against the full model on the
development set, over $30$ pieces and $4$ seeds. The pooled RMSE difference
is $-0.008$ with interval $[-0.016, -0.001]$ and the NLL difference is
$-0.019$ with interval $[-0.030, -0.008]$, a small improvement whose
intervals exclude zero, carried by articulation. The total value of the
graph on that set is $+0.017$ RMSE, so the pitch-less graph is worth about
half again as much as the score graph. On both corpora, then, the structure
that does the work is smoothness over a short time window, two beats of
score time on piano, where it includes the vertical coupling of
simultaneous notes, and two seconds of performance time on strings and
winds, and pitch proximity adds little beyond it. These ablations use
development data, and the held-out comparisons remain those of the full
model against its alternatives.

\chapter{Calibration profile of the proposed model}\label{app:extra}
Figure~\ref{fig:gp-reliability} sweeps the nominal coverage from 10\% to
95\% and plots the empirical coverage on the development set and on the
held-out test set. At the 90\% operating point the observed coverages are
92.7\% on development and \covGP{} on the test pieces. At central levels
the model is conservative, with empirical coverage above nominal, so its
intervals err on the wide side. The pooled profiles of the two sets look
