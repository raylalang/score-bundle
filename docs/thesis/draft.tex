\begin{abstract}
\noindent This thesis reads a recording of a musical performance as four
layers. The score is what was written. The expression is how each written
note was played: its micro-timing, articulation and dynamics, and on
continuously pitched instruments also its intonation, vibrato and loudness.
The timbre is what the instrument adds to a note once its expression is
fixed, and the noise is the rest. Given the score and an observation of the
performance, which is aligned piano MIDI in the first setting, tracked
audio in the second and the waveform itself in the third, the expression
layer is recovered note by note together with a calibrated statement of
confidence. The scientific aim is an interpretable, score-aligned
representation of expressive performance that can be inferred from a
recording with explicit uncertainty. The method is Bayesian
score-conditioned inverse synthesis: a score-structured prior is combined
with progressively rawer observation models, ending at the waveform, where
the timbre layer is integrated out exactly. The proposed model is one
multi-output Gaussian process on the score graph. Notes related in the
score are related in performance, in the sense of the graph Gaussian
processes of Borovitskiy et al., the expressive channels are coupled by a
coregionalization matrix, and all side information, hand-built score
features and per-note embeddings from a small symbolic music language model
trained from scratch (the \emph{music model}), enters as linear kernels,
that is, as a marginalized Bayesian linear mean. One exact marginal
likelihood per piece learns every hyperparameter.

In the piano setting every modelling decision was made on a development
set of \nPieces{} held-out ASAP pieces, and the main hypotheses were then
evaluated once on a test set of \nConfPieces{} further pieces untouched by
any modelling decision. The model recovers hidden notes with lower error
than the strongest two-stage pipeline it subsumes, a plug-in mean followed
by a graph Gaussian process on the residual, with RMSE~\rmseGP{} against
\rmseConfOldHead{} and a paired difference of \dRmseConf{} whose interval
excludes zero. The graph's contribution to calibration is supported on the
test set, with a paired NLL difference of \dNllGraphConf{}, and
nominal-90\% intervals cover $\approx\covGP$ of the truth. Six downstream
tasks map where the structure helps, in per-note recovery, confidence and
error spotting, and where it does not, in whole-performance summaries and
in extrapolation from a short excerpt.

The strings-and-winds setting carries the same prior, unchanged, to
intonation, vibrato, loudness, timing and vibrato onset delay on
continuously pitched instruments (URMP). The full estimator pipeline runs
end to end on a six-channel bundle with heteroscedastic observation noise
per note and channel. On the 13-piece held-out test set the pre-specified
hypotheses are supported for intonation recovery, for vibrato calibration
on both channels and for coverage on all six channels, whereas the
secondary hypothesis on timing calibration is not supported. Its
diagnosis, a correlated timing-noise model developed on the development
set alone, is supported in a disclosed second evaluation of the same test
set. A tonal pitch metric evaluated in the same way does not meet its
pre-specified calibration criterion and remains exploratory.

The waveform setting, in which the observation is the audio through a
differentiable synthesizer, is studied on the development set only, and no
hypothesis is tested there. From the raw waveform alone, without a pitch
tracker, the model infers whole within-note pitch curves with a smaller
median error than the tracker's frames, strengthens the six-channel bundle
as a further observation channel, and infers note boundaries jointly from
score-nominal starting points. On short chains with one note removed it
recovers the hidden note or joint from its context under a cross-note
prior, but this gain is entirely the context's common offset. Beyond that
offset, score structure adds nothing measurable at chain or phrase scale,
although at phrase scale it is better than a purely temporal prior. With that one qualification, these three results are negative. The central design constraint of the waveform setting is also
quantified: calibration must bridge the estimand gap between the harmonic
model and the annotation chain. A development finding on both corpora
attributes the graph's value. The same graph with its pitch term removed
reproduces, and on piano slightly exceeds, the main model, so the
structure that does the work is smoothness in score time over about two
beats. The test-set results stand as comparisons of the full model with
its alternatives, and their attribution is narrower than the model's name
suggests.
\end{abstract}
