\begin{abstract}
\noindent This thesis reads a recording of a musical performance as four
layers. The score is what was written. The expression is how each written
note was played: its micro-timing, articulation and dynamics, and on
continuously pitched instruments also its intonation, vibrato and loudness.
The timbre is what the instrument adds to a note once its expression is
fixed, and the noise is the rest. Given the score and an observation of the
performance, which is aligned piano MIDI in the first setting, tracked
audio in the second and the waveform itself in the third, the expression
layer is recovered note by note together with a calibrated statement of
confidence. The scientific aim is an interpretable, score-aligned
representation of expressive performance that can be inferred from a
recording with explicit uncertainty. The method is Bayesian
score-conditioned inverse synthesis: a score-structured prior is combined
with progressively rawer observation models, ending at the waveform, where
the timbre layer is integrated out exactly. The proposed model is one
multi-output Gaussian process on the score graph. Notes related in the
score are related in performance, in the sense of the graph Gaussian
processes of Borovitskiy et al., the expressive channels are coupled by a
coregionalization matrix, and all side information, hand-built score
features and per-note embeddings from a small symbolic music language model
trained from scratch (the \emph{music model}), enters as linear kernels,
that is, as a marginalized Bayesian linear mean. One exact marginal
likelihood per piece learns every hyperparameter.

In the piano setting every modelling decision was made on a development
set of \nPieces{} held-out ASAP pieces, and the main hypotheses were then
evaluated once on a test set of \nConfPieces{} further pieces untouched by
any modelling decision. The model recovers hidden notes with lower error
than the strongest two-stage pipeline it subsumes, a plug-in mean followed
by a graph Gaussian process on the residual, with RMSE~\rmseGP{} against
\rmseConfOldHead{} and a paired difference of \dRmseConf{} whose interval
excludes zero. The graph's contribution to calibration is supported on the
test set, with a paired NLL difference of \dNllGraphConf{}, and
nominal-90\% intervals cover $\approx\covGP$ of the truth. Six downstream
tasks map where the structure helps, in per-note recovery, confidence and
error spotting, and where it does not, in whole-performance summaries and
in extrapolation from a short excerpt.

The strings-and-winds setting carries the same prior, unchanged, to
intonation, vibrato, loudness, timing and vibrato onset delay on
continuously pitched instruments (URMP). The full estimator pipeline runs
end to end on a six-channel bundle with heteroscedastic observation noise
per note and channel. On the 13-piece held-out test set the pre-specified
hypotheses are supported for intonation recovery, for vibrato calibration
on both channels and for coverage on all six channels, whereas the
secondary hypothesis on timing calibration is not supported. Its
diagnosis, a correlated timing-noise model developed on the development
set alone, is supported in a disclosed second evaluation of the same test
set. A tonal pitch metric evaluated in the same way does not meet its
pre-specified calibration criterion and remains exploratory.

The waveform setting, in which the observation is the audio through a
differentiable synthesizer, is studied on the development set, and one of
its findings is then tested once on held-out pieces. From the raw waveform alone, without a pitch
tracker, the model infers whole within-note pitch curves with a smaller
median error than the tracker's frames, strengthens the six-channel bundle
as a further observation channel, and infers note boundaries jointly from
score-nominal starting points. On short chains with one note removed it
recovers the hidden note or joint from its context under a cross-note
prior, but this gain is entirely the context's common offset. Beyond that
offset, score structure adds nothing measurable at chain or phrase scale,
although at phrase scale it is better than a purely temporal prior, and
that advantage is supported in a pre-specified evaluation on the held-out
pieces of the second setting, disclosed as a third use of them. With that
one qualification, these three results are negative. The central design constraint of the waveform setting is also
quantified: calibration must bridge the estimand gap between the harmonic
model and the annotation chain. A development finding on both corpora
attributes the graph's value. The same graph with its pitch term removed
reproduces, and on piano slightly exceeds, the main model, so the
structure that does the work is smoothness in score time over about two
beats. The test-set results stand as comparisons of the full model with
its alternatives, and their attribution is narrower than the model's name
suggests.

That attribution motivates a final study. If the graph's value is
smoothness in time, a structure outside the time window may still carry
expressive information. One further edge family joins the notes of a
motif's returns by their relative position inside the occurrence, built
from motif annotations of the Beethoven sonatas and, in a second study,
from the predictions of a motif-discovery model. With whole occurrences
hidden, the family lowers the articulation error of the hidden notes on
the development set with an interval excluding zero, in a shared form and
in a per-channel form, the evidence switches it off on timing, and
predicted occurrences keep about half of the effect. One pre-specified
evaluation on further performances of the same movements closes the study.
\end{abstract}

\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

\section{Motivation}
A score specifies what is to be played, and a performance realises how it is
played. The difference between the two, in micro-timing, articulation and
dynamics, is where musical expression lives. Recovering that difference from
a recording is of scientific interest in its own right and of practical use
in analysis, in pedagogy, in expressive rendering, and as structured
supervision for audio models. Two commitments shape the whole thesis. First,
the score is treated as known, so the problem is the inverse of expressive
synthesis rather than full transcription. Second, uncertainty is a primary
output: a per-note estimate is only actionable together with a calibrated
error bar, and calibration is therefore evaluated throughout with the same
weight as accuracy.

The vocabulary of the thesis is a decomposition of the recording into four
layers. The score is what was written. The expression is how each written
note was played, a small vector of interpretable variables per note. The
timbre is what the instrument adds to a note once its expression is fixed,
and the noise is the rest. The score is given, the expression is the object
of inference, the timbre is a nuisance that the waveform setting integrates
out exactly rather than estimates, and the noise is modelled per note and
per channel. Every chapter can be placed in this picture. The piano and
strings-and-winds settings observe the expression layer through an
intermediate measurement, aligned MIDI in the one case and a pitch tracker
in the other, whereas the waveform setting observes the recording itself and
must separate all four layers at once.

Two statements of different kinds run through the thesis and are kept
distinct. The scientific objective is the representation itself: an
interpretable, score-aligned description of how a performance was played,
in per-note variables and later in within-note trajectories, that can be
inferred from a recording with explicit uncertainty. The methodological
contribution is the inference framework that recovers it, Bayesian
score-conditioned inverse synthesis, in which a score-structured prior is
combined with an observation model of the performance. The two are
complementary, and neither is asserted beyond what the experiments
validate. The representation is interpretable by construction but not
semantically validated, and the framework is validated stage by stage,
with its full integration at the waveform still open
(\S\ref{sec:future-phase3}).

The title names the object of study. With each note of the known score is
associated a small \emph{bundle} of expressive variables, timing,
articulation and dynamics at first and later intonation, vibrato and
loudness, so that a performance becomes a per-note field over the score
support and transcription becomes posterior inference over that field.
``Bundle'' is used in this everyday sense throughout. The geometric reading,
with per-note fibres and a connection over the score graph, is not developed
here.

\section{Problem statement}
Let a symbolic score fix, for each note, its pitch, its score onset and its
notated duration. A performance assigns to each note a small vector of
continuous expressive variables, here the onset residual $\tau$, the log
articulation ratio $\log r$ and the normalised velocity $v$. Given the score
and an aligned performance, the task is to infer the posterior over these
per-note variables, including for notes that are held out (masked), and to
do so with calibrated uncertainty. This is score-informed performance
transcription, or equivalently probabilistic inverse synthesis. In symbols,
with $S$ the score, $x$ the observation of the performance and $z$ the
latent expressive variables,
\begin{equation*}
  p(z \mid x, S) \;\propto\; p(x \mid z, S)\, p(z \mid S),
\end{equation*}
where $S$ is the score layer and $z$ the expression layer. The timbre and
noise layers live inside the observation model, in the waveform setting as
the harmonic amplitudes $a$, which are marginalised exactly, and the
residual $\varepsilon$. The score-structured prior $p(z \mid S)$ is the
subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:model}. The observation model $p(x \mid z, S)$ is
made progressively rawer over the thesis: per-note values read from aligned
MIDI (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}), tracker-derived per-note estimates with
their variances (Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}), and the waveform itself through
a differentiable harmonic synthesizer (Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}). Inferring
the score itself, that is, full automatic music transcription, is out of
scope (\S\ref{sec:scope} and \S\ref{sec:future-amt}).

The thesis asks three questions, in the order in which the settings answer
them.
\begin{itemize}
  \item RQ1 (representation). What do interpretable per-note variables,
        and later within-note trajectories, capture of expressive
        performance, and what do they measurably discard?
  \item RQ2 (structured inference). Does a score-structured prior improve
        both the recovery of expressive variables and the calibration of
        their uncertainty, compared with independent or purely temporal
        baselines, when observations are missing (piano) or noisy (strings
        and winds)?
  \item RQ3 (direct waveform inference). Can a differentiable harmonic
        observation model recover expressive trajectories directly from
        the waveform, and can its uncertainty separate genuine performance
        ambiguity from the model's own mismatch?
\end{itemize}
RQ2 is the core of the thesis. Its hypotheses were fixed before any test
number was seen, and support was defined as improvement on both axes at
once, accuracy and calibration (\S\ref{sec:metrics}). RQ1 is answered by construction together
with a quantified account of what the representation discards, through the
within-note drift study of Appendix~\ref{app:drift} and the curve-level
extension of Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}. RQ3 is answered on the development
set only (Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}). Joining its observation model to the
RQ2 prior, which would close the inverse-synthesis loop, is the endpoint of
the programme rather than a result of this thesis. Its first span, the
score prior over per-note constants inside the waveform inference
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-priorvalue}), is studied on the development set over
three spans. There the value of the structure is the context's common
offset, with nothing measurable beyond it against that baseline, and an
advantage over a purely temporal prior at phrase scale, the one waveform
finding carried to a held-out evaluation, where it was supported. The
purely temporal baseline of RQ2 is also examined at whole-track scale on
both corpora, again on the development set. A naive note-order chain is
worse than the score graph, but the same graph with its pitch term removed
and its two-beat time window kept matches it
(Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}), so the structure that the test-set results
reward is smoothness in score time rather than pitch proximity.
Chapter~\ref{ch:motif} then asks whether a structure that the time window
cannot see, the repetition of a motif, carries expressive information of
its own, and answers on the development set and in one pre-specified
evaluation on held-out performances.

\section{The proposed model and the two-stage pipeline}\label{sec:twostage}
Two names recur throughout the thesis. \textbf{The proposed model} is the
thesis model: one multi-output Gaussian process over all notes and channels,
in which the score graph, the hand-built score features and the per-note
representation of the music model enter a single kernel, with every
hyperparameter learned from one per-piece marginal likelihood
(\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}). The music model is a small symbolic language model
trained from scratch before everything else. \textbf{The two-stage
pipeline} is the earlier development system that the proposed model
provably contains as a special case (\S\ref{sec:special-cases}). It
consists of a plug-in mean, a cross-piece point estimate of each note's
expression, followed by a separate per-channel graph Gaussian process on
the residual.

\section{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A multi-output graph Gaussian process for expressive performance
        (\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}). Channels are coupled by coregionalization,
        the graph kernel is a spectral function of the score-graph
        Laplacian, and all side information, hand-built score features and
        learned music-model embeddings alike, enters as marginalized
        Bayesian linear means. Every hyperparameter is learned by the exact
        per-piece evidence, and the posterior and its calibration follow in
        closed form. Its hypotheses were fixed in advance and evaluated
        once on test pieces untouched by any modelling decision.
  \item An attribution of the model's value to its parts
        (\S\ref{sec:attribution}). Per-piece Bayesian weighting of
        informative features does the recovering, the graph makes the
        confidence calibrated, with its calibration contribution supported
        independently on the test set, and the music model trained from
        scratch earns its place as a feature provider inside the kernel.
        Each of these statements is isolated by nested ablations, including
        the entire two-stage pipeline as a provable special case. The graph
        term is attributed one step further on the development set of both
        corpora: the same graph without its pitch term reproduces its value
        (Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}), so what the graph contributes is
        smoothness in score time over about two beats.
  \item A map of where the structure helps, obtained from six downstream
        tasks with objective metrics and re-validated under the final
        model. It yields two boundaries (\S\ref{sec:boundary}): structure
        helps note-level judgements but not whole-performance summaries,
        and per-piece adaptation helps interpolation but not extrapolation
        from an excerpt.
  \item An evaluation protocol in which leakage control is itself a result
        (\S\ref{sec:hygiene}). An evaluation leak in the music-model
        read-out was found and corrected, and structural tests together
        with bitwise end-to-end audits now exclude it. The protocol further
        comprises contamination filtering between corpora, robustness
        safeguards that validate themselves, and a development/test split
        that exposed the inflation of numbers reused for selection.
\end{enumerate}
The waveform endpoint, the full inverse-synthesis loop with the score-graph
prior inside the waveform inference, is the intended destination of the
programme and is not presented as a contribution. Its components are
studied on the development set (Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}), and the
integration is built only in its first span, where the score-graph prior
couples the per-note intonation constants inside the waveform inference on
short chains. In that span, with hypotheses stated in advance and after a
flaw in the first design had been found and corrected, the value of the
prior is the transfer of the track's shared tuning offset to a note whose
audio is absent. Beyond that common mode, score structure adds nothing
measurable for intonation at chain or phrase scale or for timing at chain
scale, although at phrase scale it is better than a purely temporal prior,
a finding that was then supported once on held-out tracks
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}). These are three development studies and one
pre-specified evaluation (\S\ref{sec:phase3-priorvalue}). The curve-level coupling, the remaining
channels and whole-performance scale remain open: the within-note curves
still carry independent priors, and the graph meets the full waveform
posterior only post hoc, as an observation channel of the
Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2} bundle. A geometric ``connection-Laplacian''
generalisation of the graph prior likewise remains future work
(Chapter~\ref{ch:future}).

The last study of the thesis (Chapter~\ref{ch:motif}) follows from the
attribution. If the graph's value is temporal smoothing, the open question
is whether any score structure outside the time window carries expressive
information. One further edge family, built from motif annotations and
joining the notes of a motif's returns by their relative position inside
the occurrence, is added to the graph and evaluated under a masking scheme
that hides whole occurrences. On the development set the family lowers the
articulation error of the hidden notes with an interval excluding zero in
both of its forms, the evidence switches it off on timing, and edges built
from the predictions of a motif-discovery model keep about half of the
effect. Its one pre-specified evaluation, on further performances of the
same movements, is reported in \S\ref{sec:motif-heldout}.

\section{Scope and non-goals}\label{sec:scope}
The core result is scoped to piano and to symbolic performance variables
read from MIDI, namely onset timing $\tau$, articulation $\log r$ and
dynamics $v$. The score support is known, that is, the note identities,
the score onsets and the score durations are given. Evaluation is on
held-out notes and pieces and always includes calibration. Four things are
explicitly outside the scope of this first version.
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item discovering unknown score support from audio, which is full
        automatic music transcription and a future extension
        (\S\ref{sec:future-amt}).
  \item performer-controlled intonation or timbre \emph{from piano}, which
        do not exist on a fixed-pitch keyboard.
  \item arbitrary instruments and room acoustics.
  \item any suggestion that the model performs general automatic music
        transcription.
\end{itemize}
Audio input and output and continuously pitched instruments enter only
with the strings-and-winds and waveform settings. The work is staged so
that each stage produces a defensible result before the next difficulty is
taken on, and the stages are ordered toward the waveform endpoint: each
isolates one new difficulty on the way to inference from sound alone.
Table~\ref{tab:phases} records the stages and their status. The thesis
names the stages by their content.\footnote{In the code, the results files
and the names of archived artifacts the four stages are numbered 0--3 in
this order: the music model, piano, strings and winds, the waveform.}

\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{ch:related}

\section{Computational models of expressive performance}
Expressive-performance research models how performers deviate from the
notated score in timing, dynamics and articulation. Historically this was
done with rule-based systems, and more recently with sequence models trained
on aligned corpora \cite{cancino2018,oore2018}. Most such work predicts a
single expressive rendering or studies aggregate statistics. This thesis
differs in framing the problem as per-note Bayesian inference with
calibrated uncertainty. It asks not only how accurately expression can be
predicted but also how \emph{trustworthy} the per-note confidence is.

\section{Score-to-performance alignment}
Aligned corpora pair a symbolic score with a performance through a note- or
beat-level correspondence, from which continuous deviations can be read off.
ASAP provides score$\leftrightarrow$performance alignments for piano with
beat and downbeat annotations \cite{asap}, and it is the source of the
targets used here. The quality of this alignment bounds from above the
quality of the extracted targets. This dependence is treated as an explicit
limitation of the work (Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}).

\section{Structured priors: graph Gaussian processes, GMRFs, and the SPDE approach}
Gaussian Markov random fields place a Gaussian prior whose sparse precision
encodes conditional-independence structure \cite{rueheld2005}. The SPDE
approach links Gaussian fields to GMRFs and yields Mat\'ern-type priors with
interpretable range and smoothness \cite{lindgren2011}. These constructions
extend to graphs, giving Mat\'ern Gaussian processes on graph nodes
\cite{borovitskiy2021}. For vector-valued outputs, both the
intrinsic-coregionalization construction of \cite{borovitskiy2021} and the
Kronecker output coupling of Gaussian processes over graphs
\cite{venkitaraman2018} prescribe an explicit cross-output covariance.
Gaussian processes on \emph{evolving} graphs \cite{blancomulero2021} address
dynamic structure, which a fixed score does not need. The proposed model is
exactly such a multi-output graph Gaussian process, with the graph built
from musical adjacency rather than physical space. Its graph kernel is a
spectral function of the score-graph Laplacian, its channels are coupled by
a coregionalization matrix, its side information is marginalized into the
kernel as Bayesian linear means, and every hyperparameter is learned by the
exact evidence.

\section{Symbolic music language models}
Transformer and RNN models over tokenised MIDI, among them the Music
Transformer, REMI and performance RNNs, learn strong distributions over
symbolic music \cite{musictransformer,remi,oore2018}. This thesis builds a
small decoder-only model from scratch, in the spirit of nanoGPT
\cite{nanogpt}, rather than using a pretrained one. The reason is that the
uncertainty layer must attach to the model's internals. With a model built
here, the calibration of its outputs and the absence of leakage in its
read-out remain inspectable.

\section{Differentiable synthesis}
Differentiable digital signal processing renders audio from interpretable
controls, namely fundamental frequency, harmonic amplitudes and noise,
through a differentiable synthesizer \cite{ddsp}. This is the intended
waveform likelihood of the thesis. It connects the latent performance
variables to a waveform while keeping the amplitudes conditionally
Gaussian, so that they can be marginalised in closed form
(Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}). The DDSP line has since grown hierarchical
note-to-performance-to-audio \emph{generation} (MIDI-DDSP
\cite{wu2022midi}), physically structured interpretable effect modules such
as a differentiable guitar amplifier \cite{yeh2024ddspamp}, and semantically
disentangled timbre control for multitimbral synthesis \cite{dalri2026}.
Those works run the synthesis direction, with learned neural encoders and
decoders. This thesis runs the \emph{inverse analysis} direction. The
synthesizer is a hand-structured probabilistic observation model, a harmonic
basis with Gaussian amplitudes marginalised exactly, rather than a trained
neural decoder, and the recovered object is the posterior over
score-aligned performance variables rather than audio.

A complementary line puts the periodicity itself into a Gaussian-process
\emph{kernel} rather than into a deterministic basis. Spectral mixture
kernels learn a stationary covariance as a Gaussian mixture over
frequencies \cite{wilson2013}. Their non-stationary generalization, the
\emph{generalized spectral mixture} kernel, lets the frequencies, amplitudes
and coherence lengths vary with the input, each modelled as a latent
function \cite{remes2017}. Applied to music audio, per-note change windows
that switch between Gaussian processes with harmonic, exponentiated-cosine
covariances support pitch estimation and gap imputation directly on the
waveform \cite{alvarado2016}. These families are the natural priors for the
\emph{within-note} structure that this thesis measures but does not model,
such as a vibrato whose period and extent drift. Both places where they
could enter, a curve-level estimator and the pitch-curve prior of the
waveform setting, were tried on the development set, and neither improved
on the simpler alternative (\S\ref{ch:future}), so their imprint on this
thesis is conceptual. The design difference is a choice and is worth
stating. Kernel-side constructions marginalize the note's structure away,
whereas this thesis keeps named per-note variables ($c$, $\gamma$,
$f^{\mathrm{vib}}$, \dots) because the variables themselves are the object
of study.

\chapter{Model and Formulation}\label{ch:model}
This chapter derives the proposed model in full for the piano setting. The
additions that the strings-and-winds setting makes to this model are specified
together with its results in Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}, and the mathematics of
the waveform setting is given in Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}. The chapter
proceeds from the score support and the expressive variables defined on it,
through the score graph and the kernel it induces, to the coupling of the
channels, the side information, the observation model and the posterior.
It closes with the hyperparameter fit and the constrained special cases that
recover the two-stage pipeline. Appendix~\ref{app:notation} summarises the
notation.

\section{Setting}
A score is the known discrete support
\begin{equation}
  \score = \{ s_i \}_{i=1}^{N}, \qquad s_i = (p_i,\, b_i,\, d_i),
  \label{eq:support}
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is the pitch (MIDI number), $b_i \ge 0$ the onset in score time
(beats), and $d_i > 0$ the notated duration (beats). A performance attaches
to each score event a vector of continuous expressive variables. In the piano
setting this vector is
\begin{equation}
  y_i \;=\; \bigl[\, \tau_i,\; \log r_i,\; v_i \,\bigr] \in \R^{3},
  \label{eq:variables}
\end{equation}
and its entries are defined relative to the monotone score-time warp
$T(\cdot)$, which maps score beats to performance seconds. The warp is read
off the alignment. The local tempo, in seconds per beat, is estimated by
finite differences of performed time against score time along the aligned
beat grid, and $T$ is the cumulative integral of the local tempo over score
time, so that $T(b)$ is the tempo-implied arrival of beat $b$. Because $T$ is
estimated from the same performance's aligned beat grid and then treated as
known, its estimation error is not propagated. This is a limitation, and the
strings-and-winds setting addresses it with a leave-one-out warp
(Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}). Conditional on $T$, the three channels are
\begin{align}
  \tau_i   &= t_i - T(b_i), \label{eq:tau}\\
  \log r_i &= \log \frac{\delta_i}{\,T(b_i + d_i) - T(b_i)\,}, \label{eq:logr}\\
  v_i      &= \tilde v_i / 127 - \overline{\tilde v/127}. \label{eq:vel}
\end{align}
Here $\tau_i$ is the onset residual in seconds, the performed onset $t_i$
minus the tempo-implied onset $T(b_i)$. The articulation $\log r_i$ is the
log ratio of the performed duration $\delta_i$ to the warp-implied duration,
and the dynamics $v_i$ is the centred, unit-range MIDI velocity
$\tilde v_i$. Conditioning on the warp $T$ is what makes the per-note field
$y$ well defined and jointly Gaussian.

Later settings add channels on the same support. The strings-and-winds
setting adds the intonation $c_i$ in cents, the vibrato parameters and the
fundamental curve $f_0$, and the waveform setting adds the harmonic
amplitudes $a$. Not every variable is available, measurable, or even
meaningful on every instrument, and Table~\ref{tab:variables} records for
each one the instruments on which it is realistic. The piano gives reliable
timing, articulation and dynamics but no performer-controlled intonation,
which is why intonation and vibrato are studied on continuously pitched
instruments. The task is score-informed in the sense that $\score$ is given
and the posterior over $y$ is inferred, in the inverse direction, from
observations generated by the same model that, run forward, synthesises a
performance.

\section{The score graph}\label{sec:scoregraph}
Let $\graph = (V, E)$ be a weighted graph on the $N$ score events with
Gaussian-kernel edge weights
\begin{equation}
  W_{ij} \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl( -\tfrac{(b_i - b_j)^2}{2\,\ell_b^2} - \tfrac{(p_i - p_j)^2}{2\,\ell_p^2} \Bigr)
  \;+\; w_v\, \mathbf{1}[\text{voice}_i = \text{voice}_j],
  \qquad W_{ii} = 0,
  \label{eq:adjacency}
\end{equation}
with length scales $\ell_b$ (beats) and $\ell_p$ (semitones) and an optional
same-voice bonus $w_v \ge 0$. Throughout, these are untuned defaults,
$\ell_b = 2$, $\ell_p = 4$ and $w_v = 0$. The ASAP corpus carries no voice
labels, so the voice bonus is inactive in every reported experiment and is
retained for corpora that annotate voices. The combinatorial graph Laplacian
is
\begin{equation}
  \Lg \;=\; D - W, \qquad D = \diag\Bigl(\textstyle\sum_j W_{ij}\Bigr).
  \label{eq:laplacian}
\end{equation}
Two baselines are defined on the same support. The temporal-only baseline
replaces $W$ by the chain adjacency of score-time order, so that each note is
coupled only to its temporal neighbours, which gives a smoother of AR(1)
type. The independent baseline drops all edges, so that
$\Lg = 0$ and the notes are a priori unrelated.

\subsection{Choice of Laplacian}
Alongside the combinatorial $\Lg = D - W$ we consider the symmetric
normalized Laplacian
\begin{equation}
  \Lg^{\mathrm{sym}} \;=\; I - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2},
  \label{eq:lapsym}
\end{equation}
which rescales each note by its degree. The choice matters in principle
because the score graph is markedly irregular. A note inside a dense chord
has many more neighbours than one in a sparse melodic line, so without
normalisation the prior couples chordal notes far more tightly than melodic
ones. Which Laplacian is used is part of the first of the two design axes
described at the end of this section.

\subsection{Music-theoretic graph constructions}\label{sec:graphvariants}
The base adjacency \eqref{eq:adjacency} treats pitch as a ruler, since
proximity is measured in semitones. Music theory suggests two different, and
logically distinct, interventions. One \emph{replaces} that ruler, the other
\emph{adds} relations on top of it. Both are evaluated in
\S\ref{sec:kernels}, and the distinction between them is what makes that
result interpretable.

The first intervention replaces the semitone ruler by a tonal pitch metric.
Write $\mathrm{pc}(p) = p \bmod 12$ for the pitch class and place it on the
circle of fifths via $\pi(p) = 7\,\mathrm{pc}(p) \bmod 12$. The
circle-of-fifths distance is the shorter way around that ring,
\begin{equation}
  d_5(i,j) \;=\; \min\bigl(\,|\pi(p_i) - \pi(p_j)|,\; 12 - |\pi(p_i) - \pi(p_j)|\,\bigr)
  \;\in\; \{0,\dots,6\},
  \label{eq:fifths}
\end{equation}
so a perfect fifth is $1$ step, a major second $2$, and a semitone $5$. This
is tonal proximity, and it is by design unlike semitone proximity.
Substituting it for the pitch term of \eqref{eq:adjacency}, dropping the
voice bonus and adding an octave-band locality term alongside, gives
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{tonal}}_{ij} \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl( -\tfrac{(b_i - b_j)^2}{2\ell_b^2}
    \;-\; \tfrac{d_5(i,j)^2}{2\ell_5^2}
    \;-\; \tfrac{\bigl((p_i - p_j)/12\bigr)^2}{2\ell_{\mathrm{oct}}^2} \Bigr),
  \qquad W^{\mathrm{tonal}}_{ii} = 0 .
  \label{eq:tonaladj}
\end{equation}
The third term is necessary rather than decorative. Since $d_5$ is blind to
register, notes four octaves apart would be maximally close without an
octave-displacement penalty, whose length scale $\ell_{\mathrm{oct}}$ is
measured in octaves. The score-time term is unchanged.

The second intervention keeps the base graph and adds two families of edges.
A third family, which links notes whose sustained durations overlap, was
evaluated on the development set and is not used. The augmented adjacency is
\begin{equation}
  W^{\mathrm{chord}}_{ij} \;=\; W_{ij}
    \;+\; w_{\mathrm{ch}}\, \mathbf{1}\bigl[\, b_i = b_j \,\bigr]
    \;+\; w_{\mathrm{vl}}\, \mathbf{1}\bigl[\, b_i \neq b_j,\;
        |b_i - b_j| \le \Delta_{\mathrm{vl}},\;
        1 \le |p_i - p_j| \le 2 \,\bigr],
  \label{eq:harmadj}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta_{\mathrm{vl}} = 2$ beats. The first family links notes struck
together. The second links notes a melodic step apart, one or two semitones,
within a short window, which is stepwise voice leading.

The chord edges should be understood for what they are. The indicator
$\mathbf{1}[b_i = b_j]$ is onset simultaneity, used as a proxy for same-chord
membership. No harmonic analysis is performed, no chord is labelled, and no
key is inferred. Onset simultaneity is the strongest harmonic-simultaneity
signal obtainable from the score alone, and it is also the weakest possible
music-theoretic commitment. Cadential and functional edges, which would
encode tonic and dominant relations, do require a harmonic analysis and are
not implemented (\S\ref{sec:musictheory}). Each family can be removed
independently by setting its weight to zero. The edge weights
$w_{\mathrm{ch}}, w_{\mathrm{vl}}$ are untuned defaults, and a sensitivity
analysis over six settings, scaling each family's weight by $\times 3$ and
by $\div 3$ (\S\ref{sec:kernels}), shows that the resulting gain does not
depend on a narrow range of either weight.

The graph augmented by \eqref{eq:harmadj} is called the \textbf{chord graph},
and its two families the chord and voice-leading edges. They are edges of
harmony in the musical sense, recording which notes sound together and which
move by step. The name avoids the word ``harmonic'', which this thesis
reserves for the harmonic amplitudes $a$ of the waveform synthesiser
(Chapter~\ref{app:phase3}), the overtones of a single note's fundamental. The
two senses never interact.

Together with \S\ref{sec:graphgp}, the choices above fix the graph kernel
completely, and the kernel has two independent design axes. The first is the
graph $\Lg$ itself, which edges exist, under which pitch metric, and which
Laplacian, and it is fixed in this section. The second is the spectral
profile $g$, how sharply high graph frequencies are suppressed, and it is
fixed in \S\ref{sec:graphgp}. The kernel comparison of \S\ref{sec:kernels}
varies both and finds that they matter to very different degrees.

\section{The graph kernel}\label{sec:graphgp}
Each expressive channel is a scalar field over the $N$ notes, and the graph
turns it into a Gaussian process on the score graph, a graph GP, that is, a
multivariate Gaussian whose covariance is built from the graph. Let
$\Lg = U \diag(\nu)\, U^\top$ be the Laplacian's eigendecomposition, with
eigenvalues $0 = \nu_1 \le \nu_2 \le \dots$ playing the role of graph
frequencies. A graph kernel is obtained by shaping this spectrum,
\begin{equation}
  K_G(s) \;=\; U \operatorname{diag}\bigl(g(\nu;\, s)\bigr)\, U^\top,
  \label{eq:spectral}
\end{equation}
with a decreasing spectral profile $g$. Components that vary smoothly along
the graph, those with small $\nu$, keep their prior weight, rapidly varying
components are suppressed, and a single shape parameter $s$ sets how
aggressively. The profile is normalised so that $g(0) = 1$, and $K_G$
therefore carries shape only, with all scale living in the coregionalization
matrix of \S\ref{sec:coreg}. This normalisation is forced rather than
conventional. In the product $B \otimes K_G$ the split of scale between the
two factors is unidentifiable, because $(cB) \otimes (K_G/c)$ is the same
covariance, so the scale must live in exactly one place. Three classical
profiles are used:
\begin{align}
  \text{additive / regularized Laplacian:}\quad & g(\nu;\, s) = \frac{1}{1 + s\,\nu},
  \label{eq:additive}\\[2pt]
  \text{graph Mat\'ern:}\quad & g(\nu;\, s) = \Bigl(\frac{s}{\,s + \nu\,}\Bigr)^{\!\alpha},
  \qquad \alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1},
  \label{eq:matern}\\[2pt]
  \text{diffusion / heat:}\quad & g(\nu;\, s) = e^{-s\,\nu}.
  \label{eq:diffusion}
\end{align}
The Mat\'ern profile is the graph Mat\'ern Gaussian process of Borovitskiy
et al.\ \cite{borovitskiy2021}, with covariance eigenvalues
$\sigma_g^{2}(\kappa^2 + \nu_j)^{-\alpha}$ on the Laplacian eigenvalue
$\nu_j$. Here $s = \kappa^2$, and the scale
$g(0) = \sigma_g^2 \kappa^{-2\alpha}$ is carried by $B$. It is the graph
analogue of a Euclidean Mat\'ern GP through the SPDE link of Lindgren et
al.\ \cite{lindgren2011}. The diffusion profile is its $\alpha \to \infty$
limit under the joint reparameterization $s = \alpha/t$, whereas at fixed $s$
the limit degenerates to zero. The additive profile is not a fourth family.
It \emph{is} the graph Mat\'ern at $\alpha = 1$ with the shape parameter
inverted, since $1/(1+s\nu) = s'/(s'+\nu)$ at $s' = 1/s$, and it is also
known as the regularized-Laplacian kernel \cite{smolakondor2003}.

Under any of these profiles, neighbouring notes are encouraged, not forced,
to share expressive behaviour, and distant notes are coupled only weakly.
The constant profile $g \equiv 1$ gives $K_G = I$, the graph switched off
and the notes a priori independent, which is the ablation that isolates the
graph's marginal value. The additive profile is the default throughout, and
the kernel comparison of \S\ref{sec:kernels} shows that the classical
alternatives perform no differently. Throughout, $\alpha$ is only the
Mat\'ern exponent and $\sigma_g$ the Mat\'ern prior scale, while plain
$\sigma$ is reserved for the posterior standard deviation
(\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}).

\section{Coupling the channels}\label{sec:coreg}
Timing, articulation, and velocity are not independent, since a stressed
note tends to be louder, longer, and delayed together. The model therefore
couples the channels with an intrinsic coregionalization structure
\cite{bonilla2008}, one latent field over the $3N$ (note, channel) pairs with
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Cov}\bigl[\, y_{i,c},\; y_{j,c'} \bigr]
  \;=\; B_{cc'}\; \bigl[K_G(s)\bigr]_{ij},
  \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad
  \operatorname{Cov}[\, y \,] \;=\; B \otimes K_G(s),
  \label{eq:icm}
\end{equation}
where $B \in \R^{3 \times 3}$ is a positive semidefinite matrix learned
through its log-Cholesky factor. Because the $g(0) = 1$ normalisation
resolves the scale ambiguity of \S\ref{sec:graphgp}, $B$ carries all of the
scale. The per-note prior variance of channel $c$ is $B_{cc}\,[K_G]_{ii}$,
since the normalisation fixes the shape of the spectrum rather than the
diagonal of $K_G$, and the off-diagonal entries of $B$ let an observed
channel inform an unobserved one at a coupled note. Constraining $B$ to be
diagonal severs exactly this coupling and makes the three channels
independent scalar graph GPs, which is one of the constraint switches of
\S\ref{sec:special-cases}.

\section{Side information as kernels}\label{sec:features}
Two per-note feature sets summarise what is known about each note beyond the
graph. The first is a set of hand-built score features $X_{\mathrm{feat}}$,
$\sim$25 interpretable score-only columns plus a bias, covering the metric
position in the bar, pitch and melodic interval, contour, local rhythm,
chord and harmonic context, note density, and related quantities. The second
is the set of music-model embeddings $X_{\mathrm{emb}}$, the per-note
embeddings $h_i \in \R^{d}$ of the music model. Both sets enter the model in
the same way, and they differ only in what they encode.

The embedding of a note is read out before the model has seen that note's
own target. The embedding $h_i$ is read at the token preceding note $i$'s
velocity token, which is its duration token. A causal model's hidden state
at position $t$ already contains token $t$, so reading at the velocity token
would hand each note its own velocity, which is the target. The general rule
is that a read-out used at observed notes must never contain its own target.
A bidirectional model instead needs a leave-one-out read-out, in which each
note is read at a masked copy of its own velocity position. Under the strict
protocol, the velocities of held-out notes are also removed from the music
model's input, so the embedding of a held-out note depends only on the score
and on the observed notes' performances.

Each feature set enters the GP as a linear kernel with per-channel scales
$c_f \in \R^{3}_{\ge 0}$, contributing $\diag(c_f) \otimes X_f X_f^\top$ to
the covariance. Each feature matrix has its columns z-scored within the
piece, with a bias column appended, before entering the kernel. Because the
single per-channel scale $c_{f,c}$ is isotropic over columns, this
standardisation affects the model and is not a cosmetic step. The
construction is not a heuristic. A GP whose mean is a linear model
$X_f \beta_c$ with unknown per-channel weights
$\beta_c \sim \N(0,\, c_{f,c} I)$ becomes, once each $\beta_c$ is integrated
out, a zero-mean GP with $c_{f,c}\, X_f X_f^\top$ added to channel $c$'s
kernel. A linear kernel \emph{is} the marginalized Bayesian linear mean. The
feature term is channel-diagonal, so the coregionalization $B$ acts on the
graph component only and cross-channel information flows exclusively through
$B \otimes K_G$. The regression weights are thereby re-inferred for every
piece, jointly with everything else, and their uncertainty flows into the
predictive variance.

The marginalized construction differs from the classical plug-in
alternative, which fixes the weights once. That alternative fits a point
estimate $\hat\beta$ across pieces, subtracts the resulting plug-in mean
$\mu = X_f \hat\beta$, and models the residual with the graph GP. It is one
of the constraint switches of \S\ref{sec:special-cases}, and the attribution
experiments locate most of the proposed model's accuracy gain precisely at
this switch (\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

\section{Observation model and posterior}
\label{sec:gpfirst}
Assembling \S\ref{sec:graphgp} to \S\ref{sec:features}, the latent field is
observed through independent Gaussian noise,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde y \;=\; y + e, \qquad e \sim \N\bigl(0,\, \Sigma_e\bigr),
  \qquad \Sigma_e = \diag(\varsigma^2) \otimes I,
  \label{eq:obs}
\end{equation}
with one learned noise variance $\varsigma_c^2$ per channel. The covariance
of the stacked observation vector, which states the whole model in one
equation, is
\begin{equation}
K \;=\;
\underbrace{B \otimes K_G(s)}_{\text{coupled graph GP}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_f \operatorname{diag}(c_f) \otimes X_f X_f^\top}_{\text{marginalized linear means}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\operatorname{diag}(\varsigma^2) \otimes I}_{\text{observation noise}} .
\label{eq:gpfirst}
\end{equation}
Masked (held-out) notes contribute no likelihood term. Write $o$ for the
observed entries. A held-out note hides all three of its channels, which
keeps the observed block Kronecker-compatible, and exact conjugate GP
regression then gives the posterior over the whole field in closed form:
\begin{equation}
  p(y \mid \tilde y_o) = \N\bigl(m,\, \Sy\bigr), \qquad
  m \;=\; C_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, \tilde y_o, \qquad
  \Sy \;=\; C \,-\, C_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, C_{o \cdot},
  \label{eq:posterior}
\end{equation}
where $C$ is the latent covariance, the first two terms of
\eqref{eq:gpfirst}, and $K_{oo}$ the observed block of $K$, noise included.
Masked notes are predicted from the observed ones through the conditional
Gaussian, which is the held-out imputation task. The per-note posterior
standard deviation is $\sigma_i = \sqrt{(\Sy)_{ii}}$.

By construction the latent field is a sum of independent Gaussian
components, the coupled graph GP and one draw per feature kernel, so this
posterior decomposes exactly by prior component. The derivation is given at
its single point of use, the head of the attribution analysis
(\S\ref{sec:attribution}). Figure~\ref{fig:arch-phase1} lays the assembly
out as a computation graph. The graph-structure branch and the evidence
branch are built independently from the score, fuse as a sum of Kronecker
terms into the joint kernel \eqref{eq:gpfirst}, and meet the data only in
the likelihood, which is the one block that changes in the later settings
(Chapter~\ref{ch:future}).

\subsection{Predictive variance for held-out observations}
A held-out observation at entry $i$ is $\tilde y_i = y_i + e_i$, so its
predictive variance must include the observation-noise variance of that
entry. This variance is written $\sigma_e^2$ throughout. In the present
model it is the channel's $\varsigma_c^2$, and in the per-channel form of
Appendix~\ref{app:lineage} it is the per-note $(\Sigma_e)_{ii}$. The
predictive variance is therefore
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}\bigl[\tilde y_i \mid \tilde y_{o}\bigr]
  \;=\; (\Sy)_{ii} + \sigma_e^2 .
  \label{eq:floor}
\end{equation}
The latent variance $(\Sy)_{ii}$ collapses toward zero at well-determined
notes, so scoring held-out data against $(\Sy)_{ii}$ alone is severely
overconfident, and both NLL and coverage deteriorate sharply.
Equation~\eqref{eq:floor} is therefore required for all held-out
evaluation, and it is referred to below as the variance floor.

\section{Hyperparameters}
\label{sec:eb}
Every hyperparameter is learned jointly, per piece, by maximising the exact
log marginal likelihood, the evidence, of the observed block. The
hyperparameters are the graph shape $s$, the coregionalization $B$, the
feature scales $c_f$, and the per-channel noise $\varsigma^2$, and the
objective is
\begin{equation}
  \log p(\tilde y_o \mid \theta)
  \;=\; \log \N\bigl(\tilde y_o;\; 0,\; K_{oo}(\theta)\bigr),
  \qquad \theta = \bigl(s,\, B,\, \{c_f\},\, \varsigma^2\bigr),
  \label{eq:marglik}
\end{equation}
The objective is optimised in log space for $s$, $c_f$ and $\varsigma^2$ and
in log-Cholesky space for $B$, so positivity and positive semidefiniteness
hold by construction. The optimiser is L-BFGS-B on those unconstrained
coordinates, followed by a derivative-free Nelder--Mead
refinement.\footnote{Where the gradient-based optimiser of SciPy is
unavailable, the fit uses Nelder--Mead alone.} A non-finite objective value
is returned as a large finite value rather than raised as an error, so an
unsuccessful Cholesky factorisation cannot disrupt the line search. One
evidence per piece decides every parameter, including how much to trust each
information source, a choice that a plug-in construction must make once,
across pieces, by cross-validation. A calibration-split alternative
objective instead splits the observed notes and picks $\theta$ minimising
the held-out predictive NLL of the calibration subset under the floored
predictive variance \eqref{eq:floor}.

\subsection{Noise floor}
On a minority of mask realisations the unconstrained maximiser of
\eqref{eq:marglik} degenerates, with $\sigma_e^2 \to 0$, and produces an
overconfident posterior. The same principle as \eqref{eq:floor} therefore
applies to the fit itself. Each noise variance is constrained above a small
floor $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{nv}}$ inside the optimisation, for example a
fixed fraction of the observed residual variance. The constraint binds only
where the unconstrained maximiser would fall below the floor.

\subsection{Validation of the fitted hyperparameters}
Rarely, the maximiser of \eqref{eq:marglik} lands on a degenerate
configuration that the noise floor cannot catch. The fitted field then
extrapolates badly, or reports far too tight intervals, on notes it did not
fit. A safeguard therefore screens the fit on data it did not use. A
calibration subset of the observed notes ($\sim$30\%) is held back, the
fitted GP is conditioned on the remainder, and the fit is rejected if, on
the calibration subset, its RMSE exceeds a small multiple ($2\times$) of the
mean-only RMSE or its NLL exceeds the mean-only NLL by more than $0.5$ nats,
the latter being the overconfidence check. On rejection, the fit falls back
through a fixed sequence: first a refit with the noise floor raised
$5\times$, then a conservative decoupled fit with the graph and feature
kernels off and $B$ diagonal at the observed per-channel variance, which
predicts roughly the mean with a conservative scale. On healthy fits the
screen never fires, so runs with and without the safeguard are identical
except at the degenerate piece-seed combinations.\footnote{The behaviour of
this screen on the piano evaluation, including the one failure mode it
cannot detect, is reported in \S\ref{sec:guard-gp}.}

\section{Constraints and special cases}\label{sec:special-cases}
The model \eqref{eq:gpfirst} has a small set of independent
\textbf{constraint switches}, each of which turns one modelling decision
off:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,noitemsep]
  \item decouple the channels, by constraining $B$ to be diagonal
        (\S\ref{sec:coreg}),
  \item drop the side information, by setting the feature scales $c_f = 0$,
        which leaves a zero-mean graph smoother,
  \item plug the mean in, by replacing the marginalized per-piece weights
        with a cross-piece point estimate $\hat\beta$ and modelling the
        residual (\S\ref{sec:features}),
  \item fix the graph, by holding the graph parameters at constants per
        channel instead of learning them inside the joint evidence.
\end{enumerate}
Turning all four switches on simultaneously yields the classical
\textbf{two-stage pipeline}, a point-estimate trend plus per-channel
residual kriging on the graph, which is the standard construction in the
spatial-statistics and graph-GP literature. This corner of the constraint
space is retained for a methodological reason. It is the null model against
which every hypothesis of the form ``relaxing constraint X helps'' is
tested, so that a gain over it is a statement about the standard
construction rather than about an earlier version of this work. In one
regime, rendering from a short excerpt, where per-piece adaptation has too
little to adapt to, it also remains the better tool (\S\ref{sec:boundary}).
Its per-channel precision-side form, with $\Qg = \lambda I + \eta \Lg$ per
channel as the sparse-GMRF computational route, and its plug-in-mean choices
are recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:lineage}.\footnote{The constrained
systems' posterior formulas and the development lineage tables are kept in
the repository's design record, \texttt{docs/graphgp\_first\_design.md}.}

\subsection{Exact nesting of the two-stage pipeline}
Constraining $B$ to be diagonal, dropping the feature kernels, and fixing
the graph recovers the two-stage pipeline's per-channel model
(Appendix~\ref{app:lineage}) exactly on the shared-shape slice. The joint
marginal likelihood \eqref{eq:marglik} equals the sum of the per-channel
ones under $(\lambda_c, \eta_c) = (1/B_{cc},\, s/B_{cc})$, and the equality
is verified numerically. The per-channel pipeline, fit independently, may
select three different shapes, so the nesting statement is about the model
family and not about coinciding optima. The constrained systems are
therefore not merely analogous baselines but points in the proposed model's
own parameter space, which is what makes the development lineage an
ablation of one model rather than a comparison of two. Re-inserting the
two-stage pipeline's own cross-piece mean as a fixed mean inside
\eqref{eq:gpfirst} reproduces its results almost unchanged. This is how the
proposed model's gain is attributed to the per-piece Bayesian feature
weighting rather than to coupling or to joint fitting as such
(\S\ref{sec:attribution}).

The proposed model and its constrained special cases are evaluated under
one protocol. Predictions on held-out notes are scored on both axes,
recovery and calibration, by the metrics of \S\ref{sec:metrics}, with every
system contrast paired at the level of the piece. The protocol itself,
including the division of the corpus into a development set and a held-out
test set, is described in \S\ref{sec:hygiene}.

\chapter{Data and Experimental Protocol}\label{ch:data}

\section{Pretraining corpus (MAESTRO)}
The music model is pretrained on the MAESTRO corpus alone \cite{maestro}.
MAESTRO consists of competition piano performances captured on Disklavier
instruments as finely aligned audio and MIDI, of which only the MIDI is used
here. The music model is a decoder-only Transformer trained from scratch over
note-structured MIDI tokens by next-token prediction. The reference model used
throughout this thesis reaches a validation perplexity of $10.85$.

\section{Evaluation corpus (ASAP) and target extraction}
ASAP \cite{asap} is the only corpus that pairs written scores with
performances, and it supplies the piano targets $y = [\tau, \log r, v]$
through the score-to-performance beat warp $T(\cdot)$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:variables}).
High-quality piano MIDI yields these targets almost exactly, so the
observation noise in the piano setting is negligible ($\Sigma_e \approx 0$).
The inference task is therefore not denoising but \emph{structured
prediction}: the score graph prior is exercised by imputing masked notes from
the rest of the performance.

\section{Strings-and-winds corpus (URMP)}\label{sec:urmp}
The strings-and-winds setting uses \textbf{URMP}, a corpus of 44
multi-instrument chamber pieces assembled from separately recorded monophonic
tracks. Each track comes with its audio, frame-level ground-truth pitch at a
10\,ms hop, note-level onset, pitch and duration annotations, and the score,
and the 13 instruments span strings, woodwind and brass. Three preparatory
measurements govern how the corpus is used, and they are stated here because
each one changes how a later result must be read.

The first measurement calibrates the pitch tracker against the provided
ground-truth pitch before any model is fit. The tracker's median error is
2--5 cents per instrument, an order of magnitude below typical vibrato
extents, and its voicing recall is at least $0.95$. Its per-frame confidence
predicts its own errors: gross octave errors, called slips below, fall from
$9.6\%$ to $0.4\%$ across confidence quintiles. These measurements fix the
estimator chain of Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}. The estimator's own variances are
used as the observation-noise row, and the lowest confidence quintile is
discarded.

The second measurement concerns the split between development and held-out
pieces, which is fixed at the level of the \emph{composition} and constructed
deterministically without reference to the data. This granularity is forced
rather than merely prudent, because arrangements of one composition share
identical track recordings, so any finer split would place the same audio on
both sides. The composition of the held-out portion and the rules under which
it was evaluated are given in \S\ref{sec:hygiene}.

The third measurement is an absence: no clean intonation ground truth exists,
and this changes what a result means. The targets are estimator outputs, so
held-out recovery measures the model's ability to recover \emph{the
estimator's} targets at masked notes, not its agreement with a gold standard.
The provided pitch curves supply an independent cross-check. The same
estimator run on the ground-truth pitch curves gives targets that are
independent of the tracker, though not of the estimator, and these are called
the reference targets, or the reference centre, in Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}.
The result available in the strings-and-winds setting is therefore weaker in
kind than the piano result, and it is not reported as though it were the
same. Figure~\ref{fig:vibrato-compare} shows the three layers, truth,
estimate and prediction, on two notes.

\section{Contamination control and the strict protocol}\label{sec:contam}
ASAP reuses many MAESTRO recordings, so every ASAP performance whose MAESTRO
twin was in pretraining is removed ($1036 \to 653$ aligned performances), and
the model is tested only on performances the music model has not seen. A
\emph{strict} protocol additionally blanks every hidden note's velocity from
the music model's input, so that no hidden target reaches the model through
the token stream. The cost of the strict protocol is negligible on error,
$+0.0003$ for the music-model system and $+0.0000$ for the feature and
music-model stack, and small on calibration, approximately $+0.014$ in NLL.
The strict numbers are the ones quoted throughout the thesis.

\section{Development and held-out evaluation}\label{sec:hygiene}
Two disjoint sets of pieces play two different roles in every setting of this
thesis. The development set is where every modelling choice was made: the
noise floor, the correction of the music-model read-out, the feature stack,
the kernel and edge families, and the proposed model itself were all selected
on the \nPieces{} development pieces of the piano setting and on the
development side of the composition-level split in the strings-and-winds
setting. Each paired comparison on a development set is valid on its own
terms, but a set that is reused for selection favours whatever survived the
selection, so every number obtained on a development set is labelled as a
development number wherever it appears. In the usual machine-learning
vocabulary the two sets are the validation and test splits \cite{nosek2018}.
They are called the development set and the held-out test set here to make
explicit that the second was consulted only after the modelling choices had
been settled.

Each held-out test set was evaluated once, against hypotheses fixed in
advance. Before the test pieces were examined, the systems to be compared,
the primary and secondary measures, and the decision rule stating which
measure decides each hypothesis and in which direction were written down and
committed to the project's version history. The evaluation was then run a
single time, and every result of that run is reported, including the results
that did not support the hypotheses. The piano held-out test set comprises
\nConfPieces{} further contamination-filtered pieces from the same corpus
shuffle, verified untouched by any modelling decision and evaluated with mask
seeds disjoint from the development seeds. The strings-and-winds held-out
test set comprises 13 of the 44 URMP pieces, chosen at the composition level
(\S\ref{sec:urmp}) and evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}. The inflation
that this procedure protects against is visible in the results themselves:
every system's calibration is worse on the held-out pieces than on the
development set, and one development-set advantage, the pooled NLL, did not
reappear on the piano test set.

The strings-and-winds test set was used a second time, and that second use is
disclosed wherever its results appear. The first evaluation tested the main
hypotheses of the strings-and-winds setting. Two follow-up hypotheses, a
correlated noise model for the timing channel and a tonal pitch metric, were
then developed on the development set alone, with their measures and decision
rules again fixed in advance, and were evaluated once more on the same test
pieces (\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}). The consequence for interpretation is that
the second evaluation is a weaker check than the first. The follow-up
hypotheses were formed without access to the test pieces, so the second
evaluation can show that a pre-specified effect appears on pieces that played
no part in its development, but the pieces were no longer untouched, since
their first-round results were known when the follow-up hypotheses were
formed. The two follow-up results are read with that qualification. The
same pieces are used a third time in Chapter~\ref{ch:phase3}, for the one
hypothesis of the waveform setting that was carried to a held-out
evaluation, again with its measure and decision rule fixed in advance and
again disclosed where the result appears. No waveform study had read any of
those pieces before, so the contrast itself is new to them, but the pieces
had been consulted twice at the tracked-target level, and the third result
is read as the weakest of the three checks.

The motif study of Chapter~\ref{ch:motif} uses a different kind of held-out
material. Its hypotheses were developed on twenty-one Beethoven movements,
one performance each, and the held-out evaluation uses further performances
of the same movements that no run had read, at most four per movement, with
the movements as the unit of analysis. The scores, the motif annotations and
hence the edges and the masks are those of the development study, and only
the performances, which supply the targets, are new. That evaluation
therefore tests whether the development finding holds across performers of
the same pieces, not across pieces, and it is labelled as such wherever it
appears.

Every comparison between two systems in every results table follows the same
conventions. The contrast is paired at the level of the piece: the two
systems see identical pieces, identical masks and identical seeds, so their
difference is computed per piece and only then aggregated. The interval
reported with each paired difference comes from a percentile bootstrap over
pieces, in which the per-piece differences are resampled with replacement,
$B = 2000$ times, and the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of the resampled
mean form the interval. A difference is called significant when that interval
excludes zero, and significance is always stated in those words rather than
by a mark in a table. Pairing is essential here, because piece difficulty
varies far more than the gap between systems, so an unpaired comparison would
obscure every effect reported in this thesis.

\section{Sensitivity to the masking level}\label{sec:masksweep}
The operating point used in the results hides \maskRate{} of the notes, a
level inherited from earlier work rather than derived from the data. A
development-set sweep carried out after the held-out evaluation, from 50\%
hidden down to the leave-one-out limit (Appendix~\ref{app:masksweep}), finds
the ordering of the models stable at every level. The score graph's paired
contribution has an interval that excludes zero from 50\% down to 20\% and
again at leave-one-out, and coverage is insensitive to the level throughout.
The operating point is therefore arbitrary, but the conclusions drawn at it
do not depend on it.

\section{Metrics for recovery and calibration}\label{sec:metrics}
Predictions on held-out notes are scored on both recovery and calibration.
With held-out values $y_i$, predictive means $m_i$, and floored predictive
standard deviations $\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}} = \sqrt{(\Sy)_{ii} + \sigma_e^2}$
over $n$ held-out notes, the four measures are
\begin{align}
  \text{RMSE} &= \Bigl( \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i (y_i - m_i)^2 \Bigr)^{1/2},
  &
  \text{NLL} &= \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i \Bigl[ \tfrac12 \log\bigl(2\pi (\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}})^2\bigr)
        + \tfrac{(y_i - m_i)^2}{2 (\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}})^2} \Bigr],
  \\[3pt]
  \text{cov}@\gamma &= \tfrac1n \textstyle\sum_i \mathbf{1}\bigl[\, |y_i - m_i| \le z_{\gamma}\, \sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}} \bigr],
  &
  \text{cal-err} &= \sup_u \bigl|\, \hat F_{\mathrm{PIT}}(u) - u \,\bigr|,
\end{align}
where $z_\gamma$ is the two-sided standard-normal quantile for the level
$\gamma$, which is 0.9 throughout, and $\hat F_{\mathrm{PIT}}$ is the
empirical CDF of the probability integral transform
$u_i = \Phi_{\N}\bigl((y_i - m_i)/\sigma_i^{\mathrm{pred}}\bigr)$, which is
Uniform$(0,1)$ if and only if the predictive distribution is calibrated. Here
$\Phi_{\N}$ is the standard normal CDF, distinct from the waveform synthesis
operator $\Phi(z)$. The pre-specified criterion for a structured prior
requires improvement on \emph{both} recovery (RMSE) and calibration (NLL,
coverage and cal-err) against the independent, temporal-only, ridge and
music-model-mean-only baselines, on held-out, contamination-filtered data. A
method that lowers error but worsens calibration, or the reverse, does not
meet the criterion. Every pooled table additionally reports the median beside
the mean, so that a single collapsed fit cannot hide in the average, and the
safeguard of \S\ref{sec:eb} addresses the cause of such collapses.

\section{Attribution and posterior decomposition}\label{sec:attribution}

\subsection{Refitting controls}
Two development-set controls, each a refit of the model, identify the
mechanism behind the differences in Table~\ref{tab:headline-conf}. The first
is a fixed-mean control. Inserting the two-stage pipeline's cross-piece mean
as a fixed mean inside the coupled GP scores 0.390 / $-0.327$, statistically
at the level of the matching two-stage configuration with features, music
model and plain graph, while the two-stage pipeline with chord edges remains
ahead of this control by a margin whose interval excludes zero.
Coregionalization and joint fitting in themselves therefore contribute almost
nothing. The second is component removal, paired on the development set.
Removing the graph from the full model costs $+0.017$ RMSE and $+0.067$ NLL,
and removing the music-model embeddings costs $+0.008$ and $+0.034$, and both
intervals exclude zero. The mechanism is therefore that per-piece Bayesian
weighting of informative per-note features under one joint marginal
likelihood does the recovering, while the score graph makes the uncertainty
well calibrated. The music model earns its place as a feature provider inside
the kernel, not as an oracle mean.

\subsection{Exact decomposition by prior component}
The latent field is, by construction \eqref{eq:gpfirst}, a sum of
independent Gaussian components, the coupled graph GP and one draw per
feature kernel, and the observation adds independent noise:
\begin{equation}
  y \;=\; \sum_{\zeta} y^{(\zeta)}, \qquad
  y^{(\zeta)} \sim \N\bigl(0,\, C^{(\zeta)}\bigr) \text{ independent}, \qquad
  \tilde y \;=\; y + e,
  \label{eq:components}
\end{equation}
with $C^{(G)} = B \otimes K_G(s)$, $C^{(f)} = \operatorname{diag}(c_f)
\otimes X_f X_f^{\top}$, and $C = \sum_\zeta C^{(\zeta)}$. Here $\zeta$ indexes the
prior components, $\zeta \in \{G\} \cup \{f\}$, which in the piano setting
is $\zeta \in \{G, \mathrm{feat}, \mathrm{emb}\}$, and the primed $\zeta'$
below ranges over the same set. The components do \emph{not} correspond to
the three summands of \eqref{eq:gpfirst}: the middle summand there
contributes one component per feature set, and the noise summand contributes
none, for the reason given at the end of this subsection. Because the other
components and the noise are independent of $y^{(\zeta)}$, its
cross-covariance with the observations is just its own block,
$\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)},\, \tilde y_o\bigr) = C^{(\zeta)}_{\cdot o}$,
and conditioning the jointly Gaussian pair $\bigl(y^{(\zeta)}, \tilde y_o\bigr)$
gives each component its exact posterior:
\begin{align}
  m^{(\zeta)} \;&:=\; \mathbb{E}\bigl[\, y^{(\zeta)} \mid \tilde y_o \,\bigr]
  \;=\; C^{(\zeta)}_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, \tilde y_o,
  \label{eq:decomp}\\[2pt]
  \operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)},\, y^{(\zeta')} \mid \tilde y_o\bigr)
  \;&=\; \delta_{\zeta\zeta'}\, C^{(\zeta)}
  \;-\; C^{(\zeta)}_{\cdot o}\, K_{oo}^{-1}\, C^{(\zeta')}_{o \cdot}.
  \label{eq:decomp-cov}
\end{align}
Two exactness identities follow by linearity, and both are verified by unit
tests: summing \eqref{eq:decomp} over components recovers the posterior mean,
and summing \eqref{eq:decomp-cov} over all pairs recovers the posterior
covariance,
\begin{equation}
  m \;=\; \sum_\zeta m^{(\zeta)}, \qquad
  \Sy \;=\; \sum_{\zeta}\sum_{\zeta'}
  \operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)},\, y^{(\zeta')} \mid \tilde y_o\bigr).
  \label{eq:decomp-total}
\end{equation}
This is the functional-ANOVA decomposition of an additive GP. The prior
components are independent. A posteriori, the cross-terms of
\eqref{eq:decomp-cov} are negative wherever two components can explain the
same variation, which is the explaining-away effect, so their normalised
diagonals measure per-note redundancy between information sources. The
diagnostics of the next subsection are built directly on
\eqref{eq:decomp}--\eqref{eq:decomp-total}.

Observation noise is not a component of \eqref{eq:decomp}, by independence
rather than by convention. A held-out note's noise is never observed and is
independent of every observation, so its conditional mean is identically
zero. Noise enters only through the shared weights: $K_{oo}$ contains
$\Sigma_e$, so the fitted noise level sets how much of the observed variation
is explained at all, a uniform shrinkage of every component rather than a
competing direction. At \emph{observed} notes the decomposition does gain an
exact noise term, $\mathbb{E}[\,e_o \mid \tilde y_o\,] = \tilde y_o -
\mathbb{E}[\,y_o \mid \tilde y_o\,]$, which is the residual the model
declines to explain. In the predictive variance of a held-out observation the
noise reappears additively as the floor \eqref{eq:floor}, where it is
directly comparable to the components' posterior variances. Finally, the
split is the evidence-fitted model's own attribution, since it depends on the
learned $B$, $c_f$ and $\varsigma^2$. It describes how the fitted model
divides labour and makes no causal statement.

\subsection{Covariance shares, component correlations and per-piece dominance}
A third control needs no refitting at all: the exact component decomposition
of the posterior, \eqref{eq:decomp}--\eqref{eq:decomp-total}, is read off
the fitted model itself on the development pieces. The covariance shares
reported below are computed over $\nPieces \times 4$ anchor masks, and the
component correlations and the per-piece dominance and disagreement figures
over the first anchor mask only. Two diagnostics are derived from the
decomposition, per channel. The first, the covariance share, allocates the
posterior mean's variation over the held-out notes $\mathcal{H}$ of a piece
to the components,
\begin{equation}
  \omega^{(\zeta)} \;=\;
  \frac{\operatorname{Cov}_{i \in \mathcal{H}}\bigl(m^{(\zeta)}_i,\, m_i\bigr)}
       {\operatorname{Var}_{i \in \mathcal{H}}\bigl(m_i\bigr)},
  \qquad \sum_\zeta \omega^{(\zeta)} = 1
  \label{eq:share}
\end{equation}
and the normalisation is exact by bilinearity applied to
$m = \sum_\zeta m^{(\zeta)}$, so cross-component interactions land inside the
shares rather than in an unexplained remainder. The second, the per-note
component correlation, normalises the diagonal of the posterior
cross-covariance \eqref{eq:decomp-cov} by the two component standard
deviations,
\begin{equation}
  \rho^{(\zeta\zeta')}_i \;=\;
  \frac{\operatorname{Cov}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)}_i,\, y^{(\zeta')}_i \mid \tilde y_o\bigr)}
       {\sigma^{(\zeta)}_i\, \sigma^{(\zeta')}_i},
  \qquad
  \sigma^{(\zeta)}_i = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}\bigl(y^{(\zeta)}_i \mid \tilde y_o\bigr)},
  \label{eq:compcorr}
\end{equation}
which is negative where the two information sources explain the same
variation and is reported below as its average over held-out notes.

The measured shares put most of the mean on the score-feature kernel on every
channel ($0.69/0.60/0.40$ for $\tau$ / $\log r$ / $v$), give the embeddings
their largest share on velocity ($0.34$, the channel where they carry signal
the hand-built features lack), and leave the graph's share of the mean modest
($0.12/0.22/0.27$). This is consistent with the two refitting controls:
feature weighting recovers the mean, and the graph's contribution
concentrates in the covariance. The component correlations
\eqref{eq:compcorr} complete the picture. Graph by embeddings is near zero
($-0.004/-0.025/-0.059$ per channel), so the two information sources act as
complements rather than redundantly, while the visible explaining-away sits
between the two feature kernels (score by embeddings, $-0.18$ on velocity)
and between graph and score features ($-0.19/-0.18$ on articulation and
velocity). Both statements are the fitted model's own attribution under its
learned scales, not causal statements. Figure~\ref{fig:posterior-components}
shows one development piece's posterior mean split into its component curves.

\figph{posterior_components_dev.png}{1.0}{One section of a development piece
(notes 10--65 in score order, 40\% hidden, anchor mask): the posterior mean

The coupled graph GP, the score-feature kernel and the music-model-embedding

The shares above are averages, and the sharper statements are conditional,
all of them on the development set. Dominance is a property of the piece. The
per-piece evidence turns each kernel on or off per channel according to the
within-piece signal it finds: the embedding kernel, for instance, is switched
off entirely (share below $1\%$) on timing and articulation for roughly 60\%
of development pieces yet dominant on a minority, and at the extremes the
division of labour reverses completely. One piece's velocity channel rests on
the graph, with share $0.76$, its level and a late crescendo propagated from
observed neighbours and the embeddings switched off. Another's rests on the
embeddings, with share $0.69$, carrying a note-to-note dynamics convention
that the score features do not encode. Figure~\ref{fig:component-contrast}
shows the two side by side on the same channel. On timing the pattern is
diagnostic in a different way: the graph-dominated timing pieces are the
\emph{steady} ones, whose posterior mean is largely shrunk toward zero, so
graph dominance there means the graph carries what little of $\tau$ is
predictable at all.

Disagreement between components is likewise localised to particular notes.
Opposite-sign pulls with both components substantial, each exceeding a
quarter of the channel's posterior-mean spread, occur at $20\%$ of held-out
notes between graph and score features on articulation and at $15\%$ on
velocity, which is the per-note face of the $-0.19/-0.18$ component
correlations \eqref{eq:compcorr} above, and between graph and embeddings on
timing at only $6\%$. Such notes are exactly where the per-piece evidence had
to decide between overlapping explanations, and they are where the
decomposition is most informative to inspect.

\section{Leakage control}\label{sec:leak}
An earlier read-out let the music model see a note's own velocity, the very
target it was asked to predict, which inflated its apparent advantage. The
summary is now read one position earlier, so that it is blind to the note's
own velocity by construction, and an automated structural test makes the leak
impossible to reintroduce unnoticed. Everything was re-measured after the
correction, and the strict protocol of \S\ref{sec:contam} was adopted. The
corrected velocity advantage is smaller but remains, with an interval that
excludes zero: the music model's mean-only error on the velocity channel was
$0.057$ with the leak, the retracted figure, and $0.090$ with the final
read-out. The leak had also manufactured an apparent scaling gain. A
$1.3\times$ larger, lower-perplexity music model is identical downstream once
the leak is removed (RMSE $0.394$ against $0.394$), so the music model is
judged downstream, not by perplexity. The same mask-aware read-out feeds the
proposed model's feature kernel, whose full pipeline was additionally audited
bitwise: corrupting held-out targets or velocities changes no prediction by
one bit. One dependence sits upstream of that audit's scope, in the
definition of the target. The velocity centring constant is the piece mean
over all matched notes, held-out ones included, an $O(1/N)$ coupling of
observed targets to hidden velocities that is invisible to the bitwise audit,
identical for every system compared, and recorded as a limitation.

\section{Hyperparameter fragility and the safeguard}
Two distinct failure modes were found and resolved. First, on a minority of
mask realisations the fitted noise level collapsed toward zero and produced
overconfident intervals, and a noise-level floor inside the fit removes this
mode. Second, a rarer knife-edge collapse of the fitted coupling itself
occurred in one (piece, seed) fit in 120, always on the same piece, which
produced timing errors of $4.5$ and $17$ in two independent runs and
single-handedly dominated the pooled average (pooled timing RMSE
$0.16 \to 1.58$, for example). The self-validating safeguard removes both
catastrophic fits ($4.5 \to 0.24$, and $17 \to$ a healthy value) while being
bit-for-bit identical to the unsafeguarded fit on the published protocol,
since it never fires on a healthy fit. Pooled tables additionally report the
median and the worst per-fit error, so that any future collapse is visible by
construction.

\subsection{The proposed model's tail}
\label{sec:guard-gp}
The proposed model has its own rare tail, observed once on the held-out test
set: one evaluation (one piece, one seed, the timing channel) with predictive
NLL $+27.5$ while its RMSE ($0.11$) and coverage ($0.90$) are unremarkable.
The diagnosis is as follows. A very steady piece gives timing a tiny scale,
the per-piece evidence fits correspondingly tight intervals, a handful of
held-out outlier notes then land many standard deviations out, and the
Gaussian likelihood's quadratic tail amplifies them into the pooled average.
This is why the pooled-NLL comparison on the test set shows no difference
while the \emph{median} piece is better calibrated under the proposed model.
A safeguarded fit in the same spirit as the two-stage safeguard was built and
validated, with a calibration-split screen that adds the overconfidence check
the per-channel form lacks (Appendix~\ref{app:lineage}) and a fixed fallback
sequence. It leaves healthy fits bit-for-bit unchanged, and it cannot catch
this tail, because the failure is invisible from observed notes. The
limitation is reported rather than patched post hoc. The principled remedy is
a heavy-tailed (Student-$t$) timing likelihood, which has since been shown to
remove exactly this tail at deployment and at inference level, while
predictive floors prove ineffective (\S\ref{ch:future}), and the per-piece
median NLL reported throughout is robust to it by construction.

\section{Development studies}\label{sec:devstudies}
Four development studies scope the result, and each is recorded in full in
Appendix~\ref{app:phase1dev}. The first compares the music model with
hand-built features. Against the 25-feature score baseline the music model
shows no difference on average error, its advantage lies in calibration and
in the velocity channel, where the interval excludes zero, and the two
information sources stack. The second compares the proposed model with
calibrated deep baselines. A heteroscedastic MLP head and a five-member deep
ensemble, run under the identical protocol and information set, are worse
than the proposed model on both axes by wide paired margins (paired
differences of $-0.090$ in RMSE and $-0.30$ in NLL, both intervals excluding
zero), so ranking \emph{which} notes are uncertain requires per-piece
adaptation and the graph. The third tests task-matched pretraining. A
bidirectional, mask-aware retraining at matched compute is a negative result,
with pooled error $+0.011$ worse, and the same leak pattern attempted to
re-enter through the new read-out and was detected by its signature. The
fourth is the kernel comparison, run under the two-stage pipeline. The
classical spectral profiles all show no difference from the additive default,
structure beyond time adjacency is what buys calibration, and music theory
helps as \emph{added} chord and voice-leading edges and hurts as a
\emph{replacement} pitch metric. Under the final model the added edges are
redundant at the operating point, with a clean density gradient across
masking levels, and they were not taken to a second held-out evaluation.

\section{Error spotting}
Error spotting corrupts a small fraction of notes and ranks every note by
its suspiciousness, so the measure is the quality of the ranking. The
two-stage graph model ranks better than every structure-free baseline on
every channel, with AUROC rising from $0.94 \to 0.98$ at $3\sigma$ and from
$0.85 \to 0.94$ at $2\sigma$. The proposed model, which scores each note by
its joint leave-one-out predictive surprise, improves further on all three
channels, reaching a velocity AUROC of $0.995$, an articulation AUROC of
$0.986$ and a timing AUROC of $0.978$. Error spotting is therefore the task
on which structure helps most clearly, and the advantage is larger under the
proposed model than under the two-stage pipeline.

\section{Denoising}
Denoising recovers the true values of the per-note variables from added
noise. With the noise level known, structure is better than per-note
shrinkage on both error and calibration, with all 8 paired differences having
intervals that exclude zero and coverage near $0.90$. The proposed model's
oracle-noise form is slightly better still, with RMSE $0.182$ and $0.276$ at
the two noise levels against $0.186$ and $0.282$, and coverage $0.90$. A
negative result holds unchanged under both models: estimating the noise level
blind from a single piece is unreliable in \emph{both} parameterizations,
because the intervals collapse, so transcription noise must be treated as
known for each system.

\section{Completion from an excerpt}
Completion predicts the expression of the rest of a piece from its opening.
Interpolation, with scattered hidden notes, is where the model is clearly
better, with RMSE falling from $0.450 \to 0.393$ and NLL from $0.21 \to 0.10$
at half the notes observed. When the prediction extrapolates far past the
excerpt, the music-model prediction carries it and the graph is neutral or
slightly harmful. The proposed model makes this boundary sharp. Its per-piece
feature weights, fit on the opening excerpt alone, can extrapolate wildly,
and one evaluation with a prefix of 25\% reached an RMSE of
$1.6\times 10^4$, so the cross-piece read-out head, which never adapts to the
piece, is the appropriate tool for excerpt extrapolation. Per-piece Bayesian
adaptation needs observed coverage of the feature space, and rendering from a
short excerpt does not provide it.

\section{Selective prediction}
Selective prediction discards the least-confident predictions and measures
the error on those that remain. Discarding lowers error most clearly for
articulation, where keeping the confident half cuts its RMSE from $0.83 \to
0.54$, and for catching the model's own misfires, where a timing blow-up at
$0.61$ overall falls to $0.07$ on the confident half. Only the graph provides
the per-note confidence that this triage needs, because the no-graph
baselines rank at piece level only. Under the proposed model the triage is
slightly better still, with a confident-half RMSE of $0.076$ against $0.080$
at matched abstention, so the property transfers from the two-stage pipeline
to the proposed model.

\section{Two boundaries}\label{sec:boundary}
Across the tasks, two boundaries emerge. Boundary (1), the structure
boundary, holds under both models: the graph prior helps whenever notes are
judged one by one for accuracy or confidence, and does not help when a
performance is collapsed into a summary. Boundary (2), the adaptation
boundary, belongs to the proposed model: per-piece Bayesian feature weighting
is better on every interpolation-style task and breaks down at excerpt
extrapolation, where the cross-piece head remains the appropriate tool. Both
boundaries are statements this thesis makes, and each is bounded by evidence
on both sides.

\chapter{Results: Strings and Winds from Tracked Audio}
\label{ch:phase2}\label{sec:phase2}
This chapter is stage 2 of the observation ladder, in which the prior of
the piano setting meets realistic measurement uncertainty. It carries the
proposed model to strings and winds, where the performer controls pitch.
Nothing in the latent prior changes. The graph and feature kernels of
\eqref{eq:gpfirst}, its first two terms, the hyperparameter fit
(\S\ref{sec:eb}) and the exact conjugate posterior (\S\ref{sec:gpfirst})
are reused. One term is absent: the music model is trained on piano, so
its embedding kernel takes no part here, and the evidence branch consists
of the hand-built score features alone. The observation-noise term belongs
to the likelihood, and the likelihood is what changes. Three things
therefore change, and the first section specifies each of them: the
channel set, the way the targets are obtained, and the observation noise,
which stops being negligible.


\section{The model in this setting: channels, targets and noise}
\label{sec:phase2-delta}

\subsection{The channel set}
A fixed-pitch keyboard affords no performer-controlled intonation, and its
dynamics is a MIDI velocity. Neither holds for strings and winds. We call
the per-note vector of channels the \textbf{bundle}, in the everyday sense
fixed in the Introduction, and in this setting it becomes
\begin{equation}
  y_i \;=\; \bigl[\, \tau_i,\ \log r_i,\ \ell_i,\ c_i,\ f_i^{\mathrm{vib}},\ \gamma_i,\ d_i^{\mathrm{vib}} \,\bigr]
  \;\in\; \R^{k},
  \label{eq:phase2-channels}
\end{equation}
where each channel is, as on piano, one scalar per note of the known
score. The first two channels keep their piano meaning: $\tau_i$ is the
onset residual against the score-time warp, re-anchored for this corpus
by the annotated onsets as described below, and $\log r_i$ is the
articulation ratio. The loudness channel $\ell_i$ replaces the MIDI
velocity $v_i$ and is read from the note's own audio. The note's span is
cut into four equal-length chunks of samples $x^{(q)}$, $q = 1, \dots, 4$,
the energy of each chunk is summarised by its log root-mean-square, and
\begin{align}
  \ell_i &\;=\; \frac{1}{4} \sum_{q=1}^{4} \log \mathrm{rms}\bigl(x^{(q)}\bigr)
  \;-\; \overline{\ell}_{\mathrm{track}},
  \label{eq:loudness}\\
  \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}[\ell_i] &\;=\; \frac{s^2_{\mathrm{chunks}}}{4}.
  \nonumber
\end{align}

The chunking is what makes loudness an estimate rather than a bare
number. The four chunks are internal replicates, so their mean is the
note's level, and the sample variance of the four log chunk-RMS values,
$s^2_{\mathrm{chunks}}$, yields the standard error
$s^2_{\mathrm{chunks}}/4$. This standard error is automatically larger for
notes whose level changes inside the note, during the attack or the decay,
which is exactly when one loudness value per note is least well defined.
Four is an untuned small default, enough replicates to estimate a spread
while the chunks remain long enough to average over waveform periods on
short notes. The term $\overline{\ell}_{\mathrm{track}}$ is the track's
mean of the per-note values, and subtracting it centres the channel per
recording. Absolute level is arbitrary per track, through microphone gain,
distance and mastering, so only relative loudness within a recording is
meaningful. The remaining channels are new to this setting. The static
intonation offset $c_i$ measures how sharp or flat the note's held centre
sits, and $(f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}, \gamma_i, d_i^{\mathrm{vib}})$ are the
vibrato rate, extent and onset delay defined below. The superscript keeps
the delay distinct from the performed duration $\delta_i$ of
\eqref{eq:logr}.

This vector leaves two decisions open, and one of them is settled by
measurement against a criterion fixed before the numbers were computed.
The delay is identifiable on $95$--$97\%$ of vibrato-identifiable notes
on both the reference and the tracked pitch curves, with a median
agreement of $18$\,ms between tracker and reference, so
$d_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ is a channel and $k = 7$ as written. On the
evaluation corpus (\S\ref{sec:urmp}) articulation has no measured offset
chain, that is, no sequence of annotated performed note ends from which
$\log r_i$ could be computed, so the bundle evaluated there consists of
the remaining six channels. The second decision stays open: whether
$\ell_i$, which is an amplitude rather than a keystroke, is comparable
enough with $v_i$ for the two to be discussed as one channel across
settings. We do not assume that they are.

What these quantities mean to a listener, how sharp or flat and by how
much, the vibrato's speed, width and delayed onset, the note's loudness
relative to its own recording, is spelled out in musical terms in
Appendix~\ref{app:glossary}. Structurally, what matters is that none of
these values is annotated by hand. Every one is \emph{estimated} from the
recording: a pitch tracker supplies the note's pitch curve, and the
per-note fit described next turns that curve into these numbers, each
with its own uncertainty. This is why the observation noise of
\eqref{eq:phase2-noise} becomes substantial in this setting. The corpus's
human annotations supply only the note spans, that is, the onsets,
durations and written pitches. Its annotated pitch curves serve to
calibrate the tracker and never as channel values.

\subsection{From a pitch curve to per-note targets}
Let $f_0^{(i)}(t)$ be the fundamental estimated on the sounding interval
of note $i$, restricted to the samples the tracker marks as voiced. Its
deviation from the equal-tempered target, in cents, is
\begin{equation}
  c_i(t) \;=\; 1200\,\log_2 \frac{f_0^{(i)}(t)}{f_{\mathrm{ref}}\, 2^{\,(p_i - 69)/12}} ,
  \label{eq:cents-curve}
\end{equation}
with $f_{\mathrm{ref}} = 440$\,Hz the frequency of A4, whose MIDI number
is $69$, so that the denominator is the equal-tempered frequency of the
written pitch $p_i$. This curve is not itself a target, because it mixes
a static mistuning with an oscillation. Following the standard sinusoidal
description of vibrato \cite{seashore1936,prame1994,prame1997}, it is
decomposed as
\begin{equation}
  c_i(t) \;=\; \underbrace{c_i}_{\text{intonation}} \;+\; u_i(t) \;+\; \epsilon_i(t),
  \qquad
  u_i(t) \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    \gamma_i \sin\!\bigl(2\pi f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}\,(t - t_i - d_i^{\mathrm{vib}})\bigr), & t \ge t_i + d_i^{\mathrm{vib}},\\[2pt]
    0, & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:vibrato}
\end{equation}
with $t_i$ the performed onset. Thus $c_i$ (cents) is the vibrato-free
centre, $\gamma_i$ (cents) the vibrato extent, $f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ (Hz)
its rate, and $d_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ (s) the delay before it begins.

Estimating $(c_i, f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}, \gamma_i, d_i^{\mathrm{vib}})$ is a
small nonlinear least-squares fit per note on the voiced samples, and
per-note vibrato parameter estimation from recordings is an established
analysis task \cite{driedger2016}. The fit runs in two forms. A continuous
sinusoid supplies $(c_i, \gamma_i, f_i^{\mathrm{vib}})$, and in that form
the delay coordinate is only a phase, identified modulo one period. The
delayed-onset form of \eqref{eq:vibrato}, in which the oscillation grows
out of the centre at $t_i + d_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$, supplies the delay
itself, with its own identifiability rule and a variance obtained by
linearisation. The centre $c_i$ is \emph{not} the mean of $c_i(t)$: the
mean coincides with the vibrato-free centre only over a whole number of
cycles, which a note rarely supplies, and perceived pitch likewise departs
from the curve mean on short vibrato tones \cite{dalessandro1994}.

The observation rule fixes every threshold by the development
measurements of \S\ref{sec:urmp}. Note $i$'s sample set is
\begin{equation}
  J_i \;=\; \bigl\{\, \text{frames } j:\; t_j \in [t_i,\, t_i + \delta_i),\;
  \text{voiced}_j,\; \pi_j \ge q_{0.2}(\text{track}) \,\bigr\},
  \label{eq:framerule}
\end{equation}
so that the tracker's per-frame confidence $\pi_j$ is thresholded at the
track's lowest quintile, which the development measurements identified as
the gross-error filter. From this set follow three observation decisions,
stated separately.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=\parindent,noitemsep]
  \item The intonation entry is observed if and only if $|J_i| \ge 4$ and
        $|\hat c_i| \le 150$ cents, the octave-failure rule validated
        against the reference.
  \item The vibrato entries additionally require identifiability, at
        least $8$ voiced frames and at least $1.5$ cycles at the fitted
        rate, without which $(\gamma_i, f_i^{\mathrm{vib}},
        d_i^{\mathrm{vib}})$ cannot be read from the note at all.
  \item Every other entry is missing for the model.
\end{itemize}
One choice remains open, namely a robust loss rather than least squares
inside the fit.

\subsection{Per-channel missingness}
The identifiability rule makes missingness structural rather than
incidental. A short note may support $c_i$ but no vibrato parameters, so
a target can be missing for some channels of a note and not for others.
We call each (note, channel) pair an \textbf{entry}, and the resulting
pattern of observed entries the \textbf{observation mask}. The conjugate
posterior of \S\ref{sec:gpfirst} assumes per-note masks, which is exactly
what keeps the observed block Kronecker-compatible (\S\ref{sec:gpfirst}).
Per-entry missingness breaks that structure but not the exactness: the
conjugate posterior conditions on an arbitrary observed index set, so
what must give way is the per-note mask interface and any
Kronecker-structured shortcut, not conjugacy. The model therefore accepts
per-entry observation masks. Its exactness in this generalised form is
verified against brute-force Gaussian conditioning, the per-note case
reproduces the published path, and a synthetic end-to-end pilot exercises
it with estimator-induced missingness (\S\ref{sec:phase2-pilot}).
Figure~\ref{fig:phase2-datapoint} shows one data point of each kind.

The missingness mechanism deserves naming \cite{rubin1976}. The
evaluation's hidden notes are missing completely at random by
construction, through a seeded coin per note. The estimator's missing
entries are not: the identifiability rule removes a vibrato entry exactly
when the note's vibrato is short or weak, so this missingness is
informative, missing \emph{not} at random and dependent on the value it
hides. Two consequences follow. The held-out scores stay interpretable,
but for the identifiable sub-population only: random masking inside an
informatively filtered set of entries is unbiased for the notes whose
vibrato the estimator can measure, not for all notes. And the posteriors
the model supplies at estimator-missing entries borrow from vibrato-rich
observed neighbours and may therefore be biased toward audible vibrato,
of which the delay posterior's occasional sub-zero stretches are the
visible signature. Filled entries are posterior extrapolations under the
prior and are to be read as such, not as recovered measurements.

\subsection{Heteroscedastic observation noise}
On piano the targets are read from clean MIDI and $\Sigma_e \approx 0$.
Here every target is an estimate, and its uncertainty is both known and
unequal across notes: the $f_0$ tracker reports a per-frame periodicity
or confidence, and the per-note fit of \eqref{eq:vibrato} returns a
parameter covariance. Writing $\sigma_{e,ij}^2$ for the variance of
channel $j$ of note $i$,
\begin{equation}
  \tilde y \;=\; y + e, \qquad
  e \sim \N\bigl(0,\ \Sigma_e\bigr), \qquad
  \Sigma_e = \operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sigma_{e,ij}^2\bigr),
  \label{eq:phase2-noise}
\end{equation}
which replaces the per-channel term $\operatorname{diag}(\varsigma^2)\otimes I$ of
\eqref{eq:gpfirst} by a full diagonal over (note, channel). Conjugacy is
untouched, since a diagonal is a diagonal, so the posterior and the
marginal likelihood are unchanged in form. The noise floor and the
safeguard of \S\ref{sec:eb} carry over.

One decision remains: whether the estimator-supplied variances enter the
likelihood unchanged, or carry a per-channel learned scale, so that the
evidence may discover that the tracker is over- or under-confident. We
call the first option the \textbf{given variances}, the estimator's own
variances used without rescaling. The synthetic pilot
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-pilot}) reverses the a-priori guess. With calibrated
estimator variances, the given variances are better on every channel,
and the learned scale over-shrinks the vibrato-extent channel (coverage
$0.76$--$0.81$ against $0.86$--$0.87$). The learned scale is therefore
the fallback for a mis-calibrated tracker, not the default. Real data
gave the same answer (\S\ref{sec:phase2-real}): with estimator octave
failures present, the learned scale collapses while the given variances
stay healthy, and after the octave-failure rule the given variances
remain the better-calibrated choice.

\subsection{What the per-note compression discards}
The channels of \eqref{eq:phase2-channels} hold every parameter fixed
within the note: they are time-averages. A development drift study
(Appendix~\ref{app:drift}) measures what the averaging discards. About
two thirds of notes carry a real within-note drift of the intonation
centre, found by the tracked and the reference curves independently,
with a median of $10.5$ cents per note, an order of magnitude above the
entry's reported standard error. Yet the discarded structure is
graph-white: within-note slopes carry essentially no note-to-note
correlation ($+0.03$, against $+0.59$ for timing, the channel the graph
demonstrably helps). The per-note resolution is therefore empirically the
right level for this model, and the mismatch is priced into the
residual-based entry variances, which is why calibration held on the
held-out test set. The discarded structure itself belongs to the waveform
setting's frame-level likelihood, whose amplitude envelope $A_i(t)$ and
pitch-curve model are built to carry it. Slope channels remain candidates
for later work under the $d^{\mathrm{vib}}$ template, included as output
and asserting nothing.

\subsection{The score-time warp and the alignment error}
The piano setting obtained the score-time warp $T(\cdot)$ from an aligned
beat grid supplied with the MIDI. There is no MIDI here. $T$ must come
from audio-to-score alignment, and its error propagates directly into
$\tau_i$, since $\tau_i = t_i - T(b_i)$ by definition. Worse, alignment
error is correlated along score time, which is precisely the structure
the graph prior expects expressive timing to have. A model fit naively
will therefore absorb some alignment error as expressive timing and
report it with confident error bars. There are two defensible responses.
One folds the aligner's own uncertainty into the $\tau$ row of
\eqref{eq:phase2-noise}, with the caveat that \eqref{eq:phase2-noise} is
diagonal, so it captures the error's scale but cannot represent its
correlation along score time. The other withholds $\tau$ from this
chapter's hypotheses entirely and reports only the channels the audio
measures directly. This is the single largest threat to validity in this
setting, and it has no analogue on piano.

On the chosen corpus the threat has a measured resolution. URMP's
note-level onset annotations anchor the warp without any audio aligner on
76 of 78 development tracks, half by exact score-order matching and half
by a pitch DTW. The two tracks without a usable warp remain in the
evaluation with their $\tau$ entries missing, not dropped. The
feasibility statistic uses a local leave-one-out warp: with score-onset
and performed-onset pairs $(b_j, t_j)$, each note gets a tempo line with
slope $\hat\varphi_i$ and intercept $\hat\psi_i$ fitted from its
neighbours alone,
\begin{align}
  (\hat\varphi_i,\, \hat\psi_i)
  &\;=\; \arg\min_{\varphi,\,\psi}\; \sum_{0 < |j - i| \le 8}
     \bigl(t_j - \varphi\, b_j - \psi\bigr)^2,
  \label{eq:localwarp}\\
  \tau_i &\;=\; t_i \,-\, \bigl(\hat\varphi_i\, b_i + \hat\psi_i\bigr),
  \nonumber
\end{align}
so the note being judged never enters its own warp. The result is a
median $\operatorname{std}(\tau)$ of $79$\,ms with lag-1 neighbour
correlation $+0.59$, that is, piano-scale timing with exactly the
structure the graph prior models. On this basis $\tau$ is a channel of
the evaluated bundle, and its noise row is the tempo line's leave-one-out
predictive variance, which carries the aligner error's scale but not its
correlation, as noted above. This is development evidence.

\subsection{The score graph and the tonal metric}
The graph construction \eqref{eq:adjacency} and its alternatives
(\S\ref{sec:graphvariants}) carry over, with per-channel length scales.
One earlier result becomes newly interesting. The kernel comparison
(\S\ref{sec:kernels}) found that replacing the semitone metric by the
circle-of-fifths metric \eqref{eq:tonaladj} made recovery and calibration
significantly worse: expressive behaviour travels with register
proximity, not tonal proximity. But that experiment measured timing,
articulation and velocity, none of which is a pitch. This setting
introduces a channel whose target is pitch itself. It is therefore an
open and cheap question whether the tonal metric that is worse for
expression is better for intonation, that is, whether notes a fifth apart
share a mistuning that notes a semitone apart do not, as temperament and
a fixed tuning reference would predict.

The test was run on the development set, with the hypothesis stated
before the numbers were computed (Appendix~\ref{app:tonal}). The
circle-of-fifths metric improves held-out intonation significantly on
both axes while re-imposing the familiar replacement penalty on timing.
The tonal geometry helps exactly where the target is pitch and hurts
where it is not, the first evidence in this thesis that a music-theoretic
\emph{geometry}, rather than merely music-theoretic \emph{edges}, earns
its place. The hypothesis was then evaluated on the held-out test set
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}): the recovery gain held, but the calibration
gain, the pre-specified primary measure, did not, its interval reaching
zero. The metric stays exploratory, and none of the main hypotheses of
this chapter depends on it.


\section{Data and protocol}\label{sec:phase2-protocol}
The corpus is URMP, described with the other corpora in \S\ref{sec:urmp},
together with the three preparatory measurements that govern its use:
tracker calibration, the composition-level split into a development set
and a held-out test set, and the sense in which recovery means agreement
with the estimator's targets rather than with a gold standard. That sense
applies to every number in this chapter. The evaluation protocol is that
of the piano setting, held-out imputation on both axes with a paired
per-piece bootstrap (\S\ref{ch:data}), and the selection discipline of
\S\ref{sec:hygiene} applies without exception. Because the targets carry
real noise, calibration rather than RMSE becomes the more informative
axis, and the predictive variance floor is no longer a technicality but a
first-order modelling term. The held-out test set of this setting is the 13-piece set of
\S\ref{sec:hygiene}, and the hypotheses it tests
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-real}) were fixed on the development set alone.


\section{Development results}\label{sec:phase2-devres}

\subsection{A synthetic pilot}
\label{sec:phase2-pilot}
Before any real audio, the full pipeline was validated end to end on
synthetic pieces whose per-note truth is known. On these pieces the graph
model is better than the no-graph ablation on every channel. Missing
vibrato entries, which no per-note method can even express, are recovered
at roughly $4\times$ better than prior scale with coverage at nominal,
and the given-variance noise default is settled there. This is a
synthetic result: it validates the machinery and settles design
defaults, and it asserts nothing about real audio.

\subsection{Results on real audio}
\label{sec:phase2-real}
The full pipeline then ran end to end on real recordings: tracked $f_0$,
confidence-filtered per-note estimation of $[c, \log\gamma, \log
f^{\mathrm{vib}}]$, per-note loudness $\ell$ (log RMS, centred per
recording), the onset-anchored timing channel $\tau$, and the delay
$d^{\mathrm{vib}}$ from the delayed-onset fit, which together form the
six-channel bundle. The estimator variances enter as the given variances,
with per-entry missingness from the identifiability rule and the
heteroscedastic model with per-entry observation masks. The per-track
vibrato-identifiable fraction ranges from $2\%$ to $97\%$ with median
$38\%$, so missingness is the norm, not an edge case. The run covers 77
of the 78 unique development tracks, after deduplication of shared
recordings and with one track below the minimum of 30 usable notes,
hiding 30\% of notes. One target-quality rule was added with measured
justification rather than by assumption: $2.2\%$ of intonation targets
are octave slips ($|c| \approx 1200$ cents), the cross-check against the
reference rejects every one of them (0 of 358 agree with the
reference-derived value), and they are marked missing, treated exactly
like unidentifiable vibrato.

Paired against the no-graph ablation under the given-variance noise
model, the declared default, recovery improves significantly on
intonation and on both vibrato channels ($\Delta$RMSE $-0.89$ cents,
$-0.26$, $-0.30$), calibration improves significantly on both vibrato
channels and on timing ($\Delta$NLL $-3.8$, $-0.43$, $-0.29$), and
coverage is $0.88$--$0.91$ at nominal $90\%$ on every one of the six
channels. Table~\ref{tab:phase2-dev} collects the paired contrasts. Under
this noise model the loudness, timing and delay recovery contrasts are
small and not significant ($-0.003$, $-0.002$, $-0.001$), and the
loudness NLL contrast is $+0.04$ \emph{against} the graph, small but the
bundle's one significant adverse result. In the per-family breakdown,
computed under the learned-scale contrast, the brass intonation contrast
is not significant on its own, while the vibrato gain holds within every
family and the intonation gain within strings and
woodwind.\footnote{The learned-scale fallback makes all six recovery

in the results record.} The ordering of systems is unchanged when scored
against the reference targets derived from the provided pitch curves.

\section{Two follow-up hypotheses on the held-out test set}
\label{sec:phase2-reuse}
Two hypotheses developed after the evaluation of \S\ref{sec:phase2-conf}
were tested on the same 13-piece held-out test set, which had therefore
already been used once: its targets and plain-graph fits were known when
the hypotheses were selected, while the hypothesis-specific quantities
had never been computed on it. Both hypotheses were developed on the
development set alone, with the primary measure, the decision rule, the
systems and the exact computation fixed before the test set was touched.
The inputs are the archived targets of the first evaluation, and the
masks, entries and seeds are those of Table~\ref{tab:phase2-conf}. This
second use of the held-out test set is weaker evidence than the single
use described in \S\ref{sec:hygiene}.\footnote{Reproduction records: the synthetic pilot of
\S\ref{sec:phase2-pilot} is recorded in
\texttt{docs/phase2\_synthetic\_pilot.md}, the two follow-up evaluations
in \texttt{results/corrnoise\_confirmation\_results.md} and
\texttt{results/phase2\_tonal\_confirmation\_results.md}, with their

correlated-noise computation below is the routine
\texttt{gp.noise\_corr} of Chapter~\ref{ch:future}.}

\subsection{Correlated timing noise}
The timing hypothesis of the main evaluation was not supported, and the
diagnosis pointed to the noise model: alignment error is correlated along
score time, which the diagonal noise row cannot represent. The revised
noise model places an AR(1) block in the timing channel's noise
covariance, $\Sigma_{\tau}(\rho)_{ij} = \sqrt{d_i d_j}\,\rho^{|i-j|}$ in
note order, with $d_i$ the given entry variances and $\rho$ chosen per
(track, seed) by the observed block's marginal likelihood on a fixed
grid. Held-out timing is predicted through the full covariance including
the noise cross-terms. Setting $\rho = 0$ recovers the published diagonal
path, which is the paired baseline, and the correlated-noise option of
Chapter~\ref{ch:future} is numerically identical to this computation. The
primary measure was the paired timing NLL, cluster-bootstrapped over the
40 unique tracks. The paired RMSE was a secondary measure that did not
enter the decision, and coverage and the share of (track, seed)
evaluations with $\rho > 0$ were descriptive.

The primary measure supported the hypothesis. The paired difference in
timing NLL is $-0.121$ with an interval of $[-0.156, -0.089]$, which
excludes zero, against a development basis of $-0.111$. The secondary
measure agrees: the paired RMSE difference is $-0.011$
$[-0.016, -0.007]$ s, from $0.058 \to 0.047$ s. Coverage stayed at
nominal, $0.908$ against the diagonal's $0.911$, and $\rho > 0$ on 79 of
80 (track, seed) evaluations, with median $0.45$. The risk stated in
advance, that this test set's timing channel is cleaner than the
development set's, showed as a smaller absolute recovery gain rather
than as an interval reaching zero. By the decision rule fixed in advance,
the correlated timing-noise row resolves the timing-calibration
hypothesis that the main evaluation had left unsupported, on this second
use of the held-out test set.

\subsection{The tonal metric}
The exploratory finding of \S\ref{sec:phase2-delta} and
Appendix~\ref{app:tonal}, that replacing the semitone metric of
\eqref{eq:adjacency} by the circle-of-fifths metric \eqref{eq:tonaladj}
improves held-out intonation, was tested with calibration as the primary
measure: the paired intonation NLL, tonal minus plain graph, under the
given variances, per (track, seed), with a 95\% bootstrap interval
required to exclude zero. Recovery, the paired intonation RMSE, was the
secondary measure and did not enter the decision, with its power stated
in advance at $0.52$--$0.63$ on this test set. The development timing
penalty was a descriptive prediction. All five arms ran together, and
the three plain arms are those of Table~\ref{tab:phase2-conf}.

The primary hypothesis was not supported. The paired intonation NLL
difference is $-0.023$ with an interval of $[-0.049, +0.001]$: the mean
lies on the predicted side but the interval reaches zero, against a
development basis of $-0.050$ $[-0.072, -0.029]$. The secondary measure
was supported, with a recovery difference of $-0.312$ cents
$[-0.599, -0.081]$ (development $-0.213$). The predicted timing penalty
did not appear under the given variances ($-0.002$ $[-0.011, +0.007]$).
By the decision rule fixed in advance, the thesis does not state that the
tonal metric improves intonation calibration. The metric stays
exploratory and Appendix~\ref{app:tonal} keeps its status. The three
plain arms reproduced Table~\ref{tab:phase2-conf} digit for digit, so
this evaluation left the earlier result untouched. Two descriptive
results are recorded beside the hypotheses: a small adverse loudness
calibration difference whose interval excludes zero ($+0.020$), and the
tonal graph improving on the no-graph ablation in intonation calibration
($-0.053$ $[-0.099, -0.010]$) where the plain graph had not
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-conf}: $-0.029$, not significant). The latter is
suggestive, was not a pre-specified hypothesis, and is not pursued further. Of
the two follow-up hypotheses, then, one was supported and one was not,
and the held-out test set has now been used twice.

\chapter{The Waveform as the Observation}\label{ch:phase3}\label{app:phase3}\label{sec:phase3-math}
The waveform setting is stage 3 of the thesis's progression: the
observation becomes the waveform itself, the endpoint toward which the
piano and strings-and-winds settings were built. Every study in this
chapter was conducted on the development set, and one finding of its last
section, the weak form of the methodological hypothesis at phrase scale,
was then carried to a single pre-specified evaluation on the held-out test
pieces of the strings-and-winds setting (\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}), under
the protocol of \S\ref{sec:hygiene} and as a disclosed third use of those
pieces. The chapter belongs to the main line of
the thesis, because the waveform setting is the most novel step of the
programme and because its design constraints have now been quantified on
real audio rather than assumed.

The audio likelihood replaces the per-note observation $\tilde y$ with the
waveform $x \in \R^{M}$:
\begin{equation}
  x \;=\; \Phi(z)\, a + \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \N(0,\, K_n),
  \label{eq:waveform}
\end{equation}
where $z$ collects the nonlinear position variables (tempo, timing,
intonation, vibrato), $\Phi(z) \in \R^{M \times 2HN}$ is the deterministic
harmonic synthesis basis, and $a \in \R^{2HN}$ are the stacked per-note
harmonic amplitudes. The basis is the horizontal concatenation of one
$M \times 2K$ block per note, with quadrature columns
$\cos\phi_k(t), \sin\phi_k(t)$ and cumulative phase
$\phi_k(t) = 2\pi k \int_0^t f_0(s)\, ds$ determined by $z$, where $H$ counts
the harmonics. Per-note amplitudes are shaped in time by an amplitude
envelope $A_i(t)$, which multiplies the columns of $\Phi(z)$ belonging to
note $i$. One asymmetry of this specification should be stated at the
outset. Loudness lives in the amplitudes $a$ and articulation in the
envelope supports, and neither is placed under the graph prior. Assigning
every per-note channel of the previous chapters a home, in $z$, in $a$ or
in $A_i$, with its structure stated, is part of designing the waveform
setting in full rather than a detail. With a Gaussian amplitude prior
$a \sim \N(\mu_a, \Sigma_a)$, the amplitudes marginalise exactly:
\begin{align}
  p(a \mid x, z) &= \N(m_a,\, S_a), \qquad
  S_a = \bigl( \Sigma_a^{-1} + \Phi^\top K_n^{-1} \Phi \bigr)^{-1}, \qquad
  m_a = S_a \bigl( \Sigma_a^{-1} \mu_a + \Phi^\top K_n^{-1} x \bigr),
  \label{eq:amp-post}\\[2pt]
  p(x \mid z) &= \N\bigl( x;\; \Phi\, \mu_a,\; \Phi\, \Sigma_a\, \Phi^\top + K_n \bigr),
  \label{eq:collapsed}
\end{align}
where $\Phi = \Phi(z)$, $\mu_a \in \R^{2HN}$ and
$\Sigma_a \in \R^{2HN \times 2HN}$ for $H$ harmonics with two quadrature
columns each. The implementation specialises the noise covariance to
$K_n = \sigma^2 I$, and the amplitude envelope $A_i(t)$ belongs to the
specification but is not yet implemented. Inference over the nonlinear $z$,
by a Laplace approximation or by variational inference initialised from the
nominal score, is the open step. The coupled graph prior, the first two
terms of \eqref{eq:gpfirst}, plays the role of the structured component of
$p(z)$.

\section{Scalar intonation from the waveform}
\label{sec:phase3-study}

The collapsed likelihood \eqref{eq:collapsed} was first applied to real
audio in a development study designed to answer two questions. The first is
whether the waveform carries the per-note expressive information directly,
with no pitch tracker in the loop. The second is whether the resulting
posterior is calibrated. The study is exploratory, its evidence is confined
to the development set, and its outcome, a gap between the quantity the
waveform model estimates and the quantity the reference defines, shapes the
design of everything that follows in this chapter.

\subsection{Design of the single-note study}

One observation is one note's audio segment of $m$ samples at $16$\,kHz, up
to two seconds long ($m \approx 10^{3.5}$--$10^{4.5}$), cut at the annotated
note boundaries of a development URMP track. The study covers $376$ notes
from seven tracks spanning all three instrument families. For each note the
$f_0$ curve is parameterised by the variables of the six-channel bundle
(eqs.~\eqref{eq:cents-curve}--\eqref{eq:vibrato}). On vibrato-identifiable
notes the extent, rate and delay scaffold is taken from the per-note
estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}, the one estimator-supplied ingredient
of the study. The design matrix is the harmonic basis of \eqref{eq:waveform}
with amplitudes piecewise constant over the four chunks of
\eqref{eq:loudness}, giving $p = 2H \times 4 = 64$ columns at $H=8$
harmonics. The amplitudes are marginalised exactly, and an $O(mp^{2})$
Woodbury form of \eqref{eq:collapsed}, whose equality with the dense form is
verified in unit tests, keeps the segment-length likelihoods tractable.

Inference over the intonation centre $c$ is by grid search, with a coarse
pass at $1$-cent spacing over $\pm 50$ cents around the score-nominal pitch
followed by a fine refinement at $0.03$ cents ($0.06$ for the deviation
variant). This is exact 1-D inference up to discretisation, and no Laplace
approximation is involved. Four position models are compared. The first is a
constant $c$. The second adds AR(1) observation noise, a residual whitening
with the correlation fitted per note. The third adds the linear drift term
of the \S\ref{sec:phase2} drift study, with the slope profiled on a grid.
The fourth adds a marginalised pitch-curve deviation prior of eight
zero-mean Hann bumps along the curve Jacobian, with a prior standard
deviation of $5$ cents per coefficient, treated exactly like the amplitudes.
Each posterior is scored against the reference centre, the per-note NLLS
estimator applied to the corpus's annotated pitch curve, by absolute error
and by coverage at nominal $90\%$. A fifth condition calibrates the
machinery itself on synthetic notes built from real notes' fitted structure,
for which the model is true by construction.

\subsection{Accuracy and calibration against the reference}

Three facts are visible in Table~\ref{tab:phase3-study} and
Figure~\ref{fig:phase3-study}. First, the waveform carries the information.
The best variant reaches a median of $2.29$ cents from the reference,
approaching the estimator chain's $2.01$ overall and improving on it on the
winds ($2.67$ vs $3.27$), the family in which the tracker's failure modes
are concentrated. The two-note pilot that preceded the study showed the same
at $0.7$--$1.6$ cents on long clean notes. Second, every model refinement
improves accuracy, yet coverage against the reference is invariant at
$0.01$--$0.03$. The drift term of the \S\ref{sec:phase2} study transfers to
the waveform domain (paired median $-0.18$ cents), and the deviation prior
improves accuracy further. A better mean model, however, shrinks the
posterior width as fast as it shrinks the error, and the AR(1) floor does
not help, because the structured misfit is concentrated \emph{in band}, at
the harmonics, where a stationary coloured floor cannot reach. Third, the
machinery itself is calibrated: on model-true synthetic notes the same
posterior covers at exactly $0.90$ (median $|z| = 0.53$).

\subsection{The estimand gap and its consequence for the bundle}

Together these three facts locate the overconfidence. It is an
\textbf{estimand gap}: the constant the harmonic model estimates and the
constant the NLLS estimator defines on the annotated curve are different
functionals of the same performance, about two cents apart, and no
within-model refinement can close a gap between two different questions,
which is why none of the three refinements did. The design conclusion
follows. The waveform posterior should enter the six-channel bundle the way
every estimator does, as a measurement whose noise row is its posterior
variance plus an empirically calibrated per-note discrepancy floor at the
observed $\sim 2$-cent scale. This restores calibration by construction
under the convention, used throughout the bundle, that each estimator's own
variances are taken as its noise row.

A first integration study on the same seven tracks, also exploratory,
implements exactly this design. The waveform posterior enters as a seventh
bundle channel, its coupling is learned per piece by the evidence, and the
discrepancy floor is calibrated from visible notes only, at a median of
$3.1$ cents, which is the estimand-gap scale found automatically. Held-out
intonation recovery then improves on every track-seed pair: the paired
difference is $-1.21$ cents and the difference in NLL is $-0.24$, both with
intervals excluding zero, and coverage is held. Removing the floor degrades
calibration against the reference by $+0.22$, with an interval excluding
zero, while improving nothing, so the floor performs its designed role.
Waveform-native calibration, a bridge model for the estimand gap, is the
frontier beyond that. This empirically established constraint, rather than
the synthesis sketch, is the starting point for the design of the waveform
setting.

\subsection{A spectral-mixture deviation prior}

The spectral-kernel families reviewed earlier
\cite{wilson2013,remes2017,alvarado2016} were evaluated at exactly this
slot on the development set. Swapping the study's deviation prior for a
matched-rank spectral-mixture prior changes nothing at the median ($2.25$
vs $2.29$ cents) and is worse on the paired mean by $+0.11$ cents. That
difference has an interval excluding zero at the note level but not under
piece-level clustering, so it is read as no gain rather than as harm. The
difference is concentrated where the scaffold rate is unreliable, since a
vibrato band pinned at a wrong rate is worse than frequency-neutral bumps.
The bump basis is therefore retained. The contribution of these families is
conceptual, in the curve-level scoring, the coherence bound and the
realised-quantity read-out rule, and a future revival would marginalise the
band location rather than pin it.

\subsection{The task structure across the three settings}

The visible/hidden partition of \S\ref{sec:gpfirst} is an evaluation device
of the piano setting, not a feature of the method. In the piano setting the
performance is supplied as clean MIDI, so the per-note targets are read off
almost exactly ($\Sigma_e \approx 0$), and there is nothing to infer unless
a prediction task is manufactured. This is why a fraction of notes is masked
and imputed from the rest: the masking makes the structured prior testable,
but it is not intrinsic to score-informed transcription. In the
strings-and-winds setting the observation is still note-level, since audio
enters only to derive per-note targets, so the imputation protocol carries
over with substantive noise. The waveform setting is where the situation
becomes the genuine one. The model is given only the score and the waveform,
there are no per-note observations of any kind, and hence there is no
visible/hidden split: every note's expressive variables are latent and are
inferred jointly from the single audio observation through the likelihood
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:waveform}.

The unifying view across the three settings is that \emph{only the
likelihood changes}, from per-note noisy targets with a mask ($P = 0$ on
held-out notes) in the piano setting, to $f_0$-derived targets in the
strings-and-winds setting, to the global audio likelihood $p(x \mid z)$ in
the waveform setting, while the graph Gaussian process prior of
\S\ref{sec:graphgp} is carried over unchanged. Correspondingly the task
graduates from held-out imputation to full inference. At the waveform the
natural evaluation is on held-out pieces and by posterior-predictive
resynthesis, rather than by note-level masking. The remainder of this
chapter moves toward that evaluation in two steps, first by inferring a
within-note pitch curve and note boundaries from the audio, and then by
placing the score graph inside that inference.

\section{The differentiable curve posterior}
\label{sec:phase3-curve}

\subsection{Design of the curve study}

The second round of studies implements the chunked harmonic design and the
collapsed likelihood \eqref{eq:collapsed} in an automatic-differentiation
framework, with equality to the reference implementation verified in unit
tests. Inference over a within-note pitch curve, a constant $c$ plus knot
values $u$ under the two-component kernel of the estimator studies, here
acting as a prior over $u$, then runs by gradient descent, and a full joint
Laplace approximation at the MAP supplies bands and a per-note evidence.
This adopts the differentiable-synthesis idea of the DDSP line of work
\cite{ddsp} at the implementation level only. The objective remains the
exact amplitude-marginalised likelihood and the prior remains the Gaussian
process, with no neural encoder and no spectral surrogate loss. The five
kernel hyperparameters were additionally fitted by evidence ascent, shared
across notes, which gained $+717$ nats over 24 notes. Under the fitted
values both component scales loosen by a factor of $2$--$3\times$ while the
vibrato mean stays at $5.5$--$5.7$\,Hz.

\subsection{Curve-level measurements}

Four measurements on the development set, on the 376 notes and seven tracks
of \S\ref{sec:phase3-study}, characterise the curve posterior. The first
concerns accuracy. The curve posterior reaches a median reference-frame RMSE
of $3.2$ cents against the pYIN frames' $4.1$ (paired $-0.46$, better on
56\% of notes). It is clearly better on winds ($2.3$ vs $5.2$) and still
behind the tracker on strings ($4.9$ vs $3.1$), which reproduces the family
split of the scalar study, with a heavier failure tail (q90 $\sim 11$ vs
$8.6$ cents). The evidence-fitted prior is better than the hand-set scales
on 70\% of notes. The second concerns calibration. The Laplace band is a
parameter band and covers the annotation frames at $0.32$--$0.36$, which is
the estimand gap of \S\ref{sec:phase3-study} reproduced at the curve level,
while coverage on model-true synthetic notes is nominal.

The third measurement concerns integration into the bundle. Replacing the
scalar waveform channel of the bundle study by the curve's realised-average
read-out, a linear functional of the Laplace posterior, reproduces the
integration gain, a difference of $-1.36$ cents on all 14 pairs with an
interval excluding zero, and halves the empirical discrepancy floor
($3.1 \to 1.8$ cents). Reading out the estimator's own functional from the
inferred curve thus closes about half the estimand gap by construction,
and beyond this the curve and scalar read-outs show no measurable
difference at $n=14$. The fourth measurement concerns boundaries. A
perturb-and-recover pilot on two-note segments, with complementary sigmoid
windows over per-note designs and a flat prior on the boundary, converges
independently of the starting point (spread $\le 1$\,ms from
$\pm 40/\pm 80$\,ms starts) in 7 of 8 pairs, so the gradient mechanism for
joint inference works and no phase pathology appears. It converges, however,
to an acoustic transition point $11$--$87$\,ms (median $25$) from the
annotated onset: the likelihood finds the \emph{acoustic} transition,
whereas the annotation marks the \emph{musical} onset.

\subsection{Joint chains and note transitions}

The follow-up study models the transition between notes as a region.
Chains of 3--5 consecutive notes are fitted jointly, per-note curves plus
boundaries, on 47 chains and 154 boundaries, since four of the seven tracks
yielded eligible chains. Each boundary consists of two independent sigmoid
edges, the old note's fade-out and the new note's fade-in, with the width
between them under a lognormal prior. The data reject the region hypothesis
in an informative way. Free widths collapse to the 1\,ms minimum on
essentially every boundary, yet they are better than a fixed 10\,ms control
on 77\% of 256 paired fits (median $-3.4$\,ms edge error), and width does
not absorb pitch error (correlation $-0.01$). The acoustic transition, as a
harmonic likelihood sees it, is sharp. The median edge error improves to
${\sim}18$\,ms from the pilot's 25.

The principal result of this study is the deployment arm. Initialised from
score-nominal boundaries, that is from the local tempo-line prediction with
no access to the annotation anywhere, the joint fit halves the median
boundary error, $39.4 \to 19.5$\,ms. This is the first genuine instance of
the chapter's inference problem, with the score and the waveform as input
and boundaries and curves as output. Two costs are measured alongside.
Joint chains are less accurate on the curves than single-note fits
(reference-frame RMSE $4.9$--$5.2$ vs $3.16$ cents, excluding the boundary
regions), and the residual ${\sim}18$\,ms is systematic. It is the gap
between the acoustic and the musical onset restated, which makes a
transient model at the note joint, broadband columns that the harmonic
basis cannot represent, the next lever rather than a larger optimiser.

\section{The score prior inside the waveform inference}
\label{sec:phase3-priorvalue}
Everything above this point fits each note's curve under its own prior, and
the score graph of Chapter~\ref{ch:model} has not yet entered the waveform
inference. The development study of this section builds that bridge at
three spans, short chains, note boundaries and phrases, and asks the
thesis's methodological question at the waveform. The question has two
sides: whether score-informed structure adds value where the acoustic
evidence is absent, and whether it costs anything where the evidence is
strong. Two hypotheses are distinguished. The weak form holds that score
structure at least matches a naive temporal prior, and the strong form
holds that it carries information beyond the common offset shared by the
notes of a chain. Both are evaluated on the development set, the findings
are reported in full, and the weak form at phrase scale is then tested
once on held-out tracks (\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}).

\subsection{Design of the prior-value experiment}

Three cross-note priors are placed over one latent per element of a chain,
the per-note intonation constant $c$, or for the boundary span the per-joint
offset from the tempo line, at matched marginal variance and with
everything else identical: the amplitude-marginalised likelihood, the data,
the optimiser and its budget. The independent prior is $\N(0,\sigma^2 I)$.
The temporal prior is $\N(0,\sigma^2 K_{\mathrm{chain}})$ with the
note-order chain graph. The score-graph prior is $\N(0,\sigma^2 K_{G})$
with the score graph of \S\ref{sec:scoregraph}, which couples notes by time
and by pitch proximity. Both structured kernels are $(I+L)^{-1}$,
diagonally normalised, and $\sigma^2$ is the track's own reference
variance, a population scale that is not tuned. Every arm carries a free
per-chain common-mode scalar $t \sim \N(0, 50^2)$, with
$c = t\mathbf{1} + \tilde c$, marginalised as a rank-one term. This scalar
hands the independent prior the chain's observed mean, so that whatever
structured gain survives it is element-level transfer rather than the
transfer of a shared offset.

Each chain is evaluated under two conditions. In the \textbf{masked}
condition the middle eligible element is removed from the likelihood and
from the design, so that its posterior is exactly the prior conditional
given its observed neighbours, the waveform-level analogue of the held-out
imputation task of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. In the \textbf{clean}
condition the full audio is used. The hidden element must be removed from
the design and not only from the audio samples. If only its samples were
masked, its soft-window columns would remain in the likelihood, and the
window tails beyond the $50$\,ms mask margin would leave a few samples'
worth of column mass on the neighbours' audio. With the amplitudes
marginalised, that leak alone carves a well of about $38$ nats along the
hidden constant across $\pm100$ cents, against $4$--$35$ nats from the
prior. In a preliminary version of the protocol that masked only the
samples, every arm's masked estimate was accordingly a leakage-driven
optimum, uncorrelated with the truth and worse than predicting zero, and
its apparent structured gain was not evidence. With the hidden note's
columns removed, the independent arm returns exactly the prior mean, as it
must. The lesson applies to every prior-value design of this kind: a hidden
element must be dropped from the model and not only from the samples, and
the common-mode scalar must sit in every arm.

Hypotheses and a falsification rule were fixed in advance of each
evaluation. Structure is predicted to be better than independence on
masked elements, in paired comparison. Any advantage of the score graph
over the temporal chain is predicted to appear where a pitch recurs. The
arms are predicted to show no difference on clean audio. Finally, the
absence of a significant structured-versus-independent gap under the
common-mode scalar is taken to mean that score structure carries no
element-level information beyond the shared offset. The hidden element is
scored by its distance to the reference, the reference centre for $c$ and
the annotation for a joint, and by coverage at $90\%$ from a joint Laplace
approximation at the MAP. The evidence is from the development set, on four
to nine instruments.

\subsection{Intonation on short chains}

The chain-eligible notes of four development tracks give $47$ chains of
$3$--$5$ notes, $25$ of which contain a recurring pitch. The prediction was
that structure is better than independence on the hidden note. Without the
common-mode scalar, structure halves the hidden note's error, from $14.42$
to $6.80$ cents (temporal) and $7.62$ cents (score graph) at the median.
The paired differences are $-5.78$ with interval $[-8.16, -3.27]$ and
$-6.24$ with interval $[-8.49, -3.79]$, each better on $79\%$ of the
chains, while the two structured arms show no measurable difference
($-0.46$, $[-1.48, +0.51]$) and the recurring-pitch stratum shows a trend
short of significance ($-0.86$, $[-2.04, +0.34]$, $n=25$). With the scalar,
the independent arm reaches $6.42$ cents (temporal $6.55$, score graph
$5.90$) and the three arms show no measurable difference
(Table~\ref{tab:phase3-priorvalue}), the recurring-pitch stratum giving
$+0.01$, $[-0.88, +0.91]$.

The scalar moves the independent arm by $-6.85$, with an interval excluding
zero, and the structured arms by $-0.9$ and $-0.6$, neither with an
interval excluding zero. The whole structured gain was therefore the
transfer of the track's shared tuning offset, and the pre-stated
falsification condition is met. On $3$--$5$-note chains, neither score
structure nor plain temporal adjacency carries measurable note-level
intonation information beyond the shared tuning offset. On clean audio the
arms agree to the second decimal ($2.22$--$2.37$ cents in both
configurations), so the structure costs nothing where the evidence
dominates. The calibration is as follows. The structured conditional bands
cover at $0.94$ without and $0.89$ with the scalar. The zero-mean
independent band covers at $0.77$, being miscentred on tracks with a tuning
offset, and the scalar resolves this ($0.96$, conservative). The scope of
this span is intonation constants only, with the boundaries fixed at the
annotation, kernel shape $s=1$, and about $150$\,s per fit including the
Laplace approximation.

\subsection{Boundaries}

The second span applies the same design to the per-joint boundary offsets
from the tempo line, the starting points of the chain study's deployment
arm, on the same $47$ chains ($40$ non-unison), with the hidden middle joint
removed from the design by hard cuts. The prediction was again that
structure is better than independence on the hidden joint. The outcome of
the first span repeats. On masked joints the arms show no measurable
difference (Table~\ref{tab:phase3-priorvalue}), with temporal against
independent at $-3.65$, $[-10.90, +3.03]$, and medians of $28$--$31$\,ms,
while every arm is better than the tempo line by $10$--$14$\,ms, each with
an interval excluding zero. A hidden joint inherits its chain's common
offset and nothing beyond it. On clean audio the three arms land on
identical boundaries ($19.6$\,ms median, against the chain study's $19.5$),
so the prior costs deployment nothing.

Two calibration facts accompany this outcome. The masked conditional bands
are far too wide, with coverage $1.00$ at $73$--$95$\,ms, because the prior
scale is the track's full timing variance. The observed joints' bands are
far too narrow, with coverage $0.05$ at $0.7$\,ms: the likelihood pins the
acoustic transition to under a millisecond while the annotation marks the
musical onset some $20$\,ms away, which is the estimand gap of
\S\ref{sec:phase3-study} restated at the boundary. Setting the prior width
at chain scale instead, as the median within-chain timing variance, brings
the independent arm's band to coverage $0.88$ and the structured arms'
bands to $0.75$, with the point estimates unchanged. No single
population-scale width calibrates all three arms, and a per-chain width
fitted by evidence is the principled remedy, which was not pursued here.

\subsection{Phrase scale}

If pitch-proximity coupling carries information anywhere, it should be
where a pitch recurs at a distance the temporal chain cannot reach
directly. From all $78$ development tracks, $48$ legato runs of $6$--$10$
notes were selected in which the hidden middle note's pitch recurs at an
index distance of at least two (median three notes, $1.2$\,s, with a
hidden-to-recurrence edge weight of $0.83$). The pre-stated primary
contrast is supported: the score graph is better than the temporal chain
by $-1.63$ cents ($[-2.93, -0.46]$, better on $65\%$ of phrases). The
proposed mechanism, however, is not supported. The graph shows no
measurable difference from the scalar-augmented independent arm ($-0.07$,
$[-0.42, +0.27]$), the temporal chain is worse than independent ($+1.56$,
$[+0.26, +2.92]$, an interval excluding zero), and there is no
dose-response with the edge weight ($r=-0.03$). At phrase scale, adjacency
pulls the hidden note toward neighbours that resemble it less than the
chain mean does, and the pitch edge pulls it back to about the chain mean.
The graph's advantage over the temporal prior is therefore the harm of that
prior undone. Clean rows show no difference, and coverage is
$0.90$--$0.96$ throughout.

Across the three spans the two hypotheses receive different support.
Against the thesis's own stated baselines, independent or purely temporal,
the score-graph prior is never worse and at phrase scale is better than
the temporal prior, so the weak form is supported. Against the common-mode
baseline that the first span showed to be mandatory, the score-graph prior
adds nothing measurable, for intonation at either span or for timing at
chain scale, so the strong form is not supported by the evidence so far.
What the waveform does obtain from the score prior can be stated plainly: a
note or a joint with no acoustic evidence inherits its context's common
offset with a calibrated band. Each study is exploratory evidence from the
development set on four to nine instruments.

The same comparison at whole-track scale on the tracked bundle
(Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}) adds the missing statistical power and
gives the same answer from the other side. There the score graph is better
than a nearest-neighbour time chain on intonation and vibrato, with
intervals excluding zero, but it equals the same graph with its pitch term
removed, and in the piano setting the pitch-less graph is slightly better
than the principal model. At every scale, corpus and observation type
tested, the structure that helps is smoothness in score time over about two
beats, and the score-specific pitch coupling does not show.

\subsection{Held-out evaluation at phrase scale}\label{sec:phase3-heldout}

\chapter{Motif Structure in the Score Graph}\label{ch:motif}
The attribution results of the previous chapters leave the score graph with
a modest role. On both corpora its value is temporal smoothing over about
two beats, and the pitch term adds nothing measurable
(Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}, Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}). This chapter
asks whether a different kind of score structure, one that the time window
cannot see, carries expressive information: the repetition of a motif. A
performer who shapes a motif at its first statement is expected to shape
its returns in a related way, and a return that has not yet been heard
should then be predictable from the statements that have. The chapter adds
one edge family to the score graph from motif annotations, states what the
family does to a hidden note, and evaluates it on Beethoven sonata
movements under a masking scheme that hides whole occurrences. The
development studies and a pre-specified evaluation on held-out performances
follow the protocol of \S\ref{sec:hygiene}. The evaluation uses score
features only, without the music model, since the question is one score
structure against another on identical features.

\section{An edge family from motif occurrences}\label{sec:motif-family}
The BPS-motif annotations \cite{hsiao2023bpsmotif} mark, in all thirty-two
first movements of the Beethoven piano sonatas, the occurrences of each
movement's motifs: an occurrence $O_k$ is a set of notes with a span
$[b_k^{\mathrm{start}}, b_k^{\mathrm{end}}]$ in beats and a type $q(k)$, and
two occurrences of one type are returns of one motif. Returns differ in
length and in note count, so there is in general no note-to-note map
between them. The family therefore works in a coordinate that every
occurrence shares, the relative position of a note inside its occurrence,
\begin{equation}
  u_i \;=\; \frac{b_i - b_k^{\mathrm{start}}}{\,b_k^{\mathrm{end}} - b_k^{\mathrm{start}}\,}
  \;\in\; [0, 1], \qquad i \in O_k,
  \label{eq:motif-u}
\end{equation}
and joins notes of two different occurrences of the same type by their
closeness in that coordinate,
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(W_{\mathrm{motif}}\bigr)_{ij} \;=\;
  \max_{k \neq l,\; q(k) = q(l)}\;
  \mathbf 1[i \in O_k]\, \mathbf 1[j \in O_l]\;
  \exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(u_i - u_j)^2}{2\,\ell_u^2}\Bigr),
  \qquad \bigl(W_{\mathrm{motif}}\bigr)_{ii} = 0 .
  \label{eq:motif-adj}
\end{equation}
The length scale $\ell_u$ is fixed at a tenth of the span. Returns of equal
length then receive one-to-one edges between corresponding notes, and
returns of unequal length receive a soft block between the notes near the
same relative position. Nothing is aligned and nothing inside the family is
learned. A note that belongs to no occurrence, or whose type occurs only
once in the excerpt, has no motif edge.

The family enters the model of Chapter~\ref{ch:model} in two forms. In the
\textbf{shared form} the adjacency \eqref{eq:adjacency} gains the family
with one weight,
\begin{equation}
  W \;=\; W_{\mathrm{time}} + w_{\mathrm m}\, W_{\mathrm{motif}},
  \qquad \Lg = D - W,
  \qquad K_G(s) = U \diag\bigl(g(\nu; s)\bigr) U^{\top},
  \label{eq:motif-shared}
\end{equation}
so that one graph kernel, built from the Laplacian of the combined
adjacency, is shared by the three channels through the coregionalization
matrix $B$ of \eqref{eq:gpfirst}, and $\log w_{\mathrm m}$ joins the
hyperparameters fitted by the per-piece evidence. The evidence can drive
$w_{\mathrm m}$ to zero, which recovers the time-only graph exactly. In the
\textbf{per-channel form} the family is its own additive term with one
scale per channel,
\begin{equation}
  K \;=\; B \otimes K_G(s) \;+\; \diag(c_{\mathrm m}) \otimes K_{\mathrm m}
  \;+\; \sum_f \diag(c_f) \otimes X_f X_f^{\top}
  \;+\; \diag(\varsigma^2) \otimes I,
  \qquad K_{\mathrm m} = U_{\mathrm m} \diag\bigl(g(\nu_{\mathrm m}; 1)\bigr) U_{\mathrm m}^{\top},
  \label{eq:motif-pc}
\end{equation}
where $(\nu_{\mathrm m}, U_{\mathrm m})$ is the eigendecomposition of the
Laplacian of $W_{\mathrm{motif}}$ on the motif notes, $K_{\mathrm m}$ is zero
on every other note, and $c_{\mathrm m} \in \R^{3}$ is learned by the
evidence like the feature scales $c_f$. Since $K_{\mathrm m}$ factorizes as
$X_{\mathrm m} X_{\mathrm m}^{\top}$ with
$X_{\mathrm m} = U_{\mathrm m} \diag(g^{1/2})$, the per-channel form is a
feature kernel of \eqref{eq:gpfirst} and needs no change to the model's
inference. The two forms differ in what they let the evidence decide: the
shared form ties the three channels to one motif weight through $B$, and
the per-channel form lets a channel switch the family off on its own.

\section{What a motif edge does to a hidden note}\label{sec:motif-conditional}
For one channel with the additive kernel $g(\nu; s) = 1/(1 + s\nu)$ the
prior is the Gaussian Markov random field with precision $I + s \Lg$, and
the conditional of a hidden note $h$ given all others is explicit,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E\bigl[y_h \mid y_{-h}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{s \sum_{j} W_{hj}\, y_j}{1 + s\, d_h},
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}\bigl[y_h \mid y_{-h}\bigr] \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + s\, d_h},
  \qquad d_h = \sum_j W_{hj} .
  \label{eq:motif-cond}
\end{equation}
Splitting the degree into its two families,
$d_h = d_h^{\mathrm{time}} + w_{\mathrm m} d_h^{\mathrm{motif}}$, writes the
conditional mean as a mixture of two neighbourhood means,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb E\bigl[y_h \mid y_{-h}\bigr] \;=\;
  \frac{s\, d_h^{\mathrm{time}}}{1 + s d_h}\;\overline{y}^{\,\mathrm{time}}_h
  \;+\; \frac{s\, w_{\mathrm m}\, d_h^{\mathrm{motif}}}{1 + s d_h}\;\overline{y}^{\,\mathrm{motif}}_h ,
  \label{eq:motif-mix}
\end{equation}
the mean of the temporal neighbours and the mean of the corresponding notes
in the other occurrences, weighted by relative position. The full posterior
of \eqref{eq:posterior} mixes the same two sources through $B$, the feature
kernels and the noise. Two readable predictions follow. If performers shape
a motif in a related way at each return, the motif mean is closer to the
hidden value than the temporal mean, the evidence raises $w_{\mathrm m}$,
the error falls and the predictive band tightens by the extra degree. If
they do not, the motif mean is noise, the evidence drives $w_{\mathrm m}$ to
zero and every motif arm coincides with the time-only graph. The second
outcome is the family's own falsification route and needs no separate
test.

\section{Evaluation design}\label{sec:motif-design}
The data are the ASAP first movements with an annotated counterpart. The
annotation's note list is matched to the ASAP score by crotchet onset and
pitch after a global offset search over the opening notes, and a movement
is used when at least $85\%$ of its excerpt notes match. Twenty-five
annotated movements have an ASAP score. Two of them sit in the piano
held-out test set and are excluded, and two more fall below the matching
threshold, which leaves twenty-one movements, each as the first $400$
matched notes of one aligned performance, the thesis's excerpt convention.
A note belongs to an occurrence when its onset lies in the occurrence's
span and its annotated type is the occurrence's type. The arms are the
time-only Gaussian graph, which the attribution studies single out, and the
same graph with the motif family in one of its two forms, paired on
identical masks and data. Every arm carries the score features with a
per-piece bias column, the coregionalization matrix, the floored noise, the
exact per-piece evidence and the predictive-variance floor of
\eqref{eq:floor}.

Two masking schemes are used. The random scheme is the thesis protocol,
$40\%$ of notes hidden at random. The occurrence scheme hides whole
occurrences: for every motif type with at least two occurrences, each
occurrence is hidden with probability $0.4$, with at least one occurrence of
the type left observed and at least one hidden, and every note outside a
motif observed. The occurrence scheme is the application's case, a passage
not yet played whose earlier statements have been heard, and it is where the
family can act, since under the random scheme the two-beat time window
already interpolates inside an occurrence. Scores are computed on the hidden
motif notes whose type has another occurrence with an observed note, per
channel, as the Gaussian log score and the root mean squared error, paired
per piece with seeds averaged, with $95\%$ bootstrap intervals over pieces.
The hypotheses were fixed before each run. The first study predicted a
lower timing log score for the motif arm against the time-only graph on the
hidden motif notes under the occurrence scheme, with the interval excluding
zero, and a lower timing error. The second study predicted that the
per-channel form keeps that gain and removes the velocity miscalibration
of the shared form. The third study predicted that edges built from a
model's predicted occurrences keep at least half of the annotated edges'
gain on the same notes. The learned weights, the other channels, the random
scheme and the pitch term were recorded without prediction.

\section{Development results}\label{sec:motif-dev}

\subsection{The shared form}\label{sec:motif-pilot1}
With whole occurrences hidden, joining their notes to the observed returns
of the same motif lowers the timing log score on those notes by $0.117$
nats against the time-only graph, interval $[-0.245, -0.006]$, on
twenty-one movements and two seeds. The pitch term of the thesis's graph
gives $-0.014$ on the same notes, interval $[-0.046, +0.016]$. The timing
error falls by $0.0026$~s, interval $[-0.0054, -0.0003]$, which is the gain
the pitch term also gives, so on timing accuracy the motif edges do what
the pitch term does and the extra is in calibration. The clearest gain is
articulation accuracy, root mean squared error $0.637$ to $0.611$, a
difference of $-0.033$ with interval $[-0.066, -0.007]$, with the log score
moving the same way but with an interval covering zero. Velocity gets
worse in calibration, log score $+0.069$, interval $[+0.005, +0.138]$, and
the coverage of the $90\%$ band falls from $0.878$ to $0.823$: the motif
edges tie the velocities of returns together more tightly than performers
play them, and one weight shared by the three channels cannot loosen one
channel alone. Under the random scheme the family is mostly invisible, as
expected. The evidence treats the family as a switch per piece, as it
treats the feature kernels: the learned weight falls below $0.1$ on eight
movements and exceeds $2$ on six.

\subsection{The per-channel form}\label{sec:motif-pilot2}
The second study repeated the first on the same movements and masks with
four seeds, the first two of which reproduce the first study exactly, and
added the per-channel form of \eqref{eq:motif-pc}. Its prediction was that
the per-channel form keeps the timing gain of the shared form and removes
the velocity miscalibration. The second half holds: the velocity log score
on the hidden motif notes falls by $0.086$ against the time-only graph,
interval $[-0.200, -0.018]$, the velocity error by $0.0057$, interval
$[-0.0106, -0.0023]$, and the coverage of the $90\%$ band rises from
$0.878$ to $0.944$. The first half does not: the per-channel form changes
the timing log score by $-0.005$, interval $[-0.076, +0.054]$. By the rule
fixed before the run, whatever timing value the shared form had came from
the coupling of the three channels through one weight, not from the edges
on their own.

The four seeds also change what the shared form's timing gain is. On all
four it is $-0.071$ with interval $[-0.230, +0.097]$, and on the two added
seeds alone $-0.026$ with interval $[-0.247, +0.259]$. The per-movement
differences have a median near zero, with three movements far below and one
far above, so the timing effect of the first study rested on two seeds and
three movements. The effect that holds on every pair of seeds and in both
forms is articulation accuracy: the root mean squared error on the hidden
motif notes falls from $0.732$ to $0.698$ in the shared form, a difference
of $-0.035$ with interval $[-0.060, -0.013]$, and to $0.713$ in the
per-channel form, $-0.019$ with interval $[-0.038, -0.005]$, with fifteen
and thirteen of the twenty-one movements below zero. The learned scales say
the same thing directly. On the timing channel $c_{\mathrm m}$ falls below
$0.01$ on nineteen movements, so the evidence switches the family off
where the time-only graph is already well calibrated. On the articulation
channel it exceeds $0.1$ on ten movements, and on the velocity channel it
is kept on seven. The two forms trade. The shared form gives the larger
articulation gain and a small timing error gain, $-0.0027$~s with interval
$[-0.0055, -0.0004]$, at the cost of velocity coverage, $0.835$. The
per-channel form gives a smaller articulation gain, velocity on both axes
and coverage at nominal, and nothing on timing. Adding the pitch term to
the per-channel form changes nothing. Under the random scheme the family
again has little to act on, and the per-channel form's timing log score
over all hidden notes is $+0.087$ with interval $[-0.000, +0.241]$, at the
edge of worse. The measured value of the family at this stage is therefore
articulation accuracy, and the pre-specified evaluation of
\S\ref{sec:motif-heldout} takes that as its primary measure.

\subsection{Predicted occurrences}\label{sec:motif-stage2}
The third study replaces the annotated occurrences by those predicted by
a graph-based motif discovery model \cite{lalang2026motif}, taking for each
movement the predictions of the cross-validation fold that held it out.
A predicted occurrence is a set of notes, mapped to the excerpt by onset
and pitch as the annotations were, with its span the first and last
matched onset and its type the model's pattern index. The masks and the
scored notes remain those of the annotated occurrences, identical to the
second study, so the edge source is the only change. At the note level the
predicted membership has a precision of $0.12$ to $1.00$ and a recall of
$0.44$ to $0.99$ against the annotation, with a median F1 of $0.78$. On two
movements the model predicts several times the annotated number of motif
notes and the precision falls below $0.2$. The prediction fixed before the
run was that the predicted edges keep at least half of the annotated
edges' timing log-score gain on the same notes, with an interval excluding
zero.

With the shared form the predicted edges lower the timing log score by
$0.034$, interval $[-0.085, +0.003]$, which is $0.48$ of the annotated
$0.071$ on the same notes with an interval that reaches zero, so the
prediction is not met, by a small margin on both counts. On articulation
accuracy, the effect that is robust in the second study, the predicted
edges give $-0.0135$ with interval $[-0.0317, +0.0030]$, four tenths of
the annotated $-0.0345$. The two edge sources act on the same movements:
their per-movement timing differences correlate at $0.59$ and their
articulation differences at $0.86$, and fifteen of the twenty-one
movements lie below zero for both. The movements with the lowest
membership precision receive large learned weights without a gain on the
annotated notes, while among the rest the size of the effect does not
order by F1. The per-channel form with predicted edges changes nothing and
costs $0.0014$~s of timing error, interval $[+0.0005, +0.0024]$. The
family can therefore be built from a model's occurrences on a score
without annotations and keeps about half of its effect at this size, with
intervals that cover zero on twenty-one movements.

\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}

\section{Interpretation}
The attribution of \S\ref{sec:attribution} assigns the two halves of the
result to two parts of the model: per-piece feature weighting recovers the
hidden values, and the score graph calibrates the uncertainty. The posterior
decomposition sharpens this picture from inside a single fit. The graph
component and the music-model component act nearly orthogonally at each
note, with a component correlation of $\approx 0$, so the two are complements
rather than rivals. Which of them dominates is itself a per-piece property
that the evidence assigns, and the assignment can invert completely between
pieces (Fig.~\ref{fig:component-contrast}). Two questions remain that the
numbers alone do not answer: why structure improves calibration rather than
only accuracy, and why the music model's marginal contribution is
concentrated in a single channel.

Structure improves calibration because coupling correlated notes lets each
held-out note borrow statistical strength from its neighbours. Borrowing
sharpens the posterior mean, but its more important effect is on the
posterior variance, which then reflects how well a note is actually
determined by its context. An independent predictor must choose a single
global uncertainty for every note. The graph gives each note its own, and
that per-note uncertainty is what brings coverage to the nominal level.

The music model's marginal contribution is concentrated in velocity because
dynamics is the channel most predictable from learned performance
conventions. Phrase-level swells and metrical stress are conventions that a
sequence model captures and that local hand-built features do not. Timing is
the channel on which every model's error plateaus, since much of its
variance is note-local. Articulation is comparatively idiosyncratic. On
those two channels the music model's additional information therefore has
the least to add.

The motif edges of Chapter~\ref{ch:motif} are the one score structure in
this thesis whose value lies outside the two-beat time window. Where the
time-only graph is already well calibrated, on timing, the evidence
switches the family off, and it keeps the family on articulation, the
channel on which the hand-built features and the music model have least
to say. Returns of a motif inform each other on how the notes are
detached and held, and that is a kind of information no local window can
reach.

\section{Limitations}
Two results are supported on held-out test sets that were evaluated once
against hypotheses fixed in advance (\S\ref{sec:hygiene}): the symbolic
piano result and the six-channel result on real recordings. The latter rests
on a single corpus, the 13 held-out pieces of URMP, and two of its findings
run against the model. The secondary timing-calibration hypothesis was not
supported, and timing recovery showed a small adverse difference whose
interval excludes zero. Two follow-up hypotheses
were then evaluated on the same strings-and-winds test set a second time,
with the re-use disclosed (\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}). The revised timing
noise model was supported, and the tonal metric's primary calibration
prediction was not. Evidence from a re-used test set is weaker in kind than
evidence from a test set evaluated once.

The waveform evidence comes from the development set, with one exception.
Its tests of the thesis's methodological hypothesis, three spans
covering short chains, note joints and phrases
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-priorvalue}), support only the weak form of that
hypothesis. The score prior outperforms a purely temporal prior at phrase
scale, and that one finding was supported in a pre-specified evaluation
on the held-out strings-and-winds pieces, their third disclosed use
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-heldout}). Beyond the context's common offset the
score prior adds nothing measurable at the spans tested, on the
development set or on the held-out pieces. A further development-set finding qualifies the graph
itself. On both corpora the graph's value is reproduced, and on piano
slightly exceeded, by the same graph with its pitch term removed
(Appendix~\ref{app:trackscale}). The structure that does the work is
therefore smoothness in score time over about two beats, not the pitch
coupling that makes the graph a score graph. The results supported on the
held-out test sets stand as comparisons of the full model with its
alternatives, but their attribution is narrower than the model's name
suggests.

The motif study is a development result on twenty-one movements of one
composer with hand-made annotations, and its one pre-specified evaluation
uses further performances of the same movements rather than new pieces
(\S\ref{sec:motif-heldout}), so it tests generalisation across performers
and not across repertoire. Its effect is confined to one channel,
articulation. The timing effect of the first development study did not
hold on more seeds, and edges from a discovery model keep about half of
the effect with intervals that cover zero at this size.

The remaining limitations concern the data and the model's form. The piano
targets depend on the quality of a single corpus's
score$\leftrightarrow$performance alignment (ASAP), and the evaluation sets,
although held out and filtered for contamination, are modest in size, with
30 development pieces and 20 held-out test pieces. Two model-level
limitations are observed rather than hypothetical. The Gaussian likelihood's
quadratic tail can amplify a handful of timing outliers on a very steady
piece into the pooled NLL (\S\ref{sec:guard-gp}), and per-piece feature
weighting breaks down under excerpt extrapolation (\S\ref{sec:boundary}).
Generalisation beyond these settings is untested.

\section{Threats to validity}
The main threats to validity were addressed explicitly rather than assumed
away. Target leakage was found and corrected, and the correction was
verified by structural tests and by a bitwise end-to-end audit of the
proposed model's path (\S\ref{ch:results}). Train/test contamination was
filtered (\S\ref{sec:contam}). Hyperparameter fit collapse was safeguarded
against and made visible by reporting the median and the worst piece
(\S\ref{sec:eb}). Multiple-comparison and selection pressure was met by
per-piece paired significance throughout and, decisively, by the held-out
test set of \S\ref{sec:hygiene}, evaluated once against hypotheses fixed in
advance, which exposed one development-set advantage that did not replicate.
The threat that remains open is external validity beyond the two corpora
studied, ASAP for piano and URMP for chamber ensembles.

\chapter{Future Work}\label{ch:future}

\section{Robust observation models and safe completion}
Three refinements follow from observed limitations of the model whose
results are supported on the held-out test sets. Each was evaluated on the
development set, and each is implemented as an optional capability that
leaves the reported model and its published tables unchanged. None of them
is part of the reported protocol, and adopting any of them would require a
further evaluation of the kind described in \S\ref{sec:hygiene}, fixed in
advance and carried out on fresh data. The three are a heavy-tailed
observation model for timing, a predictive floor as its cheaper alternative,
and a safe mode for the completion task.

A heavy-tailed (Student-$t$) observation model for timing works at both
levels at which it can be placed. As a deployment-time predictive,
variance-matched with $\nu = 5$, it removes the NLL tail demonstrated in
\S\ref{sec:guard-gp} completely and without any change to the fit: the
documented blow-up falls from $105.2$ to $-1.7$ with coverage intact. As an
inference-level noise model, a Gaussian scale mixture fit by leave-one-out
EM reweighting, it additionally brings timing coverage to nominal and
improves articulation recovery by $-0.018$, with an interval excluding zero.
The second effect arises because an observed timing outlier corrupts the
coupled channels through the coregionalization, and only a robust fit undoes
that corruption. Fits under the $t$ noise model must be scored with the
matched $t$ predictive, since a Gaussian score of a $t$ fit is incoherent.

Deployment-time predictive floors, the seemingly cheap engineering
alternative, were found to be ineffective. The failure is a tail-shape
problem rather than a scale problem. A floor raises the predictive scale of
every note, but the outliers that dominate the tail already carry an
appropriate predictive scale, so a floor cannot reach them. The negative
result is useful in that it rules out the simplest remedy and locates the
problem in the shape of the likelihood.

The completion task of \S\ref{sec:boundary} can be made safe against
extrapolation by a combination of two tests, each of which is only half of
the answer on its own. A Mahalanobis coverage test, workable only in the
observed excerpt's low-rank frame, is silent under interpolation, as
required, but blind to catastrophic notes that lie inside the excerpt's
feature coverage. A $3\sigma$ disagreement test against the cross-piece head
supplies the other half. Together they tame every blow-up, the worst
evaluation falling from $480{,}266$ to $0.7$, while keeping the per-piece
model's advantage wherever adaptation helps. Blow-ups also occur under
interpolation masks, so the boundary concerns the determination of the
feature weights rather than extrapolation alone.

\section{Strings and winds: open questions}
The strings-and-winds result of Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2} leaves four open
items: the corpus, the timing channel, the tonal metric, and additional
channels. The first concerns corpora beyond URMP. In URMP the note onsets
are annotated, and the timing channel's noise row is the predictive variance
of a local tempo-line fit to those annotations. On any corpus without
note-level onset annotations the general audio-alignment problem returns,
and the timing channel's noise row must then carry an aligner's error.

The timing hypothesis of the first strings-and-winds evaluation, the
calibration of $\tau$, was not supported, and the diagnosis named its own
follow-up: a correlated noise row for $\tau$ that represents the structure
of alignment error along score time. That follow-up was first evaluated on
the development set. An AR(1) block over note order, with its one parameter
chosen by the evidence, improves timing NLL by $-0.111$ with an interval
excluding zero, moves coverage toward nominal, and reduces timing RMSE by
${\sim}20\%$ ($\approx 99 \to 80$\,ms). The correlation was detected on
97--100\% of the piece-seed combinations across three independent runs,
with fresh mask seeds and with a joint refit. The revised model was then
supported on the second, disclosed evaluation of the held-out test set
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}): the paired differences are $-0.121$ in timing
NLL and $-0.011$ s in timing RMSE, both with intervals excluding zero, and
coverage is at nominal. What remains is adoption. Making the correlated row
the default noise model of the timing channel would change the published
tables and therefore calls for its own evaluation fixed in advance, and on a
fresh corpus the hypothesis is untested.

The circle-of-fifths replacement geometry \eqref{eq:tonaladj}, which hurt
piano expression, helps intonation on both axes in the development set
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-delta}). It was evaluated a second time on the held-out
test set with calibration as the pre-specified primary measure
(\S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}). The recovery gain held, $-0.312$ cents with an
interval excluding zero, and the calibration gain did not, $-0.023$ with an
interval reaching zero. The metric therefore stays exploratory. What remains
is a fresh corpus and a design powered for recovery, which a power analysis
made before that evaluation places above ${\sim}20$ pieces. Nothing from
this route is adopted.

The within-note drift and decay slopes measured in \S\ref{sec:phase2-delta}
remain candidates for additional channels. They would follow the template of
the vibrato onset delay $d^{\mathrm{vib}}$, which entered the bundle as an
output with its own estimator variance and with no prediction attached to
it. Neither slope is part of the reported bundle, and adding either would
extend the output rather than change the model. Whether they are worth
adding depends on whether a transcription use-case calls for them.

\section{The waveform: the joint loop}
\label{sec:future-phase3}Chapter~\ref{ch:phase3} stops at chains of a few
notes. Within those chains the score prior couples the per-note constants
(\S\ref{sec:phase3-priorvalue}), where it transfers only the context's
common offset, and the curves themselves still carry independent priors.
What remains is the \emph{joint} loop, in which all notes of a performance
are inferred at once under the graph prior, by Laplace or variational
inference over $z$. Three further components belong to that loop: a
transient model at the note joints, which the chain study indicates as the
next step, the estimand bridge between the harmonic model's constant and the
annotation chain's functional, and the amplitude envelope $A_i(t)$ brought
under the prior. The evaluation design changes with it. With no per-note
observations there is no visible/hidden split, and the natural protocol is
held-out pieces with posterior-predictive resynthesis.

\section{Motif structure: the next steps}\label{sec:future-motif}
Chapter~\ref{ch:motif} stops at one composer, one performance per movement
in development, and hand-made annotations. Three steps follow from its
results. The first is a model across performers: the held-out evaluation
treats further performances of a movement as further draws of the same
targets, and a performer-level term, shared across the movements one
performer plays, would turn that into a model of how a performer shapes a
motif. The second is the discovery model as the only source of
occurrences, on scores without annotations and on other composers, since
the predicted edges keep about half of the annotated effect on the
Beethoven movements and the question is whether that fraction holds where
no annotation exists to compare against. The third is the form of the
family. The present family is undirected and joins every pair of returns
alike. A directed form, in which earlier statements inform later ones but
not the reverse, matches how a listener hears a return, and the width
$\ell_u$ of the relative-position kernel, fixed in this thesis, could be
learned by the same evidence that learns the weight. The family also
transfers without change to the strings-and-winds bundle, where the
returns of a motif may share intonation and vibrato as well as timing and
articulation.

\section{Incorporating music theory}\label{sec:musictheory}
Music theory can inform the model in two places, the graph or kernel and
the prior mean, which is equivalently the feature kernels. The two routes
are not exclusive, each is testable by the same held-out
recovery-and-calibration criterion, and both have been evaluated. The graph
route gave one positive and one negative result, both instructive, and the
feature route gave a negative result that an embedding probe explains. A
single criterion, stated at the end of this section, decides whether any
ingredient from either route is kept.

\subsection{In the graph and the kernel}
Two constructions in this route are implemented (\S\ref{sec:graphvariants})
and evaluated (\S\ref{sec:kernels}), and they gave opposite results.
\emph{Replacing} the semitone ruler with a circle-of-fifths pitch metric
\eqref{eq:tonaladj} makes both recovery and calibration worse, with paired
intervals excluding zero: expressive behaviour travels with register
proximity, not tonal proximity. \emph{Adding} same-onset (chord) and
stepwise voice-leading edges \eqref{eq:harmadj} on top of the plain graph is
the one intervention that improves both axes with intervals excluding zero.
The general lesson, with the qualifier that the strings-and-winds setting
supplies, is that music theory earns its place in the kernel as extra
relations rather than as a substitute geometry, for channels whose target
is not pitch. On intonation, where the target is pitch itself, the same
substitute geometry helps (Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}), so the rule is
channel-relative.

What remains open in this route is the part that the score alone cannot
supply. Cadential and functional edges, such as tonic and dominant
relations, require a harmonic analysis, since the implemented chord edges
encode onset simultaneity and not chord identity
(\S\ref{sec:graphvariants}). Metrical-hierarchy and phrase weights could
form further edge families. Their feature-level counterparts were evaluated
in the feature-kernel route below, but as graph structure they are untested.
A Tonnetz or neo-Riemannian lattice metric and the Tymoczko voice-leading
distance are alternatives to \eqref{eq:fifths} that have not been tried.

\subsection{In the feature kernels}
Fourteen music-theoretic score columns, covering local key, metrical weight,
dissonance, phrase salience, motif repetition and tessitura, change nothing
on any channel at any masking level, with no paired interval excluding zero.
(Motif repetition as an edge family, rather than as a column, is the graph
route's counterpart and is evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:motif}.)
A linear and random-features probe of the embeddings rules out the obvious
explanation, that the columns duplicate what the music model already
encodes. The embeddings encode voicing, rhythm and register but \emph{not}
tonality: scale degree, mode and local key sit at chance. Taken with the
tonal-metric result of the graph route, the consistent reading is that tonal
structure carries no measurable marginal signal for these three expressive
channels at this scale, while the rhythmic and voicing structure that the
columns add is already in the kernel. The full study is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:theoryfeat}. The columns remain available as an
interpretable probe and are not part of the reported model.

A single criterion decides whether an ingredient from either route is kept.
A music-theory ingredient is retained only if it measurably improves
recovery or calibration over the plain score graph on held-out data.
Otherwise it is reported as motivation rather than as machinery. Both routes
are directly testable within the existing protocol, so the criterion can be
applied to every candidate listed above without a new evaluation design.

\section{Unknown score support}\label{sec:future-amt}
A discrete latent layer over note identities and onsets would move the model
toward automatic music transcription proper. The score support $S$, assumed
known throughout this thesis, would become a latent variable with its own
prior, and the expressive variables would be inferred jointly with it. Every
component of the present model is conditioned on $S$, so the model would be
retained as the conditional inference inside a larger inference over the
score. It is the assumption relaxed last, once the structured performance
model is established.

\chapter{Conclusion}\label{ch:conclusion}
This thesis reads a recording as score, expression, timbre and noise, and
recovers the expression layer note by note together with its uncertainty.
The proposed model is a single multi-output Gaussian process on the score
graph, with the channels coupled by coregionalization, the side information
marginalized into the kernel, and every hyperparameter learned by the
per-piece evidence. It contains the strongest two-stage pipeline as a
special case, the diagonal-$B$, fixed-graph, plug-in-mean, no-feature slice
of its parameter space on the shared-shape restriction, and it recovers
hidden expressive detail better than that pipeline. This prediction was
fixed in advance and is supported on a held-out test set of pieces that no
modelling decision had touched.

The marginal value of each ingredient is isolated. Per-piece Bayesian
weighting of score features and music-model embeddings does the recovering,
and the graph makes the uncertainty calibrated, a contribution that the
held-out test set also supports. The music model earns its place as a
feature provider inside the kernel. One step further, on the development
set of both corpora, the graph's value is reproduced by the same graph
without its pitch term, so the structure rewarded is smoothness in score
time over about two beats. Six downstream tasks draw two boundaries:
structure helps note by note but not for whole-performance summaries, and
per-piece adaptation helps interpolation but not excerpt extrapolation. The
evaluation is protected against leakage, contamination, fit collapse and
selection inflation.

The piano contribution stands on its own, and the same prior has been
carried, unchanged in form, to intonation, vibrato, loudness, timing and
vibrato onset delay on real recordings. On the strings-and-winds held-out
test set the predictions for intonation recovery, vibrato calibration and
coverage are supported, and the secondary timing hypothesis is not. Its diagnosed remedy, correlated timing noise across
notes, was then supported on a second, disclosed evaluation of the same test
set. A tonal-metric calibration hypothesis evaluated alongside it was not
supported.

The step to audio through a differentiable synthesizer is where the most
novel work lies. Its development studies reach whole pitch curves whose
median error against the reference centre is below that of the tracker's
frames, and note boundaries inferred jointly from score-nominal starting
points. They also fix the design constraint that the remaining joint loop
must solve, bridging the estimand gap between the harmonic model and the
annotation chain. The score prior has entered that inference over three
spans, with a two-sided answer. A note or joint with no acoustic evidence
inherits its context's common offset with a calibrated band, and the score
graph outperforms a purely temporal prior at phrase scale, a finding
supported once on held-out tracks, but nothing measurable beyond the
common offset has been found, there or on the development set. The strong form of the hypothesis is left for the joint loop.

The attribution opened the thesis's last question, whether score
structure outside the time window carries expressive information. One
further edge family, from the returns of a motif, lowers the articulation
error of hidden returns on the development set in both of its forms, is
switched off by the evidence on timing, and keeps about half of its effect
when the occurrences come from a motif-discovery model instead of the
annotations. Its pre-specified evaluation on held-out performances is
reported in \S\ref{sec:motif-heldout}.

\chapter{Development lineage: the two-stage pipeline}\label{app:lineage}
The proposed model was reached through an earlier version, the two-stage
pipeline, which combines a plug-in mean with a per-channel graph GP on the
residual. The final model contains that pipeline as the corner of its
constraint space in which every constraint is switched on
(\S\ref{sec:special-cases}), so the earlier version survives as a nested
special case of the proposed model and as its ablation chain. This appendix
records the per-channel, precision-side formulation that the pipeline used,
since that is the form in which the graph prior is most easily read. The
posteriors of the constrained models, the ablation sequence of the
development, the selection history and the two-stage results of the
downstream tasks form the development record and are kept in the repository
beside the code.\footnote{The development record is the design document
\texttt{docs/graphgp\_first\_design.md} in the repository, together with the
results documents it names. It also records the ablation sequence and the
selection history that led from the per-channel form of this appendix to the
proposed model.} The main line carries the comparison of the two models on
the held-out test set (Table~\ref{tab:headline-conf}).

\section{The per-channel form, precision-side}
With $B$ diagonal and the feature kernels switched off, the joint GP
factorises into three scalar graph GPs, and each of these is more naturally
written through its \emph{precision}, the inverse covariance
$\Qg = K_G^{-1}$ scaled per channel. The two precisions used in the
development sequence are
\begin{align}
  \text{additive:} \quad & \Qg = \lambda I + \eta\, \Lg,
      && \lambda > 0,\;\; \eta \ge 0;
  \label{eq:additive-prec}\\[2pt]
  \text{graph Mat\'ern / SPDE:} \quad & \Qg = \sigma_g^{-2}\,(\kappa^2 I + \Lg)^{\alpha},
      && \sigma_g > 0,\;\; \kappa > 0,\;\; \alpha \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 1},
  \label{eq:matern-prec}
\end{align}
where the additive form matches \eqref{eq:additive} with shape
$s = \eta / \lambda$ and scale $1/\lambda$, the scale being carried by
$B_{cc}$ in the normalised parameterisation, and the Mat\'ern form matches
\eqref{eq:matern} in the same way. The additive precision has a direct
reading. The term $\eta\,\Lg$ penalises differences between connected notes,
while the ridge $\lambda I$ pulls the field toward its mean and sets the
overall scale. The choice between precision and kernel is computational
rather than a modelling choice. The precision $\Qg$ is sparse, because each
note couples only to its graph neighbours, which is precisely the Markov
property of a \textbf{Gaussian Markov random field} (GMRF), and this
sparsity makes the per-channel posterior below cheap. The full model, by
contrast, is naturally written on the covariance side, since the
coregionalization and the feature kernels add dense terms, and it is
evaluated spectrally through \eqref{eq:spectral} with one eigendecomposition
per piece.

With a plug-in mean $\mu$ and a diagonal observation precision $P$, equal to
$1/\sigma_e^2$ on observed notes and $0$ on masked notes, the per-channel
posterior is
\begin{equation}
  \Sy \;=\; \bigl(\Qg + P\bigr)^{-1}, \qquad
  m \;=\; \Sy \bigl(\Qg\, \mu + P\, \tilde y\bigr)
  \label{eq:posterior-chan}
\end{equation}
which is the precision-side counterpart of \eqref{eq:posterior}, and to
which \eqref{eq:posterior} reduces under the constraints. The plug-in mean
itself is one of three choices. The first is $\mu = 0$. The second is a
ridge regression on the hand-built features $X_{\mathrm{feat}}$. The third
is the \textbf{music-model mean} $\muLM$, a ridge head $W_{\mathrm{head}}$,
fitted once on a piece-disjoint split, applied to the embeddings of
\S\ref{sec:features},
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{\mathrm{LM},i} = W_{\mathrm{head}}^{\top}\,[\, h_i;\, 1\,],
  \qquad
  y = \muLM + \xi, \qquad \xi \sim \N\!\bigl(0,\, \Qg^{-1}\bigr),
  \label{eq:lm-mean}
\end{equation}
with the graph modelling the residual $y - \muLM$. The hyperparameters
$\theta = (\lambda, \eta, \sigma_e^2)$ are fitted per channel by the same
marginal likelihood \eqref{eq:marglik} restricted to that channel, with the
plug-in mean subtracted from $\tilde y$, with the same noise floor, and with
a per-channel safeguard. The safeguard is a fallback sequence: the marginal
likelihood first, then the calibration-split objective, then the
conservative decoupled fit with $\eta = 0$ and
$\lambda = 1/\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}[\tilde y_o - \mu_o]$. The
per-channel safeguard differs from that of the joint model in one respect.
Its screen tests \emph{recovery} only, that is, the error against the
mean-only fallback, whereas the overconfidence screen of \S\ref{sec:eb}
exists only in the safeguard of the joint model. The two screens are
related, not identical, and the ablation sequence that led from this form to
the proposed model is part of the development record named above.

\chapter{Piano development studies}\label{app:phase1dev}
This appendix collects the development studies of the piano setting in
full. Each of them has a short summary in the main line
(\S\ref{sec:masksweep}, \S\ref{sec:devstudies}, \S\ref{sec:musictheory}),
and all of the evidence reported here comes from the development set under
the protocol of \S\ref{sec:hygiene}. The studies concern, in turn, the
sensitivity of the results to the masking level, the sufficiency of
hand-built score features, calibrated deep baselines, task-matched
pretraining of the music model, the comparison of graph kernels in the
two-stage regime, and music-theoretic feature columns together with probes
of the embeddings.

\section{The masking-level sweep, in full}\label{app:masksweep}
The published operating point hides \maskRate{} of the notes of each piece.
That fraction was inherited from earlier work rather than derived, so its
influence was measured on the development pieces after the held-out
evaluation. The hidden fraction was swept from 50\% down to 10\%, with the
leak-free mask-aware embeddings recomputed at each fraction, since
leak-freeness requires blanking exactly the notes that each mask hides. The
sweep was extended to the leave-one-out limit, where every note is predicted
from all the others through the closed-form GP identity. At that limit only,
two relaxations of the protocol apply. The hyperparameters are fitted on the
full piece, which is the textbook leave-one-out cross-validation estimator,
and the embeddings are the leak-free pre-velocity readout rather than
per-mask re-embeddings. Both relaxations apply identically to all three
systems, so the comparison of their ordering remains a fair paired
comparison, although the absolute numbers of the leave-one-out row are not
strict-protocol values.

The ordering of the models, with the proposed model below the no-graph
ablation and that below the features-only model, is stable at every level
including leave-one-out, so the operating point is arbitrary but not
fortunate. The paired contribution of the graph is significant from 50\%
down to 20\% hidden and at the leave-one-out limit. The contribution of the
embeddings grows monotonically with observation density, from $-0.005$ RMSE
at 50\% hidden to $-0.021$ at leave-one-out, paired, as context-dependent
features would be expected to behave. Coverage stays at 0.92--0.93
throughout, so calibration is insensitive to the masking level. One
piece-seed combination at 30\% hidden reproduces, on a development piece,
the Gaussian-tail failure of the log score described in
\S\ref{sec:guard-gp}, identically across all three systems, which is
consistent with the failure being a property of the tail of the likelihood
rather than of any one model. Figure~\ref{fig:masksweep} shows both curves.

\section{Hand-built score features and the music model}
The second study asks whether hand-built score features suffice in place of
the music model, on the development set. Against the 25-feature hand-built
score baseline fitted under the same protocol, the music model shows no
measurable difference in average error. Its edge, which is significant,
lies in calibration and in the velocity channel. The two sources of
information also combine. Under the two-stage plug-in baseline,
concatenating the two means lowers the RMSE from $\rmseNetGraph$ to
$\rmseStack$, and in the proposed model, where both enter as feature
kernels, the marginal value of the embeddings is $-0.008$ RMSE and $-0.034$
NLL, paired and significant, with the cost of their removal reproduced in
direction on the held-out test set. Per channel, the mean-only edge of the
music model over the 25-feature baseline is confined to velocity, with no
measurable difference on timing and articulation. This is the same
placement that the decomposition of \S\ref{sec:attribution} recovers from
inside the fitted model.

\section{Calibrated deep baselines}
The comparisons of the main line give the deep model a single shared
predictive scale, which understates it as a rival on the calibration axis.
Two deep baselines that produce their own per-note uncertainty were
therefore evaluated on the development set under the identical strict
protocol and information set: the 25 features together with the music-model
embeddings, standardized per piece, with strict mask-aware embeddings at
evaluation and the same per-piece variance floor. The first is a
heteroscedastic MLP head, which outputs $\mu_c(x)$ and $\log\sigma_c^2(x)$
per channel and is trained by Gaussian negative log-likelihood on the head
pieces. The second is a five-member deep ensemble of such heads. The
proposed model is better than both on both axes by wide paired margins. Its
RMSE is $0.360$ against $0.450$--$0.454$, a paired difference of $-0.090$
with interval $[-0.115, -0.067]$, and its NLL is $-0.404$ against $-0.069$
to $-0.104$, a paired difference of $-0.30$ with interval $[-0.40, -0.21]$.
Ensembling five heads adds almost nothing over one.

Where the deep heads land is the instructive part. At an RMSE of
$\approx 0.45$ they sit at the level of the cross-piece plug-in mean
without a graph. Their coverage is respectable at $0.92$, so the variance
head does its job, but their NLL is far behind, because ranking which notes
are uncertain requires per-piece adaptation and the graph. This is the
attribution result of the main line, reached from the other side. A
follow-up at the leave-one-out limit of \S\ref{sec:masksweep}, the densest
observation and the regime in which a read-out head should be at its best,
leaves the conclusion unchanged. The proposed model is better on every
channel on both axes: against the ensemble, timing RMSE is $0.149$ against
$0.165$ and NLL $-1.12$ against $-0.50$, articulation RMSE is $0.546$
against $0.755$, and velocity RMSE is $0.066$ against $0.084$. The
uncertainty ranking of the ensemble therefore does not catch up with denser
observation. The scope of the comparison is that of read-out heads on fixed
embeddings, the natural deep comparator at matched information and training
data, and a fine-tuned end-to-end deep model remains untested.

\section{Task-matched pretraining}
A bidirectional, mask-aware retraining of the music model, matched exactly
to the evaluation task, was compared with the original at matched compute on
the development set. It is not better. Pooled error is slightly but
significantly worse, by $+0.011$, confidence quality shows no measurable
difference strict-to-strict ($-0.325$ against $-0.322$), and a $3\times$
training budget does not close the gap, since timing improves while
articulation and velocity do not. The reading is that the original read-out
had already extracted what the change of objective was meant to unlock. The
study also showed that the bidirectional read-out admits the leakage pattern
that the causal read-out excludes, because a note read at its own velocity
token contains that velocity. The pattern was recognised by its signature,
and the design rule that follows from it is a leave-one-out read-out, in
which each note is read with its own tokens masked, enforced by a
structural test.

\section{Kernel comparison in the two-stage regime}\label{sec:kernels}
The kernel comparison was run under the two-stage plug-in baseline on the
development set: the plug-in mean $\muLM$ under the strict protocol, one
scalar GP per channel, identical masks in every row, and recovery and
calibration scored paired per piece. It moved one of the two design axes of
\S\ref{sec:graphvariants} at a time, either the graph $\Lg$ or the spectral
profile $g(\nu;s)$. Three findings stand, and they are the reason the
proposed model keeps the plain graph with the additive profile.

First, the profile is inert. Graph Mat\'ern at $\alpha = 2$ and $3$, the
diffusion kernel and the normalized Laplacian are statistically
indistinguishable from the additive Laplacian on RMSE ($0.392$--$0.393$
against $0.393$) and do not improve NLL. Their evidence-fitted filters
coincide over the low-frequency components, where the prior mass lies, and
differ only where the feature kernels and the noise absorb the variance.
Second, the graph is not inert. Removing every edge raises RMSE to $0.446$
and NLL to $-0.190$, both paired differences with intervals excluding zero,
and the temporal chain shows no measurable difference from the full graph
on RMSE but is worse calibrated, with a paired NLL difference of $+0.031$
whose interval excludes zero. Third, music theory helps as extra edges and
not as a replacement metric. Tonal distance in place of semitone distance
is worse on both axes (RMSE $0.403$, NLL $-0.300$, both intervals excluding
zero), whereas adding chord and voice-leading edges gave the only
improvement on both axes, a paired RMSE difference of $-0.008$ and a paired
NLL difference of $-0.013$ with intervals excluding zero, robust to a sweep
over six settings of the untuned edge weights scaled by $\times 3$ and by
$\div 3$.

Under the proposed model the chord edges are redundant at the 40\%
operating point once the music-model embeddings enter the kernel. The
effect follows a density gradient, with a recovery gain at 30/20/10\%
hidden, no measurable difference at 40\%, and nothing at 50\%. The proposed
model therefore keeps the plain graph, and adopting the chord edges would
call for a fresh held-out evaluation under the protocol of
\S\ref{sec:hygiene}. The full tables, the row definitions and the $\mu = 0$
block are in the development record.\footnote{The kernel comparison is
recorded in \texttt{docs/kernel\_comparison\_results.md} and the density
gradient of the chord edges in \texttt{docs/kernel\_multirate\_results.md}.}

\section{Music-theoretic feature columns and embedding probes}\label{app:theoryfeat}
The incremental route adds music-theoretic score features to the hand-built
set, where they compose with any kernel. Fourteen score-only columns were
added: local key by Krumhansl--Schmuckler profiles (key clarity, mode, a
scale-degree encoding, an in-scale flag and the circle-of-fifths motion of
the local key), a metrical-weight hierarchy, within-chord dissonance and a
bass flag, phrase-boundary salience, motif-repetition counts and per-voice
tessitura. On the development set they change nothing of note. The paired
RMSE difference is $-0.002$ without the music-model embeddings, with an
interval that touches zero, and $+0.001$ beside them, with an interval
covering zero, and no single channel is significant in either regime. The
finding is not specific to the 40\% operating point. Repeated at every
masking level of \S\ref{sec:masksweep}, the columns stay non-significant
beside the embeddings at each level and reach marginal significance on the
plain model only at 10\% hidden, with a paired RMSE difference of $-0.004$.
This is the same pattern as the chord edges show: where the per-piece
weights are best determined, explicit theory carries a small signal that
the embeddings already cover.

A follow-up linear-probe study sharpens the interpretation, and not in the
obvious direction. Probing the embeddings directly, with a cross-piece
ridge fitted on the head pieces and scored out of sample on the development
pieces, shows that they encode voicing and rhythm: bass-note membership
(AUC $0.99$), metrical weight, phrase-boundary salience and register. They
do not encode tonality. Scale degree, in-scale membership, mode and local
key are all at chance from the embeddings ($R^2 \approx 0$, AUC
$\approx 0.5$), whether or not piece-level information is removed, and a
2048-dimensional random-features probe replicates every tonal result, so
this is not an artifact of probe linearity. The failure of the theory
columns is therefore \emph{not} explained by the embeddings already
carrying the tonal signal. Taken together with the tonal-metric result of
route (A) of \S\ref{sec:musictheory}, the consistent reading is that tonal
structure carries no measurable marginal signal for these three expressive
channels at this scale, while the rhythmic and voicing structure that the
columns add is already in the kernel. This sharpens the attribution of
\S\ref{sec:attribution} in an unexpected direction: the value of the
embeddings lies in rhythm, texture and dynamics convention, not in harmony.
The columns remain available in the implementation as an interpretable
probe and are switched off by default.

\chapter{Strings-and-winds development studies}\label{app:phase2dev}
This appendix collects the development studies of the strings-and-winds
setting in full. Each of them keeps a short summary in
Chapter~\ref{ch:phase2}. All of the evidence reported here comes from the
development set under the protocol of \S\ref{sec:hygiene}, and none of the
hypotheses evaluated on the held-out test set depended on any of it. The
appendix opens with the meaning of the six channels in musical terms, then
reports the drift study of within-note variation, the exploratory
tonal-metric test on intonation, and the comparison of three priors at

\section{The channels in musical terms}\label{app:glossary}
Before the estimation machinery, it helps to say what these quantities mean
to a listener. \textbf{Intonation} $c_i$ is whether a note is played sharp
or flat, and by how much. It is the deviation of the held pitch centre of
the note from the position that equal temperament assigns to the written
note, measured in cents, one hundred cents to a semitone, with positive
values meaning sharp. The centre is the centre of the pitch curve with the
vibrato oscillation removed, and it is not, by design, the average of the
curve, for the reason given in the drift study below. \textbf{Vibrato} is
the intentional periodic oscillation of pitch around that centre which
singers, string players and wind players add to sustained notes
\cite{seashore1936}. Three numbers describe it per note. The rate
$f_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ is the number of oscillations per second, typically
five to eight, with a mean of $6.0$\,Hz \cite{prame1994}. The extent
$\gamma_i$ is how far the pitch swings from the centre at its peak, in
cents: tens of cents on the strings and winds studied here, and
$\approx 71$ cents on average in professional singing \cite{prame1997}. The
onset delay $d_i^{\mathrm{vib}}$ is how long after the note begins the
oscillation starts, since performers often begin a note straight and let
the vibrato grow. \textbf{Loudness} $\ell_i$ is how strongly the note
sounds, read from the energy of the audio through \eqref{eq:loudness} and
taken relative to the level of the recording itself.

None of these values is annotated by hand. Every one of them is estimated
from the recording: a pitch tracker supplies the pitch curve of the note,
and the per-note fit turns that curve into these numbers, each with its own
uncertainty, which is exactly why the observation noise of
\eqref{eq:phase2-noise} becomes substantial on tracked audio. The human
annotations of the corpus supply only the note spans, that is, the onsets,
the durations and the written pitches. The annotated pitch curves of the
corpus, the reference curves, serve to calibrate the tracker and are never
used as channel values.

\section{The drift study of within-note variation}\label{app:drift}
Equations \eqref{eq:loudness} to \eqref{eq:vibrato} hold every parameter
fixed within the note, so the channels are time-averages, and it is fair to
ask what the averaging discards. The study that answers this is simple.
Every vibrato-identifiable development note that both pitch curves can
measure ($n = 5{,}145$) is refitted with the vibrato model of
\eqref{eq:vibrato} plus one added ingredient, a linear drift of the centre,
$c + s\,(t - \bar t)$ under the vibrato, and the fit is run twice per note,
independently on the tracked curve and on the reference curve. The
two-curve design is the control: a slope that appears in one curve only
would be measurement noise, whereas a slope that two independent
measurements agree on is the player. Loudness receives the same check
through the four chunk levels of \eqref{eq:loudness}.
Table~\ref{tab:drift-study} collects the numbers and
Figure~\ref{fig:drift-study} shows the argument.

The findings follow in the order of their importance. The drift is music
rather than tracker noise: about two thirds of the notes carry a
significant slope in each curve independently, and the two curves agree on
its direction $97\%$ of the time (panel B of Fig.~\ref{fig:drift-study}).
The drift is also large relative to the reported precision. The median note
drifts by $10.5$ cents, an order of magnitude above the median reported
standard error of $0.9$ cents on the intonation channel, so $c$ is a
precise estimate of the \emph{average} intonation of the note rather than
of a constant (panel A of Fig.~\ref{fig:drift-study}). Loudness moves even
more, although the falling shares in Table~\ref{tab:drift-study} indicate
that most of its movement is decay envelope rather than expressive shaping.
The discarded structure, finally, is white with respect to the graph. The
within-note slopes carry essentially no correlation from one note to the
next, where timing, the channel the graph demonstrably helps, carries
$+0.59$ (panels C and D of Fig.~\ref{fig:drift-study}).

Three consequences keep the per-note resolution defensible rather than
refuted. The mismatch is priced into the noise model, since the per-note
variances of \eqref{eq:phase2-noise} are residual-based, so a drifting note
reports itself as more uncertain, which is why calibration held on the
held-out test set. The reference targets pass through the same
parameterization, so the contrasts between the graph model and the no-graph
model compare like with like. And a within-note slope channel is precisely
the kind of channel that the graph prior could not help, so the per-note
resolution is empirically the right level for this model. The discarded
structure itself belongs to the frame-level likelihood of the waveform
setting, whose amplitude envelope $A_i(t)$ and pitch-curve model are built
to carry it. Slope channels remain candidates for a later evaluation under
the $d^{\mathrm{vib}}$ template, included as output with no hypothesis
attached to them.

\section{The tonal-metric exploratory test on intonation}\label{app:tonal}
The hypothesis of \S\ref{sec:phase2-delta}, that a circle-of-fifths metric
would improve the intonation channel, was fixed before the numbers were
seen, and the test was designated exploratory in the pre-specified design.
On the development set, replacing the semitone metric by the
circle-of-fifths metric improves held-out intonation on both axes. The
paired difference, tonal minus plain under the given variances, is $-0.21$
cents in RMSE with interval $[-0.34, -0.09]$ and $-0.05$ in NLL with
interval $[-0.07, -0.03]$, both intervals excluding zero. The replacement
also re-imposes the familiar penalty on timing, an NLL difference of
$+0.03$ against the tonal metric with an interval excluding zero. The
vibrato-rate channel picks up a small recovery gain of $-0.027$, with an
interval excluding zero, and the remaining channels are indifferent
(Fig.~\ref{fig:tonal-intonation}). On the held-out test set, under the
re-use described in \S\ref{sec:phase2-reuse}, the recovery gain was
reproduced, at $-0.312$ cents with interval $[-0.599, -0.081]$, whereas the
calibration difference, $-0.023$ with interval $[-0.049, +0.001]$, was not.
The tonal metric therefore keeps its exploratory status.

\section{Three priors at track scale}
\label{app:trackscale}
The waveform studies of Chapter~\ref{ch:phase3} found, on chains of three
to ten notes, that the score prior transfers only the common offset of the
context. The same question can be asked at whole-track scale on the tracked
six-channel bundle of this chapter. There, the development evaluation
already holds out $30\%$ of the notes per track, and both the graph model
and the no-graph ablation carry a per-piece bias column, so every contrast
is a contrast beyond the common offset. Two further priors were added to
that evaluation on the development set, with their hypotheses fixed in
advance, over $78$ tracks and $153$ track-and-seed combinations, under the
given variances and with paired differences per combination. The first is
a time-only chain, the same GP with the score graph replaced by unit-weight
edges between consecutive notes. The second is a time-only Gaussian graph,
the score graph with its pitch term removed and its two-beat time window
kept.

The result is two-sided in the same way as the waveform spans, but with the
statistical power that the chains lacked. The score graph is better than
the time-only chain on intonation, vibrato extent, vibrato rate and
loudness, with intervals excluding zero (Table~\ref{tab:trackscale}, first
column), and the chain itself is not reliably better than no structure,
since its NLL is worse on loudness and vibrato onset. The score graph shows
no measurable difference from the time-only Gaussian graph on intonation
($-0.02$ cents, with an interval covering zero) and is slightly worse on
the two vibrato channels. The time-only Gaussian graph against the chain
reproduces the margins of the score graph, and against no structure it
reproduces the intonation gain of the score graph that the held-out
evaluation established ($-0.88$ against $-0.90$). The value of the graph at
track scale is therefore temporal smoothing over a window of about two
beats. The pitch-proximity coupling that makes it a \emph{score} graph adds
nothing measurable here, as it added nothing at chain and phrase scale from
the waveform.

The piano counterpart gives the same answer. The piano model of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} with the pitch term removed from its graph, the
two-beat window kept, which on polyphonic piano links the notes of a chord
at full weight, was paired per piece against the full model on the
development set, over $30$ pieces and $4$ seeds. The pooled RMSE difference
is $-0.008$ with interval $[-0.016, -0.001]$ and the NLL difference is
$-0.019$ with interval $[-0.030, -0.008]$, a small improvement whose
intervals exclude zero, carried by articulation. The total value of the
graph on that set is $+0.017$ RMSE, so the pitch-less graph is worth about
half again as much as the score graph. On both corpora, then, the structure
that does the work is smoothness in score time over about two beats,
including the vertical coupling of simultaneous notes, and pitch proximity
adds nothing and costs a little. This attributes the graph value
established on the held-out test set without retracting it, since the
held-out comparisons are of the full model against its alternatives, and a
pitch-less graph as the default would change a reported protocol and
require its own pre-specified held-out evaluation.\footnote{The three
track-scale comparisons are recorded in
\texttt{results/phase2\_chain\_prior\_dev.md},
\texttt{results/phase2\_timegauss\_prior\_dev.md} and, for the piano
counterpart, \texttt{results/piano\_timegauss\_dev.md}.}
